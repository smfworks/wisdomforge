# Welcome

You do not need to be a studio artist to teach a five- to ten-year-old real art looking and making.

You need an object on the table before the name, and a child’s mark on the child’s page before polish.

That is what this book is for.

## Why this book exists

Many adults remember art as coloring sheets, a laminated color wheel, and a quiet “make it nice” from the adult at the elbow. That memory can leave you feeling rusty when a child asks how to draw a dog, or freezes at a blank page, or wants you to finish the ear. Feeling rusty is normal. It is not a character flaw.

At ages 5–10, art does not begin with a medium survey or a gallery critique. It begins with looking and making. A child can notice yellow dots on a postcard before anyone says *pointillism*. A child can follow a mug’s rim with a fat crayon. A child can tear paper and glue a scrap story. A child can pinch soft clay and redesign when a wall falls. A child can say what they see — and what makes them say that — without racing to “pretty.”

Those moves are not a waiting room for middle school. They are the work.

This book teaches you how to run a short **Art Hour** at home — roughly twenty to forty minutes — with the object down before the vocabulary, and with **look before name** and **make before polish** as the spine. Inside the chapters you will see two planning bands: **5–7** and **8–10**. Place your child by skill, not by birthday. A careful six-year-old and a fluent nine-year-old may sit in the same looking move on different days.

The focus lock for the whole book is simple:

**Look before name. Make before polish.**

Hold that, and the rest of the manual becomes practice.

## What a good Art Hour looks like

Picture a kitchen table. Not a museum field trip every day. Not a silent “color this packet” hour. Not a product chase for the fridge.

You put a mug, a leaf, fat crayons, or two tempera puddles on the table. You cover any caption that already names the famous work. You ask what the child notices — before you tell them what this is called. You wait. They look. They make one short thing with the tool in *their* hand. You ask one authentic question about what they see or what the hand tried, and you wait again. Then you name the idea only as far as the looking and the mark can carry. Then you stop talking. You leave one crumb: a sticky notice, a three-word caption, a “whose mark” check, or the child’s choice of what goes on the wall.

That is the hour. Library picture-book sits, museum one-work visits, grocery color carts, and nature texture walks motivate. They do not replace the look-before-name move on the table.

You will meet the full session shape in **The Art Hour**. Later chapters point back to it. You do not rebuild the spine every week.

## Two readers at once

This is a teaching manual for the parent or co-teacher at the table, with short student sections a child can actually use.

When you read a parent section, you are learning today’s looking-and-making move well enough to hear a wrong turn — a name-first rush, an adult-filled mark, a “draw it nice” freeze, a coloring-book-as-only-hour week, a generated image filed as the child’s work, a demo with no child’s page, a coverage lecture with no object on the table. You learn what to say next without grabbing the brush or filling the silence with “pretty.”

When your child reads (or hears) a student section, they get respect. No baby-talk. No lecture about you. They get a tiny worked example, two tries, and a chance to explain it back.

At 5–7 they can notice before a name, mark freely with fat tools, tear and glue, pinch soft clay, choose whether a page goes on the wall, and say what they see. At 8–10 they can follow a mug’s rim more slowly, try blind contour soft, mix with fewer colors, write a short because-I-see caption, redesign a fallen pinch, and say whose mark is on the page. Write — and speak — as if they can.

## What this book will not do

It will not hand you a 180-day craft dump.

It will not sell you a curriculum, crown one program, or promise a score.

It will not reprint *Art for Homeschooling* — that book serves grades 1–12, with later medium courses, portfolio edges, AP Art and Design, and solvent/oil/kiln safety for older work. For that map, see that book once. This book teaches the 5–10 Art Hour.

It is not a sequel to *History for Little Thinkers*, *Science for Little Thinkers*, *Critical Thinking Skills for Little Thinkers*, *English for Little Thinkers*, or *Math for Little Thinkers*. One pointer each is enough. Then we teach art as looking and making. The grain of those manuals — short hour, talk box, named try-its, exit crumb — rhymes. The content does not mash. Fair-test is science. Piece-from-then is history. Contour and tempera puddles are art.

It will not title your subject **Little Thinkers Art I**. A stranger-readable title is **Art**. Drawing only when the year’s work is mostly drawing.

It will not turn the hour into a culture-war pamphlet. Safe art objects only: a mug, a shoe, a leaf, a pear, a window view, fat crayons, tempera puddles, torn paper, a foam stamp, air-dry clay, a dated sketchbook. No child portraits in generators or book illustrations. No deepfake “artist friend.”

It will not pitch “art makes you smarter at reading or math.” Justify art by what art teaches — looking, marking, talking about the work.

It will not put solvents or craft knives on the kitchen table for this age band. Adult present. Materials with an ACMI **AP** seal and/or labelled **Conforms to ASTM D-4236**. Washable tempera and soft clay are enough.

It will not ask you to wait until middle school to begin. Children this age can look and make. They do not become artists because someone bought a sample craft kit.

## How the book is built

Front matter tells you how to use the week, five things to remember, and the Art Hour once — including the safety spine and the AI box for ages 5–10, said carefully and only once.

Eight teaching chapters follow: what art looks like at 5–10; looking at art and objects; mark-making and line; color and paint light; collage, print, and paper; clay and 3-D soft; talking and writing about art; short assessments and progress checks.

Then records and resources. Records keep a dated sketchbook, a looking note, the child’s make, and an optional process photo under **Art**. Resources name common programs by fit — parent load, look-then-make vs step-by-step, placement — with library, museum, and gallery first. Fit, not rank.

You do not need to be a studio artist. You do need to put the object on the table before the name, and to leave the child’s mark on the child’s page.

# How to use this book this week

Pick one looking-and-making move. Run the Art Hour a few times. Leave one crumb on Friday.

That is the honest “lesson plan.” Not a 180-day craft calendar. Not a unit binder that never returns to the mug.

## This week’s shape

1. **Choose one chapter focus.** Examples: notice before name on a mug or postcard; child’s first mark block; follow-the-edge; two-puddle mix; tear collage; foam stamp; pinch pot; tell-me talk (not “pretty”); a short whose-mark check.
2. **Run three to five Art Hours** of the named shape in **The Art Hour** (~20–40 minutes each). Same focus. Fresh object if you can; same mug or leaf revisited if that is what the week holds.
3. **Do one in-home try-it** from the chapter’s practice section (mug look, fat crayon block, tempera puddle, tear collage, foam stamp, pinch pot, window frame, light/dark water).
4. **Do one out-of-home try-it** once in the week when you can (museum one-work sit, sculpture walk, nature line/texture, library picture-book look first, grocery color cart, architecture edge walk, gallery child choice). Ordinary safety. Adult present outdoors.
5. **Friday: one diagnostic pattern + done-enough.** Hear one wrong turn without crushing the look — name-first, adult-filled mark, empty pretty loop, coloring-sheet-only week, generated image in the folder. Check whether the child can show the move two ways, and whether a page still feels like theirs after a night. Details live in each chapter’s “done enough” and in the assessments chapter.

If a day only holds twenty minutes: object + notice + short make + exit crumb. One named move done well beats a rushed craft medley. There is no nationally representative study of art minutes per day in U.S. homes that this book can quote — so there is no honest “required” dose to post on the fridge — and no need to shame a week that held three short sits.

## Placement by skill (5–7 / 8–10)

Use the bands as planning help, not as birthday locks.

**5–7** leans look-heavy: fat tools, tear before fancy cut, playdough or air-dry, oral exit, you may scribe the caption they said, process over likeness.

**8–10** may take a longer make: blind contour soft, foam print, overlap, written because-I-see crumbs — still refuse MFA critique. If looking habits are weak, stay on 5–7 LOOK and MARK longer, regardless of a ninth birthday.

A seven-year-old who can look at a rim and mark it may run early observation moves. A nine-year-old still melting down on long sits needs shorter material play. Soft band placement lives as a try-it shape in the assessments chapter — place by what they can show, not by the cake candles.

## How to read a teaching chapter

Read **Why this matters** and **For the parent** first — alone, if you can. Do the five-minute parent warm-up with tomorrow’s object or paper before the child sits. Practice the sentence “What do you notice?” aloud once. Check that the materials are actually on the table, not still in a cupboard. Check the seal (ACMI **AP** and/or **Conforms to ASTM D-4236**) before you open a new bottle.

Then use **How to teach it this week** as your script for the table. Exact wording is there on purpose. Use it. Age-band moves for **5–7** and **8–10** live inside that heading.

**Practice that actually builds learning** names try-its with time, materials, mess/safety, the fun, and the skill — at least one inside the home and at least one outside. The talk box gives the locked opening question, follow-ups, how to wait, and what a stuck silence usually means.

Read **For the student** with your child, or let them hear it. Warm, direct, short. Never a lecture about research.

Use **If it isn’t clicking** when something stalls. Three diagnostics. No shame. Correct a wrong reason without crushing the look. Rescue order is ask, wait, hint, short demo on *demo paper*, return the tool.

**Tools, including AI** stays short and points back to the Art Hour AI box. Do not rebuild policy every chapter.

**What “done enough” looks like** is your leave-this-chapter checklist. Placement by skill, not birthday. A publisher’s “grade 2 art workbook” is a scope, not a legal grade, and not a transcript line.

## Sibling and mixed-age homes

Share the object. Split the make.

The younger child may tear, stamp, notice, or say one thing they see. The older child may follow an edge more slowly, write a short caption, or redesign a fallen pinch with help.

Give each child a turn to speak. After one answers, wait so the next can add — not so the older becomes unpaid tutor as their only role. They need their own mark too.

## Multilingual homes

Looking works in the family’s talk language.

Notice, mark, and talk work before English art labels arrive. When a book page is in another script, you read the name aloud after the look; the child still points at what they see. You may scribe a caption the child said. Do not delay Art Hour until decoding is “ready.” Looking is not decoding.

## Activity banks are a menu

Later chapters pull named try-its from banks (look, mark, color, collage, print, clay, line, shape, space, talk, write, outings, safe, assessment — seventy-nine named across the book). A chapter picks a few. It does not assign all seventy-nine. Coverage itch is the enemy of looking.

## What to skip for now

Skip chapters you are not ready to teach this month. The map is a path, not a race.

Skip vendor shopping until you have run a few Hours with free lookables: a mug, a leaf, a window, fat crayons, scrap paper, a library picture book, a museum postcard.

Skip AI tools until you have read the Art Hour AI box once. The child looks and marks. You may use a tool on your account. The child does not sit alone with an open chat or an unsupervised generator.

## Records without drama

Date the sketchbook page. Title it **Art**. Keep the looking note, the child’s make, and an optional process photo. That file is the record. Details live in the records chapter. You do not need a brand name on the spine. Never title the subject **Little Thinkers Art I**.^2

# If you only remember five things

Keep this page short. Pin it where you plan the week. When the hour feels scattered, return here before you add more materials.

## 1. Look first

Put the mug, the leaf, the postcard (caption covered), the tempera puddles, or the clay on the table first. Looking is the lesson. The adult name waits. A fluent lecture with no object is not an Art Hour. Library, museum, and gallery days motivate; they do not replace the object on the table. One lookable beats a dizzying tour of twenty crafts.

## 2. Child’s tool, child’s mark

The child holds the crayon, brush, stamp, or clay. Your demo — if any — lives on *demo paper*, then you cover it or put it away. An adult-finished dog is not the child’s drawing. A matched sample as the only product is craft display, not this hour’s work. Process is the effect of making on the child. The fridge picture is evidence a sitting happened — not the grade. Presenting still exists: the child chooses which page goes up and can say one reason.

## 3. Struggle before rescue

Ask. Wait. Hint. Short demo on demo paper. Return the tool. Do not grab the brush. Do not fill the silence with “pretty.” Do not paste a machine’s picture as theirs. “I can’t draw” often means looking got ahead of the hand — shorten the task to one rim; do not talent-shame.

## 4. Talk points at the work

One authentic question about what they notice before a name, what they see and what makes them say that, whose mark and what the hand tried, what happened when colors met, what paper or press did, what clay did, what they can tell about the work — not whether it is pretty alone. Then a slow three. Look at the object or the page — not at the child — if the silence is hard. Empty pretty loops teach preference, not looking.

## 5. Safe materials and mess honesty

Check for an ACMI **AP** seal and/or **Conforms to ASTM D-4236** before you open a bottle (SAFE-01). No **CL** products through grade 6. No solvents — no turpentine, mineral spirits, or solvent adhesives as kitchen art. Labelled children’s scissors; no craft knives for this age book. Cover the table. Food and drinks out of the art area. Name the mess before you start. Wash hands. Adult present for wet, sharp, outdoor, and small parts. Poison Control 1-800-222-1222 if needed. “Non-toxic” does not mean edible.

# The Art Hour

![A kitchen table with a looking mug, fat crayons, a tempera puddle, and an open sketchbook — still-life, no people, no logos.](book/art/art-hour-setup.jpg){width=100%}

Twenty to forty minutes. Five beats. Stop while wanting one more mark.

This chapter is the session spine for the whole book. Later chapters say “run the Art Hour” and point here. You do not rebuild the shape every week.

## Parent warm-up (alone, about five minutes)

Before the child sits, put tomorrow’s object or paper on the table. Practice one sentence aloud: “What do you notice?”

Do not rehearse a lecture. Cover any caption that already names the famous work or the vocabulary word. If the only “still life” you have is a generated picture of a mug, put a real mug on the table instead.

Check the seal on new materials. Cover the table if paint or glue is coming. Preview the mess in one plain sentence.

Without this warm-up, the Hour collapses into name-first or polish-first.

## The five beats

### 1. Look / notice (3–8 minutes)

One object, reproduction (caption covered), prior page, or materials on the table. One lookable, not a stack of kits.

Ask what they notice. Where does your eye go first? What do you see that you did not expect?

You do not dump the name yet. If they ask “what is this called?” right away, turn them back to what is on the table. Silent look for a slow count when that helps.

### 2. Short make (8–18 minutes)

One named try-it. The child holds the tool. Adult demo — if any — is short and on *demo paper*, then covered or put away.

Struggle before rescue: ask → wait → hint → short step on demo paper → hand the tool back.

This is the child’s make. A parent demo with no child’s page is a picture, not the hour’s work.

### 3. Talk box (3–8 minutes)

Point at the work or the materials. One authentic question. Wait. Specific uptake — yellow dots, a drip kept, a rim followed — not “pretty” alone.

### 4. Name lightly (2–5 minutes)

After evidence: one kid-language name that fits (“those repeated stamps are a pattern”; “you mixed a new orange”). Optional. Skip if the child is spent — naming can wait until tomorrow’s look.

### 5. Exit crumb / show choice (2–5 minutes)

One durable trace: a sticky notice, a three-word caption, a process sentence, a photo of process (not only product), a “whose mark” check. You note one diagnostic pattern if any — for tomorrow’s teaching, not a graded quiz.

Optional show: fridge, folder, or the child’s choice of display. Done-enough today is looked, marked, talked — right, or wrong-with-a-reason for tomorrow. The exit should stand without a chat window.

### Optional sixth beat — siblings

After one child answers, wait so the next can speak. Share the object; split the make. Do not make the older child the unpaid tutor as their only role.

## Bands inside the hour

**5–7.** Look-heavy; fat crayons, large brushes, tear before fancy cut; playdough or air-dry; oral exit; you may scribe captions they said; process over likeness. Prefer sits nearer twenty minutes when attention is thin. No live open chat as the child’s partner.

**8–10.** Longer make optional; blind contour soft; foam print; overlap; written caption crumbs; still refuse MFA critique. If looking habits are weak, stay on 5–7 LOOK and MARK longer — regardless of a ninth birthday.

Place by skill, not birthday. Move a child between bands by what they can show.

## Talk-box shape (once for the book)

Every teaching chapter carries a talk box. The shape is always the same.

**Opening question.** Authentic. About what they notice before a name, what they see and what makes them say that, whose mark and what the hand tried, what happened when colors met, what paper or press did, what clay did, what they can tell about the work (not pretty), or what they can show two ways. Not “did you have fun.” Not “what is the vocabulary quiz word.”

Locked openings live in each chapter. Chapter 1 opens with notice before name. Chapter 2 opens with what you see — and what makes you say that. Chapter 3 opens with whose mark and what the hand tried.

**Three to five follow-ups.** One for look (“what do you *see* / *notice*?”). One for make (“what did your hand / the material do?”). One for evidence (“what do you see that makes you say that?”). One for process or check (“how did you do that?” / “whose mark?”). One that lets them revise (“you may change your mind” / “try another look” / “keep the drip — what could it become?”).

**How to wait.** After you ask, a slow three is a useful convention — drawn from classroom wait-time research, labelled as classroom-origin, used here as pause craft for Art Hour, not an art experiment on your kitchen table.^4 After they stop, wait again. If they are mid-look or mid-mark, do not cut them off. Look at the object, the page, or the materials if the silence is hard.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the question was vague; you did not wait; they are hunting a vocabulary word because you asked name-first; they fear “draw it nice”; every hour has been coloring-book lines; they point at a phone image as “my art”; they shrug at “what is it?” (use tell-me); the caption was uncovered before looking.

Pose, wait, and point back at the object or the page — do not fill the silence with “pretty.”

Typical in-home lookables and makes: mug look, fat crayon block, two-puddle mix, tear collage, foam stamp, pinch pot, light/dark water, window frame, follow-the-edge.

## Out-of-home try-it shape (once)

Same fields — time, materials, mess/safety, the fun, the skill — plus ordinary outing care.

Typical outings: museum one-work sit, sculpture walk, nature line/texture, library picture-book look first, grocery color cart, architecture edge walk, gallery child choice.

**Safety and courtesy.** Roads, weather, poison plants, museum hands-off rules, grocery courtesy. Leave living things unharmed. Cover plaques and captions until after the look when you can.

Outings motivate. They do not replace the look-before-name move at home.

## Safety spine (once for the book)

1. **Adult present** for wet media, scissors, outdoors, small parts, clay tools, and anything that drips or stains.
2. **ACMI AP** and/or **Conforms to ASTM D-4236** for child materials; **no CL** products through grade 6.^7 Read the current label. (SAFE-01 seal check before open.)
3. **No solvents** as the default kitchen path for ages 5–10 — no turpentine, mineral spirits, or solvent-based adhesives as kitchen art. (SAFE-04.)
4. **Labelled children’s scissors**; adult present; snips before interior cuts; **no craft knives for this age book**. Follow the tool label and the child’s skill — this book invents no age statute.
5. **Mess honesty** before you start; cover the table; food and drinks out of the art area. (SAFE-02, SAFE-05.)
6. **Small amounts** of paint; wash hands; ventilate ordinary rooms.
7. **Outdoor:** traffic, poison plants, leave living things unharmed.
8. If you cannot name the hazard and the adult control, do not run the try-it.
9. Poison Control **1-800-222-1222** if needed. “Non-toxic” does not mean edible.
10. **Stop the hour when safety fails.** Looking and making continue another day.

When in doubt, do not run it. A chatbot cannot be the safety officer. Later chapters point back here in one sentence.

## Dose honesty

If a day only holds twenty minutes: object + notice + short make + exit crumb. One named move done well beats a rushed craft medley. Three to five Art Hours plus one out-of-home try-it is a full week.

That rhythm lives here, in later “done enough” checklists, and in the records you keep. This book does not invent a ninth teaching chapter titled “life of the habit.”

## AI at the art hour (ages 5–10) — said once

The child looks at a real object and makes the mark. You may use a tool on your account. The child does not sit alone with an open chat or an unsupervised generator.

**Ages 5–7:** no live open chat as the child’s partner. You may generate a four-line SCRIPT for after the attempt, and you may ask a tool to explain a named page or invent still-life setups *to you*. No child-facing chat. No child account.

**Ages 8–10:** still parent in the room. The child may sit next to you while you use a tool. They may hear a hint after they have tried. They may not have their own companion account. They may not be asked to “prompt the image tool until it looks finished.” Unaided first on looking and marking. SCRIPT after the try.

**You may**, on your account, after the child has tried (or before, for your prep):

1. Explain this week’s idea *to you* from a named textbook or lesson page — not to the child as a live partner.
2. Still-life setup ideas using objects already in the kitchen — then the child draws from the **objects**, not from tracing a generated mockup as the project.
3. A composition thumbnail to look at together (What’s cropped? Where is the darkest dark?), then **put it away** before the child draws from life or from a print on paper.
4. Sketchbook-challenge prompts the child will *draw* — the model wrote the list; the child made the marks.
5. A short labelled SCRIPT you will say *after* they look and mark — questions that keep *your* voice and the child’s eyes on the object or the page.
6. A hint after an attempt — a question or a named looking move, not a finished drawing.
7. Diagnose work they already produced (crop the photo to the **paper**, not the face) — then *you* talk to the child.
8. Discussion questions you will vet and ask yourself — the child answers you, not the tab.

**You may not:**

1. Ask the tool to **make my child’s project** or hang a generated image as Drawing.
2. **Turn in a generated image as student work** (name it generated if it appears in process notes; do not label it the child’s drawing).
3. Leave a **generator unsupervised during making** — not at 5, not at 10.
4. Ask the tool to **generate a “child drawing” / crayon-kids style picture as the child’s mark**.
5. Photograph a looking quiz for a filled-in key (**photo-to-key**).
6. Ask the tool to **write the child’s artist statement as the only draft**.
7. Run a **voice-clone “artist friend”** or companion that generates pictures when you leave the room.
8. **Upload the child’s face** into a generator.
9. **Detector-grade** the child’s drawing or short statement and call the score honesty.
10. Invent research or trust a fluent citation / artist quote without opening the book.
11. Hand the child an agent (Hermes Agent, Grok Bot, or any cloud-computer worker) as an art partner.

**Grids vs generators (one line):** a grid, trace, or projector still goes through the child’s eye and hand; a generator skips looking and marking. Grids can be misused. Generators used as the project are not the child’s art.

**One crutch sentence, labelled OFF-AGE for art hour, then teach:** in a **high-school math** trial (not this art band), an unguarded chatbot made practice look better and left students worse on the test with the window closed (Bastani et al., 2025).^5 This is a useful study, not a promise that every home will see the same result — and it is **not a 5–10 art experiment**. The paper itself notes creative subjects lack that kind of scoring. The kitchen-table rule it supports: the model may prepare the adult and the next looking page; it may not do the child’s looking or marking when those are the lesson.

**COPPA.** Ages 5–10 are under 13. Parent holds the account always.^6 Parent in the room. The 22 April 2026 compliance date has passed. Keep accounts, logs, and keys where you can open them. An art tool is not a secret friend.

**Names that stay separate.** Hermes 4 is not Hermes Agent. Grok is not Grok Bot and is not a national “Grok-as-tutor” program. Grok Imagine is not Grok Bot. There is no Grok Bot education product this book can honestly name as a school SKU. Game artist makes pictures, not an art course. Agents are not chatbots.

**Detectors and fake citations (parent-side).** Do not grade a child’s contour from a detector score.^8 If you asked a tool for a paper on looking, open Lowenfeld, Salome, or the National Core Arts Standards framework yourself.

## What art looks like at 5–10

## Why this matters

Art at five to ten is not a waiting room for middle school. It is looking and making right now — with a mug on the table, a fat crayon, a torn scrap of paper, a short sit that ends while the child still wants one more mark.

Two flips unlock the whole book.

**Look before name.** Put the object or the picture first. Ask what they notice. Wait. Only then offer a light name — edge, texture, crop — if a name helps. Vocabulary after eyes, not instead of them.

**Make before polish.** The child’s tool stays in the child’s hand. A demo lives on demo paper. Accidents can be kept. Likeness is not the grade. The fridge picture proves a sitting happened; it is not the score.

These flips matter because the wrong week is easy to slip into. You name “still life” before anyone looks. You finish the dog’s ear so it will look nice on the fridge. You hang a generated “kids crayon” picture and call it theirs. You wait until they are “ready,” or you run five crafts that match a sample and call the year Art. None of those teaches looking. None of those leaves the child’s mark on the child’s page.

What this chapter unlocks is a spine you can trust for every later Art Hour: notice first, mark first, talk that points at the work, place by skill not birthday, and stop while wanting one more mark. Later chapters add color puddles, tear collage, soft clay, captions, and short kitchen checks. They do not retire these two flips. If a week gets loud with materials and thin on eyes, return here.

A path through elementary art looking is the promise. A diploma is not. A percentile is not. An art-makes-you-smarter pitch is not. Coverage is a map. Depth is the hour.



**Why struggle is allowed.** Looking past the first obvious name is work. Leaving a wobbly line on the page is work. Waiting through silence is work. The struggle is the learning. Rescue too early and the child learns that the adult’s hand is the real artist. Rescue too late with shame and the child learns that art is a talent audition. Ask, wait, hint, demo on demo paper, return the tool — that order keeps dignity and skill in the same Hour.

**Why ordinary objects beat fancy kits this week.** A mug already lives in your kitchen. A shoe already has edges worth following. Kits can wait. Coverage itch is the enemy of looking. One object, five notices, one open mark block, one talk box — that is a real Art Hour.


You do not need to be a studio artist. You do need to put the object on the table before the name, and to leave the child’s mark on the child’s page.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

Many adults feel rusty on the art they were taught as coloring sheets and a laminated color wheel. That is normal, not a character flaw. Your job this week is not to become an MFA critic. Your job is to hear a name-first rush, an adult-filled mark, a “draw it nice” freeze, a coloring-book-as-only-hour week, a generated image filed as the child’s work, a demo with no child’s page, and a coverage lecture with no object on the table — and to ask one good question without grabbing the brush or filling the silence with “pretty.”

**Everyday picture.** Tomorrow morning, set one mug on the cleared table. Do not say “mug,” “still life,” or “cylinder.” Sit for one quiet minute and list five things you notice — the handle’s shadow, a chip on the rim, the way the glaze pools near the base, a fingerprint in the dust, the oval of the opening from where you sit. Only after that list would you teach a word. That is look before name in your own body. If you catch yourself racing to a label, start the minute again.

**Precise picture — maps, not verdicts.** Teachers and researchers have long described how children’s drawings often change across childhood. Lowenfeld and Brittain mapped typical graphic behaviors — a **map**, not a giftedness or delay verdict.^10 Roughly: pre-schematic around ages 4–7 (marks and early symbols), schematic around 7–9 (repeated symbols that “stand for” things), dawning realism around 9–11 (more care about how things look). Use the map to choose a try-it, not to crown or shame a child. Davis’s U-curve pattern about expressiveness judgments near 8–10 is a research pattern about how adults rate children’s work — teach through a stiffer week; do not pathologize it as “not an art kid.”^11

The National Core Arts Standards (voluntary, dated 4 June 2014) name four processes — Creating, Presenting, Responding, Connecting — as a voluntary map for schools, not a mandate for your kitchen table.^12 Presenting still exists at home as the child’s choice of which page goes on the wall plus one reason — not as polish panic. NAEYC process defaults fit early years: process is the effect of making on the child. The fridge picture is evidence a sitting happened.^13 It is not the grade.

REAP (Winner and Hetland) reviewed arts education research at scale (188 reports, 275 effect sizes, 10 meta-analyses in that project’s framing) and found that arts-rich education **correlates** with verbal and math indicators in some datasets; they did **not** find evidence that studying the arts **causes** those academic indicators to improve. This is a useful study map, not a promise that every home will see the same result. Justify art by what art teaches — looking, making, noticing, talking about work — not by a smarter-at-reading sales pitch.^14

Studio Habits of Mind name eight studio habits from **high-school** studio research. Adapt lightly for a kitchen Hour (stretch and explore when a drip happens; observe; reflect). Do not claim a K–2 randomized trial from high-school studio findings.^15 TEKS and other state element timing (for example, when “value” appears in a state list) are labelled state maps when you meet them — not universal kitchen law.

**Grip and attention as limits, not excuses to withhold looking.** Schneck and Henderson (1990) tracked mature pencil grips in young children (n=320, ages 3.0–6.11); mature grips rose from 48% to 90% across that span. Fat tools and short sits respect developing hands.^16 CDC names attention of about 5–10 minutes for arts and crafts at age 5 as a milestone pattern; scissors skill at that age is a tip, not a locked milestone.^17 Do not withhold looking from a five-year-old who can notice yellow dots. Do not dump grades 6–12 design, high-school Drawing credit, or AP Art and Design as the spine of this book. Those live in *Art for Homeschooling* if you need them later.

**What this book is not (one pointer each, then teach).** This book is not a reprint of *Art for Homeschooling* (grades 1–12 grade map, medium courses, portfolio for strangers, later solvents and kiln depth). One pointer, then teach five-to-ten looking and making here. It is not a sequel to *History for Little Thinkers*, *Science for Little Thinkers*, *Critical Thinking Skills for Little Thinkers*, *English for Little Thinkers*, or *Math for Little Thinkers*. Rhyme the grain of short sits and talk boxes; do not mash History’s piece-from-then or Science’s fair-test into the art spine. Never title the subject Little Thinkers Art I. The transcript title a stranger can read is **Art**.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. **Name-first rush.** Child draws an almond “eye” or a circle “sun” without looking at *this* object. Usually means a symbol bank or vocabulary arrived before eyes. Fix: put the mug present; ask notices first (LOOK-01); catch it softly later with AS-03.
2. **Fridge-as-grade.** Success equals “pretty enough for the door.” Usually means process got swapped for display. Fix: child chooses whether a page goes up and can say one reason; still keep the messy try in the folder.
3. **“Wait until middle school for real art.”** Usually means fear of mess or a memory of craft mania. Fix: short open mark + notice this week; depth is the hour.
4. **“This will raise reading / math.”** Usually means a sales pitch replaced the art reason. Fix: one plain REAP mute sentence, then teach looking.
5. **Lowenfeld as gifted/delay label.** “She’s schematic so she’s behind / he’s gifted so skip looking.” Usually means map turned into identity. Fix: place by today’s skill — looking, mark control, talk — not by birthday or chart alone (AS-04).

Neighbor wrong turns you will meet again: adult-filled marks; “draw it nice” freeze; coloring-book-as-only-hour; generated image as child’s work; demo with no child’s page. Chapter 3 owns authorship depth; Chapter 8 owns the full assessment shapes. Hear them early so you do not build a week on them.

**Five-minute parent warm-up (alone, before the lesson).** Set tomorrow’s object or a blank sheet. Practice saying aloud: “What do you notice — before I tell you what this is called?” Count a slow three after you ask. Touch the object, not an answer key. Put the phone face down. If you invent a lecture in your head, erase it and look again. You are practicing wait, not rehearsing coverage.

## How to teach it this week

**Session shape (Art Hour, ~20–40 min).** Five beats you already met in the Art Hour chapter:

1. **Look / notice** — 3–8 minutes. Object or image present. Caption covered if there is one.
2. **Short make** — 8–18 minutes. Child holds the tool. Demo on demo paper only.
3. **Talk box** — 3–8 minutes. Locked opening below. Wait.
4. **Name lightly** — 2–5 minutes. Optional. Can wait until tomorrow.
5. **Exit crumb / show choice** — 2–5 minutes. Notice / make / wonder; optional wall choice with one reason.

Dose honesty: three to five Hours this week beat one marathon craft day. Stop while they still want one more mark.

**Exact wording you can say.**

- Opening (locked): “What do you notice — before I tell you what this is called?”
- After a silence: “I’m looking at the handle. What do you see?”
- Before make: “Your tool. My demo stays on this other paper.”
- Mid-make (if they freeze): “One more mark. You choose where.”
- After make: “Tell me one thing your hand tried.”
- If they ask “is it good?”: “Tell me what you notice on your page.”
- Exit: “One notice, one make move, one wonder — sticky note or say it.”
- Show choice: “Does this page want the wall, the folder, or the recycle — and why?”

**First try-it for the student this week.** LOOK-01 Object Before Name with a mug or shoe, then MARK-01 Child’s First Mark Block on large paper with a fat crayon. No sample to match. No “draw a mug” prompt until after free mark — and even then, prefer “follow one edge after looking” later in the week or in Chapter 3.

**How to fade help.**

- Day 1: You may point at one edge after they speak a notice.
- Day 2: You wait longer; paraphrase one notice without adding vocabulary.
- Day 3: They lead the notice list; you only ask the opening.
- Any day: hands off their page. Struggle before rescue — ask, wait, hint, short demo on *demo paper*, return the tool.

**When to stop talking.** After you ask the opening, wait. After they answer, wait again. If they are mid-look or mid-mark, do not cut them off. Name lightly only after looking and making — or skip naming until tomorrow. Specific noticing (“yellow dots near the rim”) beats generic praise.

**Age-band moves inside this week**

**5–7.** Look-heavy. Fat crayons, tempera sticks, large paper taped down. Oral exit crumbs; you may scribe their words on a sticky note. Process over likeness. Tear and open mark beat fancy cut this week. Keep sits inside honest attention windows; a second short sit beats one drained hour. Playdough and air-dry arrive in later chapters — this week’s spine is notice + free mark.

**8–10.** Slightly longer look and make. They can try a slower rim follow after looking (preview of LINE-04 in Chapter 3) once free mark has happened. Written three-word crumb if ready (WRITE-01 soft preview). If looking habits are weak — name-first drawings, freeze at “draw it nice,” only coloring-book talk — stay on 5–7 LOOK/MARK longer. Place by skill, not birthday (AS-04). Refuse MFA critique voice; keep kitchen warmth.

## Practice that actually builds learning

Named try-its below include time, materials, mess/safety, the fun, and the skill. Practice lives **in the home and outside it**. Library, walk, museum, kitchen, and window *are* the practice — they do not replace the look-before-name move; they host it.

#### In-home — LOOK-01 Object Before Name

- **Time:** 10–20 min
- **Materials:** One ordinary object (mug, shoe, leaf, key); blank paper optional
- **Mess / safety:** Low mess; table clear of food; adult present
- **The fun:** Silence feels like a game. Five notices before any adult art word.
- **The skill:** Eyes before vocabulary; wait craft; refuse name-first.

**Steps.** Clear the table. Place the object. Cover any label if there is one. Say the locked opening. Silent look about 60–90 seconds. Child names five notices. Only then may you lightly say a word if it helps — “rim,” “shadow” — or wait until tomorrow. Optional: quick sketch after notices, not before.

**Stuck pattern.** Child shrugs and says “it’s a mug.” Reply: “Yes — and what do you *notice* on it before we use any art words?” Point at the object. Wait.

#### In-home — MARK-01 Child’s First Mark Block

- **Time:** 15–25 min
- **Materials:** Large paper taped down; fat crayon or tempera stick (look for ACMI AP seal and/or “Conforms to ASTM D-4236”); table cover
- **Mess / safety:** Low–med; cover table; washable preferred; food/drinks off the art area; wash hands after; no solvents; read the current label (“non-toxic” does not mean edible)
- **The fun:** No “draw a ___” for the first 5–8 minutes. Freedom is the point.
- **The skill:** Authorship; tool in child’s hand; make before polish.

**Steps.** Tape paper so it does not slide. Hand over the tool — do not hover with a second crayon “just in case.” Free mark block. Your hands stay off their page. Demo, if any, on demo paper only, then cover it (MARK-05 depth in Chapter 3). Stop while they still want one more mark. Optional process photo of hands and paper — crop to the work; do not upload the child’s face into a generator.

**Stuck pattern.** “I don’t know what to draw.” Reply: “You don’t have to draw a thing-with-a-name yet. Make marks. Fast ones. Slow ones. Your choice.”

#### In-home — LOOK-08 Window Frame Find (warm-up companion)

- **Time:** 10–20 min
- **Materials:** Window; cardboard viewfinder or hands as frame
- **Mess / safety:** Low; no climbing sills
- **The fun:** Framing a slice of the world like a tiny movie.
- **The skill:** Notice edges that cut objects; name “crop” lightly only after looking.

#### In-home — AS-04 Band Placement Soft

- **Time:** 10 min
- **Materials:** Recent folder of work (a few pages is enough)
- **Mess / safety:** —
- **The fun:** Sorting “what my hand can do” without a birthday lecture.
- **The skill:** Place by looking, mark control, and talk — not by age alone.

Lay out three piles soft: “notices came easy,” “marks felt free,” “needed more looking first.” An eight-year-old may still need 5–7 LOOK/MARK. That is placement, not failure. A five-year-old who notices carefully and marks boldly may stretch toward longer sits — still fat tools, still short honesty.

#### In-home — AS-06 Done-Enough Checklist Soft (preview)

- **Time:** 5 min
- **Materials:** This week’s focus sticky note
- **Mess / safety:** —
- **The fun:** A short yes-list instead of a perfect sample.
- **The skill:** Done enough = looked, made with own mark, talked once, left a crumb — not identical to a sample.

#### In-home — AS-01 Exit Crumb Trio (Friday shape)

- **Time:** 5 min
- **Materials:** Sticky notes
- **Mess / safety:** Low
- **The fun:** Three tiny tickets out the door of the Hour.
- **The skill:** Reflect without grading.

Exit: (1) one notice, (2) one make move, (3) one wonder. Not a grade. Adult may scribe at 5–7.

#### Out-of-home — OUT-04 Library Picture-Book Look First

- **Time:** 20–30 min
- **Materials:** Picture book; card or sticky to cover words until after looking
- **Mess / safety:** Low; library manners
- **The fun:** Pictures first feels like a secret spy mission.
- **The skill:** Notice before story dump; same flip as the mug.

**Steps.** Choose one spread. Cover the text. Silent look. Ask notices with the locked opening adapted to the page (“What do you notice — before we read what this is called?”). Then uncover and read. National survey tables show library visits are common among homeschool families in a past-month window — use the outing as looking practice. Browsing alone is not Art Hour; this sit is.

#### Out-of-home — OUT-01 Museum One Work Sit (when available)

- **Time:** 20–40 min at site
- **Materials:** Museum or gallery; optional tiny sketchbook; follow no-touch rules
- **Mess / safety:** Venue rules; adult present
- **The fun:** One work only — scavenger mania off.
- **The skill:** Depth over coverage; notice before the wall label.

**Steps.** Child helps choose one work (OUT-07 ownership soft). Cover or ignore the label first. Silent look. Notices. Optional tiny sketch of one edge. Then read the label if you want. Art gallery/museum/historical site visits also show up in homeschool outing rows — motivate with the outing; still run look-before-name.^19

If museum is not this week, the library sit still counts as OUT. A grocery produce aisle with three different greens (OUT-05) can wait for the color chapter; this week’s OUT stays on looking without a name race.

#### Talk box (this chapter)

**Opening (locked):** “What do you notice — before I tell you what this is called?”

**Follow-ups (3–5):**

1. **Look:** “What do you *see* on the rim / in the shadow / in the shiny spot?”
2. **Make:** “What did your hand try on the big paper?”
3. **Evidence:** “What do you see that makes you say that?”
4. **Process / check:** “Whose mark is on this page?”
5. **Revise:** “You may change your mind — look again. What else? Keep the drip — what could it become?”

**How to wait.** After you ask, a slow three is a classroom-origin pause craft (Rowe’s elementary science wait-time work; Stahl’s think-time) used here as Art Hour pause craft — labelled classroom-origin, not an art RCT, not a kitchen wait coefficient.^18 After they stop talking, wait again. If they are mid-look or mid-mark, do not cut them off. If silence is hard, look at the object or the page, not at the child.

**What a stuck silence usually means.** The question was vague; wait-time was zero; they are hunting a vocabulary word because you asked name-first; they are waiting for you to finish the dog; they fear “draw it nice”; every hour has been coloring-book lines; they point at a phone image as “my art”; they shrug at “what is it?” (bad question — use notice / tell-me); the caption was uncovered before looking. Pose, wait, point back at the object or page. Do not fill with “pretty.” Do not turn the table into a fight.

**Blocked for a new move.** Name-first drawing; adult finishing marks; “wait until middle school”; art-makes-you-smarter as the reason to sit; Lowenfeld chart as identity; generated image in the child-work folder.

**Mixed — when to use this week’s spine.** Every Art Hour from here forward. Color, collage, clay, talk/write, and short assessments add moves; they ride on look-before-name and make-before-polish.

**One incorrect example to diagnose (illustration, not a reported family).** Parent uncovers a postcard caption, says “This is Cubism,” then asks the child to “draw it nice like the sample.” Child freezes, then copies a phone JPEG of a “kids drawing style” picture into the folder labelled Drawing. Diagnosis: explain-before-look + polish pressure + generated-as-mark. Rescue order: cover the caption; LOOK-01 with a real mug; MARK-01 with the child’s crayon; AS-02 honesty that tool-made images are not their mark; delete the JPEG from the child-work folder; keep process notes truthful.

## For the student

Art hour is looking and making — not guessing the right name first.

Here is the idea in one line: **look first, then make your own mark.**

You are a person who can notice. You are a person who can hold a tool. You do not have to be “an art kid” to belong at this table. You belong because you look and you try.

**Tiny worked example.** Imagine a blue mug on the table. You look without talking for a little while. Then you say: “There’s a shiny spot. The handle makes a dark shape. There’s a tiny chip. The top looks like an oval from here.” Only later might someone say “rim” or “shadow.” Then you get a fat crayon and a big paper taped so it does not slide. You mark for a few minutes — swirls, presses, quiet lines. Nobody finishes your page for you. That page is yours. Maybe you choose it for the wall. Maybe it goes in a folder. Either way, you can say one thing you notice on it.

**Try 1 — Notice five.** Pick one thing on the table (mug, shoe, leaf, key). Look quietly. Tell five things you notice before anyone says the art word. You may change your mind when you look again.

**Try 2 — First mark block.** Tape a big paper down. Hold a fat crayon. Mark freely for several minutes. No “draw a perfect ___” yet. Stop when you still want one more mark.

**Explain it back.** Tell a grown-up in your own words: What does “look before name” mean? What does “my mark” mean? What is one thing you are *not* supposed to pretend is your drawing (hint: a picture a machine made)?

**Challenge.** At the library (or with a picture book at home), cover the words on one page. Look. Notice. Then uncover. Same game as the mug. If you visit a museum, pick **one** work and sit with it — not a race through every room.

You are allowed to wait. You are allowed to keep a drip or a smudge if you want to turn it into something. You are allowed to say a page is yours. You are allowed to say you want the folder instead of the fridge.

## If it isn’t clicking

**Diagnostic 1 — Everything becomes a vocabulary quiz.** They freeze until you say the “right” word. Next move: cover labels for two whole Hours; ask only notices; name lightly tomorrow or not at all this week. Put the object back on the table every time the talk drifts to quiz mode.

**Diagnostic 2 — They only color inside printed lines.** Sheets as the only hour; talk is only about staying in the lines. Next move: demote coloring books to optional quiet toys; schedule MARK-01 and LOOK-01 as the Art Hour. Chapter 8’s AS-05 owns the full coloring-book-only flag — plant the habit now.

**Diagnostic 3 — “I can’t draw” / talent shame rose early.** Looking got ahead of the hand, or polish arrived too soon, or an adult finished yesterday’s dog. Next move: shorten the task to free mark or one rim; refuse talent lectures; struggle before rescue — ask, wait, hint, short demo on *demo paper*, return the tool. Do not grab the brush. Do not fill silence with “pretty.”

**Extra pattern — phone JPEG as “my art.”** Honesty first (AS-02): tool-made is not their mark. Delete from child-work folder. Start MARK-01 today.

**When to slow down.** Name-first drawings dominate; adult hands keep “helping”; silence never gets a wait; every success metric is fridge polish.

**When to go ahead.** Child offers notices before titles; open mark happens without a matched sample; exit crumb is spoken or sticky-noted; they can say whose mark is on the page.

**When to get a human tutor.** Persistent pain, sensory distress with materials, or a need beyond kitchen Art Hour — seek a person who knows children’s art and your child. A Lowenfeld chart is not a clinic. Screening is not an identity verdict.

Never correct the look by crushing it. Rescue order is ask, wait, hint, short demo on demo paper, return the tool. “Never correct the child” is not this book’s rule — correct a wrong *reason* kindly (name-first, adult-filled marks, generated-as-theirs) without grabbing the brush.

## Looking at art and objects

## Why this matters

Looking is a skill you can practice on a mug, a postcard, a library picture book, a window slice, and one museum work — not a talent you either woke up with or missed.

This chapter owns the eyes. Chapter 1 gave you the spine: look before name; make before polish. Here you learn how to *run* looking — caption covered, silence first, notices with evidence, texture with eyes and hands, the same object in two lights, a fridge tour that talks process instead of “pretty,” a window frame that crops the world, and one focused sit outside the home.

Without this chapter, Art Hour drifts into scavenger mania at the museum, “guess the famous artist,” uncovering the caption first, or explaining the picture before anyone has looked. With it, a five-year-old can notice yellow dots and a rough patch of bark. An eight-to-ten-year-old can add evidence talk — what they see that makes them say that — and a soft VTS three-question sit when the image has a little mystery.

Looking matters because making without looking becomes symbol-copy and polish panic. Talk without looking becomes empty preference. Outings without looking become worksheets that never look twice. The move is simple and hard: put the work or the object first, ask what they see, wait, ask what makes them say that, wait again.

Library, museum, gallery, grocery aisle, and nature walk motivate. They do not replace the look-before-name move. They are where you practice it with new things to see.

**Why struggle is allowed.** Staying with one image when your feet want the next room is work. Saying “because I see the dark rectangle” instead of “I don’t know” is work. Letting a child’s story differ from the wall label — while still asking for evidence — is work. Rescue too early with the “right” title and they learn looking is a quiz. Shame a wrong story and they learn silence is safer than eyes. Ask for evidence. Wait. Look again together.

**Why ordinary looking beats fancy culture quizzes this week.** A covered postcard teaches the same flip as a famous canvas. Depth is the hour. Coverage is a map. One work, well seen, beats twenty works checked off.

You do not need museum membership to teach looking. You need one image, one object, one question, and the courage to wait. That is enough to begin — and enough to repeat next Tuesday when the flips slip.


**Shared looking and outings motivate — and still need the move.** A family that loves museums but always reads the plaque first is practicing culture, not yet practicing Art Hour looking. Keep the love. Add caption cover. A family that never leaves home can still run LOOK-02 and LOOK-01 richly; schedule OUT-04 at the library when you can. Neither family is behind if eyes work first.

**Process on the fridge is looking too.** When you tour prior work, you are looking at the child’s marks as evidence of sittings — not grading prettiness. That habit stitches Chapter 1’s process lock to this chapter’s eyes.



## For the parent: understand it yourself

Many adults were trained to read the plaque first and the painting second. That habit feels responsible. In Art Hour it steals the lesson. Your job is to become fluent enough in looking craft to hear caption-first, scavenger mania, guess-the-artist, explain-before-look, and pretty-only talk — and to put eyes back on the work without scolding.

**Everyday picture.** Take a postcard or a picture-book page. Cover the title and any wall-label words with a sticky note. Set a quiet minute. List five notices without saying the artist’s name or the story. Only then uncover. Notice how fast your brain wanted the caption. That itch is what your child feels when you uncover first.

**Precise picture.** Several looking traditions travel into a kitchen talk box without becoming a museum course. Project Zero’s See–Think–Wonder offers three stems: What do you see? What do you think about that? What does it make you wonder?^21 Visual Thinking Strategies (VTS) offers a family of three questions often used with narrative images: What’s going on in this picture? What do you see that makes you say that? What more can you find? Parent paraphrase and pointing matter more than praise contests.^22 Barrett’s “point with words” keeps talk specific.^23 NAEYC noticing grain (“look at all the yellow dots”) treats description as care. Kennedy Center “Start with the Arts” grain supports picture-first before text in picture books. None of these require you to become a docent.^24 All of them refuse explain-before-look.

Caption cover is the practical lock. If the label is visible, many children hunt for the “right” answer instead of looking. Cover it. Look. Talk. Then uncover if you want the name.

Object looking still belongs beside picture looking. A mug under two lights teaches edges and shadows before anyone says “value.” Fabric and bark teach texture before the quiz word. A window viewfinder teaches crop before the vocabulary sentence. Fridge work teaches process talk when you refuse pretty-only.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. **Uncover caption first.** Child freezes or recites the title. Usually means explain-before-look. Fix: cover; LOOK-02; wait.
2. **Scavenger mania at the museum.** Clipboard race through twenty works; no second look. Usually means coverage itch. Fix: OUT-01 one-work sit; optional OUT-07 child choice.
3. **“Guess the famous artist.”** Quiz replaced noticing. Usually means adult anxiety about culture knowledge. Fix: notices and evidence first; name lightly after, or tomorrow.
4. **Explain-before-look.** Adult story-dumps the myth or the movement before eyes work. Usually means teaching urge outran the Hour. Fix: silent look; then “What do you see?”
5. **Empty pretty loop.** Only talk is “I like it” / “It’s pretty.” Usually means preference without evidence. Fix: TALK-02 point with words; locked opening with “what makes you say that?”

Neighbor patterns: name-first drawing when looking never happened (return to Chapter 1 + AS-03); pointing at a phone JPEG as “my art” (AS-02 honesty — looking week still needs authorship honesty).

**Five-minute parent warm-up.** Cover a caption on any image in the house. Practice aloud: “What do you see — and what do you see that makes you say that?” Count a slow three. Paraphrase your own notice without adding a lecture. Put the phone away. Tomorrow you will ask the child the same way.

## How to teach it this week


**Parent fluency goal for Friday.** You can hear caption-first in your own mouth and stop mid-sentence. You can paraphrase a child’s notice without upgrading it into a lecture. You can walk past nineteen works to sit with one. You can leave a label covered until after the talk box. If those four are true, looking is becoming a habit in the adult — which is half the teaching.

**Student fluency goal for Friday.** They can offer a notice before a title. They can add one because-I-see. They can survive a short silence. They can choose whether a tiny edge sketch goes in the folder. If those are true, eyes are working — which is the whole point of this chapter.


**Session shape.** Keep Art Hour’s five beats (~20–40 min). This week beat 1 (look/notice) grows — often 5–10 minutes. Beat 2 (make) can be a tiny edge sketch, a texture rubbing, or a quick window crop sketch — looking still leads. Beat 3 uses this chapter’s locked opening. Beat 4 names lightly after, or waits until tomorrow. Beat 5 exit crumb: one notice, one evidence phrase (“because I see…”), one wonder; optional show choice.

Dose honesty: three strong looking sits beat seven rushed ones. Stop while they still want one more look.

**Exact wording you can say.**

- Opening (locked): “What do you see — and what do you see that makes you say that?”
- Silent start: “We’ll look without talking first. I’ll tell you when.”
- After silence: “What’s going on here?” (VTS soft) or “What do you see?” (See–Think–Wonder soft)
- Evidence nudge: “What do you see that makes you say that?”
- More: “What more can you find?”
- Paraphrase: “You’re noticing the dark shape under the handle — did I get that right?”
- Texture: “Feel this one. Where else do your eyes see something like that?”
- Two lights: “What changed when the flashlight moved?”
- After uncover: “The label says ___. Does that change what you notice, or not?”
- Exit: “One see, one because-I-see, one wonder.”

**First try-it for the student.** LOOK-02 Picture Silence Then Notice with a postcard or picture-book page, caption covered. Same day or next: LOOK-01 with a real mug so looking stays tied to objects, not only reproductions.

**How to fade help.** Early week you model one paraphrase. Midweek you only ask the questions. Late week the child leads “what more can you find?” Do not turn paraphrase into a grade. Do not correct a wrong story mid-look — ask for evidence.

**When to stop talking.** During silent look — stop. After each question — wait. If they point, let the point finish. If you feel a lecture rising about a movement or artist, write it on a sticky for *tomorrow* and return to notices today.

## Practice that actually builds learning

Named try-its include time, materials, mess/safety, the fun, and the skill. Practice lives **in the home and outside it**. Library, walk, museum, kitchen, and window *are* the practice — they host the looking move; they do not replace it.

### In-home — LOOK-02 Picture Silence Then Notice

- **Time:** 10–20 min
- **Materials:** One reproduction / picture-book page / postcard; caption covered with sticky or card
- **Mess / safety:** Low
- **The fun:** Secret looking before the “answer” appears.
- **The skill:** Silence; notices; refuse explain-before-look.

**Steps.** Cover caption. Silent look. Ask “What do you notice?” Wait a slow three (classroom-origin pause craft). No story dump yet. Collect notices. Uncover last.

**Stuck.** Child peeks under the sticky. Smile: “Eyes first — we’ll read after.” Re-cover.

### In-home — LOOK-01 Object Before Name

- **Time:** 10–20 min
- **Materials:** Mug, shoe, leaf, or key; blank paper optional
- **Mess / safety:** Low; food off table
- **The fun:** Ordinary object becomes interesting.
- **The skill:** Object before vocabulary — Chapter 1 spine inside looking week.

### In-home — LOOK-03 See–Think–Wonder Soft

- **Time:** 15–25 min
- **Materials:** Artwork or interesting object; three sticky notes or paper columns
- **Mess / safety:** Low
- **The fun:** Three columns feel like a tiny lab of eyes.
- **The skill:** See / think / wonder with evidence talk.

**Steps.** Columns: See / Think / Wonder. Fill See first with observable notices. Think only after See has words — and ask what they see that makes them think that. Wonder last. Adult paraphrases; does not grade.

### In-home — LOOK-04 VTS Three Soft (band 8–10; stretch 5–7)

- **Time:** 15–25 min
- **Materials:** Narrative image with some mystery; no caption
- **Mess / safety:** Low
- **The fun:** Detective talk without a single right plot.
- **The skill:** What’s going on? Evidence? What more?

**Steps.** Ask the three. Point and paraphrase. No praise contest. No “actually it’s ___” mid-protocol. Name lightly after if needed.

### In-home — LOOK-05 Texture Eyes-and-Hands

- **Time:** 15–25 min
- **Materials:** 3–5 safe textured objects (fabric, bark rubbing paper, sandpaper scrap)
- **Mess / safety:** Low–med; wash hands; choke check for younger siblings
- **The fun:** Touch-and-sort before the vocabulary quiz.
- **The skill:** Look and touch; sort by feel/look before naming “texture.”

### In-home — LOOK-06 Same Object Two Lights

- **Time:** 15–25 min
- **Materials:** One object; window light + flashlight; optional quick sketch
- **Mess / safety:** Low
- **The fun:** Magician light changes the same mug.
- **The skill:** Notice edges/shadows before naming “value” or “shade.”

### In-home — LOOK-07 Fridge Gallery Tour

- **Time:** 10–20 min
- **Materials:** Family fridge / wall of prior work
- **Mess / safety:** Low
- **The fun:** Child as curator of their own wall.
- **The skill:** Process talk; specific noticing; refuse pretty-only.

**Steps.** Child picks one piece. “Tell me about how you made it — not whether it is pretty.” Adult offers specific noticing (“I see repeated dashes in the corner”).

### In-home — LOOK-08 Window Frame Find

- **Time:** 10–20 min
- **Materials:** Window; cardboard viewfinder or hands as frame
- **Mess / safety:** Low; no climbing sills
- **The fun:** Crop the world like a tiny movie.
- **The skill:** Edges that cut objects; “crop” named lightly after looking.

**Opening (locked):** “What do you see — and what do you see that makes you say that?”

**Follow-ups (3–5):**

## For the student

Looking is a superpower you already have. Art hour asks you to use it before anyone tells you the title.

**The idea.** You look. You say what you see. You say what makes you say that. You may change your mind when you look again.

**Tiny worked example.** A postcard sits on the table. The words are covered. You look quietly. You say: “I see a big dark shape on the left. I think it might be a doorway because I see a rectangle and a slice of light. I wonder what is on the other side.” Then you uncover the words. Maybe the title matches your idea. Maybe it does not. Either way, your looking counted.

**Try 1 — Cover and notice.** Cover the title on a picture-book page or postcard. Look without talking. Then tell three things you see. For each one, add “because I see ___.”

**Try 2 — Object, texture, or window.** Choose one: (a) look at a mug and name five notices; (b) feel two safe textures and say how eyes and hands agree or disagree; (c) frame a piece of the window with your hands and tell what the frame cuts off.

**Explain it back.** Why do we cover the words first? What is the difference between “I like it” and “I see a jagged edge”? What should you do at a museum — race every room, or sit with one work?

**Challenge.** At a museum or library, choose **one** picture or one book spread. Sit with it. No race. Use the question: “What do you see — and what do you see that makes you say that?” Optional: sketch one edge you noticed.

You are allowed to wonder. You are allowed to be wrong about the story and still be a good looker — if you point at what you see. You are allowed to change your mind on the second look.

## If it isn’t clicking

**Diagnostic 1 — They only recite the title or the myth.** Caption or adult story came first. Next move: cover labels for several sits; LOOK-02 only; paraphrase notices; uncover at the very end or next day. Write your own urge to explain on a sticky for tomorrow.

**Diagnostic 2 — Museum meltdown or boredom race.** Too many works; feet ahead of eyes. Next move: one-work rule; child chooses (OUT-07); short sketch of one edge; leave while they still want one more look. Gift shop is not the assessment.

**Diagnostic 3 — Pretty-only talk.** Preference loop with no evidence. Next move: TALK-02 / locked evidence question; require one “because I see” before any like/dislike. On fridge tour, ask how they made a mark, not whether it is pretty.

**Extra pattern — looking quiz on a phone.** Photo-to-key is banned in the Art Hour AI box. If a tool offers a filled answer key for a postcard quiz, close it. Eyes first.



**Diagnostic 4 — They look for two seconds and demand the next picture.** Coverage itch in the child. Next move: timer visible for a short silent look (even one minute); celebrate staying; one-work rule on outings; stop before boredom turns into fight.

**Diagnostic 5 — Adult cannot stop narrating.** Your own explain-before-look habit. Next move: parent warm-up alone daily for three days; write facts on tomorrow’s sticky; during the Hour only questions and paraphrases.


**When to slow down.** Silent look keeps getting skipped; every sit becomes a quiz; outings are worksheets only; VTS length arrives before silence habits.

**When to go ahead.** Notices before vocabulary; one OUT or reproduction sit with caption covered; evidence phrases showing up orally; child revises a first idea kindly.

**When to get a human tutor.** Vision concerns, language access needs, or anxiety that does not ease with shorter sits — ask a human who knows your child. Looking protocols are not therapy claims. A scavenger failure is not an identity verdict.

Rescue order: ask, wait, hint, short demo of *looking* (you model one notice on the shared image), return the turn to them. Do not grab the conclusion. Do not fill silence with “pretty.”

## Mark-making and line

## Why this matters

Marks are how a hand thinks on paper. Lines are paths and edges — fat, thin, shaky, bold, accidental, kept.

This chapter owns authorship. Chapter 1 locked make before polish. Chapter 2 trained eyes. Here the tool stays in the child’s hand: first mark block, tool swap, cover-the-demo, accident keep, fat-to-thin ladder, follow-the-edge mug, blind contour soft at 8–10, line hunt walk, music line map, left/right-hand soft as a fun stretch — not a therapy claim.

Without this chapter, Art Hour becomes adult-finished dogs, uncovered demos the child copies stroke-for-stroke, “I can’t draw” met with talent shame, and coloring-book lines as the only hour. With it, a five-to-seven-year-old marks freely with fat tools and learns that drips can become something. An eight-to-ten-year-old slows along a mug’s rim after looking, tries blind contour soft, and can say whose mark is on the page.

**Why struggle is allowed.** A wobbly rim is information. A drip is a chance. A demo that stays covered forces the hand to search. Rescue too early — grabbing the pencil “just to show” — teaches that the adult’s hand is the real artist. Shame a wobbly line and they learn to freeze. Ask, wait, hint, short demo on *demo paper*, return the tool.

**Why the child’s page is sacred this week.** Matching a sample is not the goal. Likeness may grow later from looking. Process is the effect of making on the child. The fridge picture proves a sitting happened. It is not the grade. Presenting still exists: they choose whether a page goes up and can say one reason.

You do not need to be a studio artist. You do need to leave their mark on their page — and to keep your demo on demo paper.

**Why coloring books are demoted, not demonized.** Sheets can quiet a waiting room. They are not Art Hour when they are the only mark diet. Open mark teaches authorship. Sheets teach staying inside someone else’s edge. Both can exist in a house. Only one fulfills this chapter.

**Why generators fail this lesson even when they look charming.** A fluent “kids crayon” JPEG skips the child’s looking and marking. Filing it as Drawing lies about whose mark. Parent tools may prepare *you*; they may not become the project. Chapter 1 and the Art Hour AI box already said this — this chapter enforces it at the page.



## For the parent: understand it yourself

Many adults “help” by finishing. The ear looks better. The dog stands up. The fridge looks nicer. The child learns a quiet lesson: my hand is not enough. This week you practice a different fluency — hearing adult-filled marks, uncovered demos, “draw it nice” freezes, coloring-book-as-only-hour, and “I can’t draw” talent talk — and answering with a shorter task and a returned tool.

**Precise picture.** Contour, in ordinary language, means slow edge-following — eyes on the edge, hand reporting. Blind contour soft means eyes on the object while the pencil moves, with a peek version allowed for younger or nervous hands.^27 Salome’s studio emphasis on points of high visual information and contour travel as looking moves — keep as studio craft, not as a kitchen RCT. Studio Habits “Stretch and Explore” grain fits accident keep — adapt lightly from high-school studio research; do not claim a K–2 trial.^30

Shape play (SHAPE bank) may support mark and collage later; this chapter stays on MARK and LINE as primary. Color puddles wait for Chapter 4 unless a washable drip appears in accident keep.

## For the student

Your marks matter. Nobody else gets to pretend their hand was yours.

**The idea.** You look. You hold the tool. You try. Wobbly is allowed. Accidents can stay. A picture a machine made is not your mark.

**Tiny worked example.** A mug sits on the table. You look at the rim. A grown-up draws one slow rim on their own paper, then covers it. You get a blank. You follow the rim. It wobbles. You keep going. Later someone asks: “Whose mark is on this page — and what did your hand try?” You say: “Mine. I tried a slow rim. I pressed harder on the handle side.” That is art hour working.

**Challenge.** On a walk, hunt straight, curved, and zigzag lines (LINE-01). Sketch one. Or rub a leaf or bark (OUT-03) and talk about the lines you got. At home, try blind contour soft of a shoe for one minute — funny lines welcome.

You are allowed to struggle. You are allowed to ask for a hint after you try. You are allowed to say “please don’t draw on my page.” You are allowed to keep a page that is not pretty.

## If it isn’t clicking

**Diagnostic 1 — Adult hands keep “helping.”** Polish anxiety. Next move: physical rule — adult pencil never lands on child paper; demo paper only; AS-02 every Friday until habit sticks.

**Diagnostic 2 — Freeze at likeness / “I can’t draw.”** Looking ahead of hand or talent story. Next move: shorten to free mark or one rim; fat tool; refuse talent lecture; peek blind contour; celebrate what the hand tried.

**Diagnostic 3 — Only coloring books all week.** No open mark diet. Next move: schedule MARK-01 and LINE-04 as Art Hour; sheets become optional quiet toys (AS-05). Not banned as toys — banned as the only hour.

**Diagnostic 4 — Demo addiction.** Panic when demo is covered. Next move: shorter demo; longer cover; hint with words (“watch the place where the handle meets the cup”) before any re-demo.

**Diagnostic 5 — Generated image in the folder.** Authorship blur. Next move: honesty talk; delete from child-work; MARK-01 today; process notes may name a tool used for *parent* prep, never as their drawing.

**When to slow down.** Adult-filled marks recur; every page aims at fridge polish; free mark never happens; scissors/knives enter early (they don’t belong in this age book’s spine).

**When to go ahead.** Child’s page is theirs; one edge followed after looking; demo stays covered; they can answer the locked opening; accident kept or transformed once.

**When to get a human tutor.** Pain while gripping, sensory distress with tools, or fine-motor concerns beyond kitchen tips — ask a human who knows your child. Fun left/right swaps are not therapy. Grip research is context, not diagnosis.

Rescue order: ask, wait, hint, short demo on *demo paper*, return the tool. Correct the reason (adult-filled, generated-as-theirs) without crushing the look or the try.

# Chapter 4 — Color and paint light

![Tempera color puddles and a fat crayon on a wooden table — still-life, no people, no logos.](book/art/ch04-opener.jpg){width=100%}

## Why this matters

Color arrives at the kitchen table before anyone opens a wheel chart. A yellow puddle touches a blue puddle. Something new shows up on the paper. That meeting is the lesson.

If you teach the name first — primary, secondary, complementary — the child hunts for the right word instead of watching the paint. Look before name still holds here. Make before polish holds too. A muddy orange that the child mixed is worth more than a perfect rainbow the adult painted on the side for “how it should look.”

This chapter unlocks three ordinary skills. First, the child learns that colors change when they meet — on paper, on a tray, in water, even in the grocery aisle where three greens refuse to be the same. Second, the child learns to notice light and dark inside one hue before anyone says *value* or *tint*. Third, you learn to keep your hands off a mix you would “fix,” and to cover the table before the lid comes off.

Worth the struggle: a five-year-old who can point at what happened when red met yellow will later mix with purpose. An eight-year-old who can work from two tubes plus white will not freeze when a sample kit offers twelve. A parent who can say “what happened when the colors met?” instead of “now make green” is teaching looking, not decorating.

You do not need a studio. You need washable paint with an **AP** seal (or labelled **Conforms to ASTM D-4236**), a covered table, a fat brush, and the courage to leave a drip alone. Mess honesty before you start. Food and drinks off the art table. The Art Hour safety spine already said this once; this chapter lives it with paint.

Color week is not a miniature design course and not a promise that art raises reading scores. Justify the hour by what looking and mixing teach. Then put two puddles down.

A second reason this chapter matters: paint teaches honesty about materials. Washable tempera behaves differently from a crayon. Water changes a pan color. A covered table is part of the craft, not a punishment. When you preview the mess and keep food off the table, you are teaching the same care a studio teaches — scaled to a kitchen. When you refuse solvent cans from the garage, you are protecting the hour so looking can stay the point.

A third reason: color is one of the places adult polish shows up fastest. “Pretty” and “right green” arrive before the child has looked. If you can hold the line here — notice the meeting, name lightly, leave the drip — you will hold it more easily in collage, print, and clay. Look before name; make before polish. Color is the loud practice ground for both.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

**Everyday picture.** Your child has two puddles on a tray — yellow and blue. They swirl. The mix turns a soft green-brown. Your hand wants to grab a clean brush, add more yellow, and rescue the rainbow. You say, “That’s muddy — let’s fix it so it looks like real green.” The child’s face closes a little. The page becomes yours. Tomorrow they wait for you to finish every mix.

**Precise picture.** Same puddles. Same soft green-brown. You wait. You ask, “What happened when the colors met — before we name the mix?” The child points: “It got darker.” or “It turned funny green.” You say, “Yes — you made that.” Later, lightly, you may say *green* or *olive* if the word helps. You do not wipe the mix away. Muddy is data. The child’s mark stays the child’s mark. Next week they swirl on purpose to see what happens.

Color play at this age is noticing change. Limited palette — two or three colors, maybe white — is a craft constraint that sharpens looking. It is not a punishment for wanting more tubes. Warm and cool can wait until the child has sorted piles by feel; then you name lightly. Light and dark in water come before the word *value*. A grocery aisle with three different greens is real color study without opening a bottle.

Many adults feel rusty because their own art hours were laminated wheels and “stay inside the lines.” That is normal. You are not behind. You are learning to hear a name-first rush and an adult-filled fix — and to ask a good question instead of grabbing the brush.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. **“Make it the right green like the chart.”** Usually means name-first and sample-matched polish. Reply: “Show me what your green did. We can look at the chart tomorrow — after your mix.”
2. **“I’ll just fix this muddy part for you.”** Adult rainbow fix. Reply: hands off. Talk muddy as information. Offer a clean corner of paper if they want a second try — their brush, not yours on their page.
3. **“We can’t paint until we learn all the color words.”** Coverage itch. Reply: two puddles first; words after seeing. Depth is the hour; the wheel is a map.
4. **“These oil paints from the garage are fine — it says art on the can.”** Wrong materials. Stop. Check for ACMI **AP** and/or **Conforms to ASTM D-4236**. No solvents for this age book. No **CL** products through grade 6. Kitchen ≠ adult oil studio.
5. **“Snack on the paint table while we mix.”** Food on the art table. Move food. Cover the table. Wash hands after. “Non-toxic” does not mean edible.

**Five-minute parent warm-up.** Alone, before tomorrow’s Hour: cover a scrap of newspaper. Put two washable puddles down. Mix once with a fat brush. Ask yourself out loud: “What happened when the colors met?” Practice waiting three slow counts before any vocabulary sentence. Notice one drip you would usually wipe. Leave it. Put the scrap away. You do not need to be a painter. You need to leave the child’s mix alone long enough for looking to happen.

**One more precise picture — light and dark.** Same blue pan. One fat wet stroke. One watery pale stroke. The child says “that one is sleepy” or “that one is loud.” You do not rush to say *tint* and *shade*. You say, “You noticed a light one and a dark one in the same blue.” Tomorrow you may offer the word *lighter* as a bridge. The word *value* can wait until it earns its keep at the table — and even then, one plain sentence is enough.

**About charts.** A color wheel is a map, not a gate. Some families enjoy looking at a wheel *after* several mix hours, the way a map is useful after a walk. Looking at the wheel before any puddle often turns the hour into vocabulary class. If a vendor page or workbook opens with the wheel, you may skip to the mix, then return to the chart as a “does this match what we saw?” check — child’s mix first.

## How to teach it this week

**Session shape (Art Hour, ~20–40 min).**

1. **Look / notice (3–8 min).** Put tomorrow’s two colors out still closed, or put three crayon stubs of “yellow” that are not the same. Ask what they notice — before any wheel, before any mix.
2. **Short make (8–18 min).** Child holds the brush. Two puddles meet. Or same hue with more and less water. Demo only on *demo paper*, then return the tool. Do not paint the “right” version on their sheet.
3. **Talk box (3–8 min).** Locked opening below. Wait. One or two follow-ups. Stop talking while they look.
4. **Name lightly (2–5 min, optional).** One word after seeing: green, darker, warmer, olive. Naming can wait until tomorrow. Do not turn the end of the hour into a quiz.
5. **Exit crumb (2–5 min).** Child chooses: keep the drip, wash for next time, point at one place the colors met, or dictate three words you write in the margin.

**Exact sentences you can say**

- “What do you notice about these two puddles — before we mix?”
- “Your turn with the brush. I’ll watch.”
- “What happened when the colors met — before we name the mix?”
- “Muddy tells us they mixed a lot. That’s information, not a mistake.”
- “We can name it after you’ve looked.”
- “I’m leaving your mix. Tell me what it did.”
- “Same blue family — what changed when you added water?”

**First try-it for the student: COLOR-01 Two-Puddle Mix** (band 5–7; fine for both).

- **Time:** 20–30 min inside the Hour.
- **Materials:** Tempera or liquid watercolor with **AP** seal; two primaries (yellow + blue, or yellow + red); fat brush; paper; table cover; smock; wet cloth.
- **Mess / safety:** Medium–high. Cover table first (**SAFE-02**). Smocks. Food off table. Wash hands. Washable only. Adult present for wet media.
- **The fun:** Watching a third color appear that neither bottle held alone.
- **The skill:** Mix on paper or tray; notice the new color; name after seeing. No perfect rainbow chart required on day one.

**How to fade help.** Day one: place the two puddles and wait. If the child freezes, hint: “What if the brush visits both puddles?” Short demo on demo paper — one swirl only. Return the brush. Do not finish their green. Day two: they choose which two colors meet. Day three: they decide whether to mix a little or a lot, then tell what changed.

**When to stop talking.** After the opening question. After one follow-up. While the brush is moving. When they are mid-look at the meeting place. Silence is part of the mix. Do not fill it with “pretty” or with a lecture on primaries.

**Age-band moves inside this week**

Mess preview (**SAFE-05**) before lids open: “This hour will be wet. We have cover, smock, and wash time.” Prefer water-based washables. Tear-and-crayon color walks are allowed on a low-energy day.

Still refuse adult rainbow fix. Still washable AP. Written three-word crumb optional. If looking habits are weak, stay on two-puddle and grocery greens longer even if the birthday says nine. Place by skill, not birthday.

**This-week plan (honest “lesson plan”).** One focus: notice mix before name. Three to five Art Hours. One out-of-home color look (**OUT-05** or a neighborhood yellow hunt). Friday: one diagnostic pattern (did an adult fix appear?) plus done-enough checklist. Not twelve tubes. Not a 180-day craft dump.

## Practice that actually builds learning

**Opening (locked):** “What happened when the colors met — before we name the mix?”

**Follow-ups (pick three to five):**

1. Look: “What do you *see* in the place they touched?”

3. Evidence: “What do you see that makes you say that?”
4. Process: “How did you get that darker part?” / “Whose mark made that swirl?”

### Mixing variables you can name lightly (after seeing)

When the child is ready, you may point at *what changed* without a lecture:

- **Which colors met** (yellow + blue vs yellow + red).
- **How much of each** (a little blue vs a lot).
- **How much water** (fat dark vs pale bloom).
- **How long the brush stirred** (quick touch vs long swirl).

Each variable is something they can try on purpose next time. You are not assigning a science fair write-up. You are giving handles for the next mix. ## For the student

Color is what happens when paints or crayons meet — and when light or water changes how a color looks. You do not have to know the fancy names first. You look. You mix. Then we talk about what you see.

**Tiny example.** Yellow puddle. Blue puddle. Your brush visits both. A new color shows up in the middle. That new color is yours because your hand made the meeting. If it looks “funny” or muddy, it still counts. Muddy means the colors mixed a lot. That is useful information.

**Try 1 — Mix and point.** Put two puddles on your paper or tray. Mix once. Point to where they met. Tell someone what you see — before anyone says the color’s name. You can say “darker,” “softer,” “like grass,” or “weird green.” Your words are enough.

**Try 2 — Same color, different water.** Take one color and a water jar. Make a fat dark spot. Make a pale watery spot of the same color. What changed? Point at both. Keep one watery bloom on purpose.

**Explain it back.** In your words: what should you do first — mix and look, or say the name of the mix? Tell a grown-up. If you change your mind after another look, that is allowed.

**Challenge.** Keep one accidental drip on purpose. Tomorrow, tell what that drip could become without wiping it away. Optional: at the store or on a walk, find three greens (or yellows) that are not the same. Tell how they differ.

You are allowed to wait. You are allowed to use a fat brush. You are allowed to stop while you still want one more swirl. Your muddy mix is still your mark. Nobody else gets to finish it for you unless you ask for a tip after you have tried — and even then, the tip happens on demo paper, then the brush comes back to you.

**A note about “I can’t paint.”** Often that means looking got ahead of the hand, or someone once wiped your mix away. Shorten the job: two puddles, one meeting, one sentence about what you see. You do not have to fill the page. You do not have to make a rainbow. You have to notice.

**Whose mark?** If a grown-up paints on your page to “help,” ask for a fresh sheet. Your Art folder should show your swirls. A picture a computer made is not your mix — even if it looks “better.” Your looking counts.

## If it isn’t clicking

**Diagnostic 1 — Adult rainbow fix.**  
You hear yourself saying “I’ll just fix this,” or you reach for their brush, or you paint the “right” green on their page.  
Next move: put your hands in your pockets. Say the locked opening. Offer a clean corner for a second try with *their* brush. If you already painted on their page, label that corner “demo” honestly and start a fresh sheet for them. Rescue order: ask → wait → hint → short demo on demo paper → return the tool. Do not grab the brush. Do not fill the silence with “pretty.”

**Diagnostic 2 — Mess meltdown.**  
Tears, refusal, panic, or shutdown when paint hits the table or the sleeve.  
Next move: you likely skipped mess honesty. Next time, **SAFE-05** before lids: “This hour will be wet. We have cover, smock, and wash time.” Smaller puddles. Washable AP only. Shorter make block; keep look and talk. Tempera sticks or crayon color walks on a hard day. Product-only fridge hour is not the goal — but a flooded table is not required either. Build wash time into the clock so the hour does not end in a rush.

**Diagnostic 3 — Wrong materials or solvent smell.**  
Can says oil, enamel, or shows a **CL** seal; or you smell solvent; or someone suggests “real artist” turpentine for kids.  
Next move: stop. Cap it. **SAFE-01 / SAFE-04**. Switch to washable children’s tempera or watercolor with AP / ASTM D-4236. Kitchen art for ages 5–10 does not use turpentine, mineral spirits, or solvent adhesives. Read the label. “Non-toxic” is not a snack invitation. If something was swallowed or got in eyes, follow the label and call Poison Control 1-800-222-1222.

**Extra pattern — Name-first freeze.** Child shrugs, whispers “green?” and watches your face. Soften: point at the paper. “What do you *see* — before the name?” Accept “darker” as a full answer.

**When to slow down.** Stay on two puddles and oral notice until the child can point at a meeting place without hunting a vocabulary word. Stay there even if a workbook says “grade 2 color wheel.”

**When to go ahead.** If they mix and notice easily, add light/dark water, then warm/cool sort, then limited palette still from a real object.

**When to get a human tutor.** If sensory distress with wet media stays intense after mess preview and small amounts, or if fine-motor pain appears with brushes, ask a trusted teacher or occupational therapist for tool ideas — fat tools, tempera sticks, crayon resist — while you keep look-before-name. No shame. Looking can continue with dry color walks. A tutor helps tools and comfort; they do not replace the child’s mix with a sample.

**Diagnostic note on boredom.** If the child says “this is babyish,” check skill not birthday. Offer limited palette still from a real object, or grocery greens with a because-I-see sentence. If they are actually tired of wet mess, switch to COLOR-03 dry collect for a day, then return. Boredom can mean wrong band *or* too much polish pressure — ask which.

**Correcting without crushing.** Wrong reason: “Muddy means I failed.” Better frame: “Muddy means a lot of meeting happened. Next time you can mix less if you want a brighter middle — your choice.” Then let them try. Do not brighten it for them.

Correct a wrong reason without crushing the look: “You thought muddy meant broken. Muddy means the colors mixed a lot. That’s useful.” Never correct by wiping their page clean for them.

You may look at a composition thumbnail together, then put it away before the child works from life. You may ask for a labelled SCRIPT of questions for *you* to say after the try. You may ask for a hint that is a question (“what if you rinse and try a watery corner?”), not a finished painting.

You may not ask a generator to “make my child’s painting,” hang a fluent mix as theirs, generate a “kids crayon” JPEG as their mark, turn in a generated image as student work, leave a generator unsupervised during making, or write their artist statement as the only draft. Bastani’s high-school math trial is **OFF-AGE for art hour** — one caution from the Art Hour box, not a kitchen coefficient.

## What “done enough” looks like

Before you leave a focused color week (or before you thin the focus to one COLOR card as review):

- [ ] Child mixed at least two colors (or one color with water) and noticed a new color or a light/dark change.
- [ ] Naming happened **after** looking — or waited until tomorrow.
- [ ] Table was covered; food off the art table; washable **AP** (or ASTM D-4236) materials used; mess preview said out loud at least once.
- [ ] You caught at least one adult-rainbow-fix impulse and left the child’s mix alone (or labelled your demo honestly).
- [ ] Talk box used the locked opening once; child pointed at something on the page or produce.
- [ ] One out-of-home color look happened (grocery, walk, or window) *or* is clearly scheduled this week.
- [ ] Exit crumb exists: oral notice, three-word caption (adult may scribe at 5–7), kept drip with a reason, or a photo of *their* page in the dated folder — not a generated stand-in.
- [ ] Folder or sketchbook shows the child’s marks, not adult-finished rainbows filed as theirs.

Place by skill, not birthday. A seven-year-old ready for limited palette may try it. A nine-year-old who still freezes at mud may stay on two puddles and grocery greens. A “grade 2 art workbook” rainbow page is a publisher’s scope — not a legal grade, and not your transcript line. The work lives under **Art**. Never under a brand name. Never under Little Thinkers Art I.

Done enough is permission to move toward collage and print with the same spine: look before name; make before polish. Revisit a favorite mix next Tuesday if you want. Mix last week’s idea into a torn-paper hour later. Stop while wanting one more swirl. The fridge picture is evidence a sitting happened — not the grade. Process is what the mix did to the child’s looking.
**Life of the habit (no ninth chapter).** Next Tuesday, reopen a dry mix page and ask what they notice now. Mix last week’s leftover idea into a torn-paper hour in Chapter 5. Stop while wanting one more swirl. The habit is the rhythm — look, mix, talk, name lightly, crumb — not a diploma and not a percentile.


If energy is low, shorten the make block, keep the look and the talk box, and stop while wanting one more mark. Dose honesty includes rest. There is no national study that says otherwise — and you do not need one to choose a shorter Tuesday.

Tomorrow’s object can be as small as two closed bottles and a covered table. That is enough to begin. Look first. Mix second. Name lightly. Leave the drip. Your child’s meeting place on the paper is the curriculum for this chapter.

When in doubt, ask the locked opening again and wait. The paint will answer if you let it.

# Chapter 5 — Collage, print, and paper

![A tear collage beside a foam stamp — still-life, no people, no logos.](book/art/ch05-opener.jpg){width=100%}

## Why this matters

Paper teaches two big looking moves before any fancy knife enters the room. First: arrange, then stick. Second: press, lift, look. Collage and print share this chapter because both ask the child to notice what the *material* did — a torn edge, a layered peek, a stamp’s repeat, a leaf’s veins on the page — not only whether the finished page matches a sample on the fridge.

Look before name still holds. You do not begin with “this is monoprinting” while the paper is still dry. Make before polish holds harder here than in crayon hour. Adult hands love to cut the “nice” shapes, line up the mosaic, and reprint the stamp until it looks store-bought. The child’s torn edge and slightly crooked press are the work.

This chapter unlocks authorship in paper. A five- to seven-year-old who tears before fancy cutting learns that irregular edges are information. An eight- to ten-year-old who pulls a foam-plate print compares plate to paper and sees what the press changed. You learn to keep craft knives out of this age book, to prefer glue stick over solvent adhesives, and to ask what the paper or the press did that they can point at.

Worth the struggle: collage without a photocopied template teaches decision. Print without intaglio tools teaches cause and effect you can see. Library scraps and fallen leaves cost little. The skill is arrange-before-stick and press–lift–look — not a craft-store diploma.

Safety stays ordinary and firm: labelled children’s scissors; adult present; no craft knives; washable inks and AP glues; non-toxic plants for leaf prints; choke check on small scraps if younger siblings share the floor. Tear before fancy cut. Air and paper first; polish never first.

Paper is also a bridge between drawing and making in space. A torn shape sits on a page the way a coil will later sit on a board. Overlap in tissue prepares eyes for overlap in drawing. A stamp’s repeat is rhythm you can hear with your eyes. You are not rushing into a print studio credit. You are giving the child material cause-and-effect they can point at — the same spine as color week’s puddle meeting, now with paper and pressure.

Homeschool art hours often default to coloring-book outlines because they feel tidy. Collage and print politely refuse tidy as the only virtue. Mess honesty still applies: ink hours need cover and wash time. Glue hours need a wipe cloth. The goal is not chaos. The goal is authorship you can see in a torn edge.


Worth one more sentence for busy parents: you do not need a die-cut machine, a silhouette cutter, or a craft-knife set to teach real paper art at 5–10. You need scraps, a glue stick, washable ink, time to arrange, and the will to leave a crooked stamp alone. That will is the teaching. The materials are ordinary.

If your home has been coloring-book-only for months, this chapter is a gentle reset. Tear week is not an insult to a nine-year-old. It is skill placement. AS-style honesty later will ask whose mark is on the page — start practicing that honesty with torn edges now.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

**Everyday picture.** Everyone at the table gets the same printed template. You pre-cut the shapes “so it looks nice.” The child glues where the dotted lines suggest. Three pages look identical. You praise “pretty.” Tomorrow they wait for you to cut again. Authorship quietly left the room.

**Precise picture.** A stack of colored paper. One background sheet. You say, “Tear some shapes. Arrange them before any glue.” The child tears a jagged blue river and a red sun with a fuzzy edge. They move pieces three times. Then they glue. You ask, “What did the paper or the press do that you can point at?” They point at the fuzzy sun edge: “It looks soft because I tore it.” You do not re-cut the sun. Naming *collage* can wait until after the arrange.

For print: a foam scrap or a leaf, washable paint, paper. Press. Lift. Look. The surprise mark is the teacher. A second pull is optional. Foam plate lines at 8–10 use a dull pencil — not a craft knife. If a potato stamp appears, *you* cut the potato; the child presses.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. **“Let me cut that so it looks right.”** Adult takeover for polish. Reply: “Tear version first. If you want scissors later, labelled children’s scissors — your hands.”
2. **“Everyone make the same owl from the template.”** Sample-only collage as the hour. Reply: template can be a warm-up look; the make is child’s arrangement from scraps.
3. **“We’ll carve this with a craft knife — it’s more real.”** Wrong tool for this age book. Reply: foam plate + dull pencil, or stamp soft. No craft-knife intaglio here. Later depth lives in other books and older years.
4. **“This glue from the garage works better — it says industrial.”** Solvent adhesive risk. Reply: AP glue stick preferred for 5–7; school glue that is water-based and labelled for children. No solvent glues as kitchen art.
5. **“Just stamp until it matches the sample border.”** Press–lift–look replaced by product matching. Reply: one press, look, talk; repeats for rhythm after noticing, not for identical store finish.

**Five-minute parent warm-up.** Alone: tear three shapes from scrap paper. Arrange on a background. Move them twice before any glue. Glue once. Ask yourself: “What did the paper do that I can point at?” Notice one torn edge you would usually trim. Leave it. For print warm-up: press a leaf underside with washable paint onto scrap; lift; look at veins before any vocabulary. Put tools away. You are practicing arrange-before-stick and press–lift–look — not building a portfolio piece.

**Precise picture — print compare.** Foam plate with a dull-pencil spiral. Light ink. Paper pressed with a spoon. Lift. The spiral on paper is softer than the plate. The child says, “It got quieter.” You say, “You can point at what the press did.” Later you may say *print*. You do not recarve the plate to force a sharper match to an Instagram sample. Compare plate to paper is the looking move.

**About “real” printmaking.** Copper plates, acid, and craft-knife linocut are real in older studios. They are not this book’s tools. Foam, leaf, stamp, and mono pull are not fake art. They are age-fit presses. *Art for Homeschooling* can own later medium depth; this Hour owns press–lift–look without knives.


**Warm/cool of paper.** Scraps have temperature too — red tissue vs blue magazine sky. After overlap peek, you may invite a soft warm/cool sort of leftover scraps for tomorrow’s bin. Name lightly after their sort. Color chapter skills recycle; do not restart vocabulary class.

**Five wrong-answer repairs in one breath.** Hear polish → return tool. Hear template-only → scrap basket. Hear knife → foam/dull pencil. Hear solvent glue → AP stick. Hear “match the sample” → locked opening about what *their* paper did.

## How to teach it this week

**Session shape (Art Hour, ~20–40 min).**

1. **Look / notice (3–8 min).** Feel paper edges — torn vs cut scraps already on the table. Or look at a leaf’s underside veins. Or look at a stamp’s shape before inking.
2. **Short make (8–18 min).** Tear and arrange before glue; or press–lift–look once. Child’s hands on scissors only when labelled children’s scissors and skill are ready — tear first for 5–7. Demo on demo paper only.
3. **Talk box (3–8 min).** Locked opening. Wait. Point with words at paper or press marks.
4. **Name lightly (2–5 min, optional).** *Collage*, *print*, *overlap*, *repeat* — after seeing. Can wait.
5. **Exit crumb (2–5 min).** Choose a page for the wall with one reason; or say one process sentence: “I tore first” / “I pressed and lifted.”

**Exact sentences you can say**

- “Tear some shapes. Arrange before any glue.”
- “What changed when you moved that piece?”
- “Press — lift — look. What do you see?”
- “What did the paper or the press do that you can point at?”
- “I’ll cut the potato. You press.”
- “Dull pencil on foam only — no craft knives in this hour.”
- “Your torn edge stays. We are not trimming it pretty unless you ask after you’ve looked.”

**First try-it: COLLAGE-01 Tear Not Cut First** (band 5–7; useful reset for any age).

- **Time:** 20–30 min.
- **Materials:** Colored paper; glue stick (AP); background sheet; table cover.
- **Mess / safety:** Medium; glue stick preferred over liquid for 5–7; wash hands; cover table.
- **The fun:** Fuzzy edges and surprise shapes that scissors would have “fixed.”
- **The skill:** Tear shapes; arrange before gluing; look at tear edges. Looking at edges of tears is the point.

**How to fade help.** Day one: you may start one tear to show the motion on demo paper, then stop. Day two: child tears all. Day three: arrange for a full minute before glue bottle appears. For print: you ink once as demo; they press. Fade until their presses are the page.

**When to stop talking.** After “arrange before glue.” After the locked opening. During the press. Do not narrate every scrap into a story they did not choose. Do not say “pretty” as the only word.

**Age-band moves**

- **5–7:** Tear-first collage; glue stick; large scraps; hand/found stamp soft; leaf underside with adult; mono pull with small paint amounts; oral process notice; adult may scribe. Scissors only as labelled children’s scissors with adult present — snips on strips before interior cuts (**SAFE-03**). No craft knives. Choke check if yarn bits or buttons appear.
- **8–10:** Add overlap peek with tissue; cut strip mosaic; found-paper portrait soft (not a photocopy of adult sample); foam plate line print with dull pencil; repeat stamp border with rhythm talk; written process crumb optional. Still refuse craft-knife intaglio. Still child’s presses. If tear-and-arrange habits are weak, stay there even if birthday says nine.

**This-week plan.** Focus: arrange-before-stick *or* press–lift–look (you may alternate days). Three to five Hours. One OUT cousin — nature leaf collect for PRINT-03, or library picture-book look at collage/print pages *after* a home make, not instead of it. Friday: done-enough + one diagnostic (adult cut everything?).

**Sample dialogue (illustration).**  
Parent: “Tear five shapes. No glue yet.”  
Child: *(tears, arranges, rearranges)*  
Parent: “What changed when you moved the blue one?”  
Child: “It looked like water under the red.”  
Parent: “Glue when you like it.” *(later)* “What did the paper or the press do that you can point at?”  
Child: “This edge is fuzzy. Scissors would make it sharp.”  
Parent: “Yes. That’s something the tear did.”

**Scissors card in the week.** One short **SAFE-03** sitting: labelled children’s scissors, snip paper strips into a pile for later mosaic. Open/close practice. No invented hard age lock — follow the tool label and the child’s skill. Adult present. Tear days remain available forever; scissors are an add, not a promotion that ends tearing.

**Safety this week (paper and press).** Labelled children’s scissors; adult present; **no craft knives**. Washable stamp pads / tempera. AP glue. Non-toxic plants only for leaf prints; wash hands. Small parts — choke check. Food off table. Cover for inky hours. Solvent adhesives stay in the garage, not on the art table.


**Fade map across five sittings.** (1) Tear + arrange, adult holds glue until asked. (2) Child glues own arrangement. (3) Add one scissors strip pile. (4) First stamp presses with look-between. (5) Foam plate or leaf compare. Skip ahead only when the prior sitting’s skill shows up without nagging. Birthday does not skip the map.

**When to stop the make early.** If glue frustration spikes, switch to dry arrange-only for five minutes and end with talk box. If ink floods, wipe, smaller amounts, one more press maximum. Ending early with a real notice beats finishing a soggy page for the fridge.

## Practice that actually builds learning

### Collage try-its (in-home)

**COLLAGE-01 Tear Not Cut First** — above; default opener.

**COLLAGE-02 Scrap Story Soft** — band both; 20–35 min.  
Materials: magazine or safe scrap paper; labelled children’s scissors; glue; background.  
Mess / safety: medium; adult present for scissors.  
The fun: building a scene or abstract from chosen scraps after a look prompt (“find busy textures” / “find quiet colors”).  
The skill: choose after looking; child decides when glued. Not a race to cover the page.

**COLLAGE-03 Texture Collage Tray** — band both; 20–35 min.  
Materials: fabric scraps, paper, yarn bits; glue; tray.  
Mess / safety: medium–high; choke check for younger siblings; wash hands.  
The fun: feel-then-stick. Rough next to smooth.  
The skill: sort by feel; name texture lightly after sorting. Looking with fingers supports looking with eyes.

**COLLAGE-04 Overlap Peek** — band 8–10; 20–30 min.  
Materials: translucent paper or tissue (AP dyes); glue stick; background.  
Mess / safety: medium; tissue can stain — cover table; smocks.  
The fun: colors change when layers meet — a cousin of paint mix, in paper.  
The skill: layer to see through; notice what changes when papers overlap. Ask: “What do you see through the top piece?”

**COLLAGE-05 Cut Strip Mosaic** — band both; 20–35 min.  
Materials: paper strips (adult may pre-strip for 5–7; child cuts strips at 8–10 with children’s scissors); glue; background.  
Mess / safety: medium; labelled scissors.  
The fun: filling a big drawn shape with strips like a puzzle.  
The skill: pattern talk *after* making. Rhythm of repeats without requiring perfection.

**COLLAGE-06 Found-Paper Portrait Soft** — band 8–10; 25–40 min.  
Materials: scraps; glue; optional pencil guide the *child* draws.  
Mess / safety: medium.  
The fun: creature or face from unexpected scraps — button eyes only if choke-safe and you agree.  
The skill: portrait or creature from scraps — not a photocopy of an adult sample. Child’s guide lines, child’s glue.

**SHAPE support (as needed).** SHAPE-02 cut a shape family, SHAPE-04 positive/negative tear, SHAPE-05 shape city soft can visit this chapter when shape play helps collage — sort and build before geometric names. They support; they do not replace tear-first or press–lift–look.

### Print try-its (in-home)

**PRINT-01 Hand/Found Stamp Soft** — band 5–7; 20–30 min.  
Materials: washable stamp pad or tempera on sponge; paper; foam scrap or cookie cutter; potato optional.  
Mess / safety: medium–high; washable ink only; **adult cuts potato if used**; wash hands; cover table.  
The fun: press–lift–look; pattern from repeat.  
The skill: child’s presses. One look after first press before filling the page.

**PRINT-02 Foam Plate Line Print** — band 8–10; 25–40 min.  
Materials: foam plate or foam sheet; dull pencil; washable paint; paper; spoon or clean hand for press.  
Mess / safety: medium; **no craft knives**; adult present.  
The fun: lines you drew in foam appear reversed or softened on paper.  
The skill: incise lines; ink lightly; pull print; compare plate to print. Talk what the press changed.

**PRINT-03 Leaf Underside Print** — band both; 20–30 min.  
Materials: fallen leaves from a safe area; washable paint; paper.  
Mess / safety: medium; wash hands; **non-toxic plants only**; outdoor collect with adult.  
The fun: veins appear like a secret map.  
The skill: paint leaf underside; press; look at vein marks before naming *print*. Nature line/texture outing feeds this card.

**PRINT-04 Mono Pull Surprise** — band both; 20–30 min.  
Materials: tray or plastic lid; paint smear; paper.  
Mess / safety: medium–high; cover; smocks; small amounts.  
The fun: one pull; accident marks welcome.  
The skill: talk accident marks; second pull optional. Keep a surprise blob — what could it become?

**PRINT-05 Repeat Stamp Border** — band both; 15–25 min.  
Materials: one stamp shape; paper strip or page edge.  
Mess / safety: low–medium.  
The fun: a marching border of repeats.  
The skill: notice rhythm after making. Not “make them identical.” Notice same-same with tiny differences.

### Talk box

**Opening (locked):** “What did the paper or the press do that you can point at?”

**Follow-ups:**

1. Look: “What do you *notice* on that torn edge / in that stamp?”
2. Make: “What did your hands (or the press) do?”

### Blocked / mixed / incorrect example

**Blocked.** Craft knives. Solvent adhesives. Adult cuts every shape “for niceness.” Identical template as the only product. Generator collage filed as the child’s work.

**Mixed — when to use which.** Tear-first when polish anxiety is high. Texture tray when hands need a feel break from scissors. Overlap peek when color mix ideas should transfer to paper. Foam plate when 8–10 are ready for compare-plate-to-print. Leaf print when you need an OUT-linked make. Mono pull when accident-keeping needs practice. Repeat border when rhythm talk is the crumb.

**Incorrect example (illustration).** Parent downloads a “cute fox collage,” prints templates, pre-cuts all pieces, and has the child glue tabs A–F. Three siblings’ foxes match. Diagnosis: sample-only hour; adult-filled cuts; arrange-before-stick never happened. Repair: scrap basket, tear session, locked opening about *their* edges. Template fox can be looked at, then put away.

**Incorrect print pattern.** Parent uses a craft knife to carve a detailed stamp “because foam looks babyish,” then hands the stamp to the child for one press. Diagnosis: wrong tool; adult-made plate as fake child authorship if filed as theirs without honesty. ### Practice notes that keep the hour honest

### Arrange minute — teach it as a ritual

Set a visible timer for sixty seconds (or count slowly together) during which glue stays off the table. The child may move pieces freely. When the minute ends, ask once: “Ready to stick, or one more move?” This is not rigid theater. It is a teachable pause that blocks glue-as-race. If sixty seconds is too long at five, try thirty and grow.

### Demo paper for scissors and stamps

Anything you must show — how to open scissors, how to ink a foam scrap, how hard to press a leaf — happens on paper or scrap labelled DEMO. Then the tool returns. If you ink their leaf “to help,” you stole a press. Ink a demo leaf; let them ink theirs.

### Mixing collage and print in one week

You may collage Monday and print Thursday without “finishing” a unit. The shared skill is noticing what the material did. A torn-paper sun and a stamped border can live in the same sketchbook. Caption crumb: “torn” / “pressed” / “layered.” Adult scribes at 5–7.

### What stuck silence sounds like in paper hour

- Shrug after “What is it?” — bad question; switch to locked opening.
- “Can you make it nice?” — polish fear; answer with tear-first and process praise.
- Waiting with hands in lap while you cut — adult-takeover training; put scissors down; invite tear.
- Pointing at a phone collage as theirs — authorship honesty; separate sample from make.

### Positive/negative and shape city as optional bridges

At 8–10, positive/negative tear (SHAPE-04): tear a shape from dark paper, keep both the shape and the hole, glue both on light ground. Notice both. Shape city (SHAPE-05): build a place from paper shapes — child’s architecture, not a tracing of a map. Use these when collage energy wants structure; return to free tear when structure becomes sample-matching.

### Ink and glue amounts

Small beads of glue beat puddles. Thin ink beats soaked foam. More material is not more learning. Mess honesty means naming wetness, not celebrating floods. Wash time on the clock prevents the hour from ending in a fight at the sink.

### Courtesy on collects

Leaf and texture walks: take fallen leaves; leave living plants unless you own them and know they are non-toxic. Grocery and library trips for looking do not require purchases. Staff courtesy is part of out-of-home try-its. Looking in public is practice for museum sits later.

### Connecting back to color week

Tissue overlap is color meeting in paper. A stamp pad’s blue next to yellow scraps can echo two-puddle noticing without opening tempera. Ask: “What happened when these colors met on your page — before we name the mix?” Same locked spirit; different materials. Do not force the color chapter’s opening word-for-word if today’s locked opening fits better — use ch05’s opening for collage/print hours.

### Incorrect rescue to refuse

Parent completes the mosaic after bedtime so morning fridge looks finished. That is adult mark filed as child work. Better: unfinished page with a sticky note “more strips tomorrow.” Process over polish. The fridge is not a grade.

### When siblings share the table

Separate scrap trays. Separate presses. Whose-mark talk: “Point at your torn edges.” Older child may demo on demo paper once — then younger works. Do not let the older child’s sample become the required product for the younger.

## For the student

Paper can be torn, cut, layered, pressed, and pulled. The important part is what *you* do with it — and what you notice when the paper or the press answers back.

**Tiny example.** You tear a blue shape. The edge looks soft and fuzzy. You place it on white paper, move it, then glue. That fuzzy edge is something you can point at. Or: you paint a leaf’s underside, press it down, lift — veins appear. You can point at the veins. That pointing is looking.

**Try 1 — Arrange before stick.** Tear at least five shapes. Move them around for one whole minute before any glue. Then stick. Tell what the paper did that you can point at.

**Try 2 — Press, lift, look.** Use a stamp, a leaf, or a paint smear on a tray. Press paper. Lift. Look before you press again. What surprised you?

**Explain it back.** What should happen first in collage — glue everything fast, or arrange and look? What are the three words for print practice: press, lift, …?

**Challenge.** Keep one “wrong” torn edge or one surprise blot on purpose. Tomorrow, tell what it could become. Optional: find a leaf on a walk (with a grown-up) and print its underside at home.

Your page does not have to match a sample fox. A computer picture of a collage is not your collage. Your torn edges and your presses count.

**More tries if you want.** Layer two tissue pieces and peek through. Fill a big circle with paper strips. Draw a simple face guide yourself, then build it from scraps — your guide, your glue. For print: make a border of repeats and notice which stamp landed heavier. Keep a mono-pull surprise as tomorrow’s “what could it become?” game.

**Explain more.** Why might a grown-up want to cut your shapes for you? What will you say if that happens? Practice: “I want to tear mine.” You are allowed to say that.


**Tiny worked story.** Maya tears green paper into bumpy hills. She places a yellow tear for sun, moves it left, then right, then glues. Her grown-up asks what the paper did. Maya points: “The hill edge is rough. I like rough.” That is a complete Art Hour. No owl template required.

Sam paints a leaf, presses, lifts. The first pull is faint. He inks more and presses again. Second pull shows veins. He points: “The press made the lines show.” That is print looking. Crooked leaf stamp still counts.

## If it isn’t clicking

**Diagnostic 1 — Adult takeover.**  
You cut everything; child only glues; or you re-press until the stamp is perfect.  
Next move: hands off cutting for one full Hour — tear only. For print: one demo press on demo paper, then their presses only. Say: “Your edges. Your presses.” If you already cut a stack, put the stack on demo corner and start a fresh tear pile for them.

**Diagnostic 2 — Identical outputs / sample-only.**  
Every page matches the printout; pride is only in sameness.  
Next move: hide the template after a one-minute look. Scrap basket. Locked opening about differences they can point at. Praise a decision (“you moved the sun three times”) more than likeness to sample.

**Diagnostic 3 — Knife temptation or solvent glue.**  
Someone wants craft-knife carving or garage adhesive “for better craft.”  
Next move: refuse for this age book. Foam + dull pencil; washable ink; AP glue stick. Say plainly: knives and solvent glues are not tools for this Hour. Later sculpture and print depth belong in older work and other manuals — not here.

**Extra — Scissors freeze or fight.** Return to **SAFE-03**: snip strips first; open/close practice; tear-first days. Skill not birthday. Adult present.

**When to slow down.** Stay on tear-and-arrange until the child moves pieces before gluing without being told every time.

**When to go ahead.** Add scissors strips, overlap tissue, foam plate compare, found-paper creature.

**When to get a human tutor.** Persistent scissors pain or sensory distress with glue after small amounts and stick preference — ask OT or trusted teacher for adaptive scissors or glue alternatives while keeping arrange-before-stick. No shame.

Correct without crushing: “Crooked isn’t broken — it’s your press. Want to try a second press and compare?”

**Diagnostic 4 — Glue-as-race.** Child dumps glue everywhere to finish fast; looking never happens.  
Next move: glue stick only; arrange minute with glue in your pocket; then glue appears. Say: “Glue is the last step today.”

**Diagnostic 5 — Generated sample as ‘my art’.** Phone shows a polished collage; child shrugs at their tears.  
Next move: name the phone image generated or photographed sample. Fridge and folder keep *their* page. Looking at samples is fine; filing samples as their mark is not.

## Tools, including AI

No craft knives. No solvent adhesives. Seal-check new inks and glues (**SAFE-01** grain).

Parent holds the account. 5–7: no live open chat as partner. 8–10: parent in the room. Unaided first on arrange and press. Point back to the Art Hour AI box.

Library free look first. Museum postcards of collage can inspire after a make — caption covered first if you practice looking moves from earlier chapters.

**AI reminder in one line.** Tool may prep *you*; it may not arrange or press for the child, and it may not become the page in the folder.

# Chapter 6 — Clay and 3-D soft

![Pinch pots on a wooden table — still-life, no people, no logos.](book/art/ch06-opener.jpg){width=100%}

## Why this matters

Clay tells the truth with gravity. A pinch pot’s wall thins. A coil softens. A tall idea leans and falls. That fall is not failure — it is the material talking. This chapter teaches redesign when soft 3-D fails, with the child’s hands still on the clay.

Look before name: notice what the clay did before anyone says *pinch pot* or *coil*. Make before polish: adult hands do not re-smooth a “nice pot” before the child has struggled, tried, and chosen a next move. Soft clay only — air-dry or playdough with an **AP** seal as the default. No kiln required for this age book. No craft knives as armature default. Toothpicks or skewers, if used, stay adult-supervised and tip-only after struggle.

Worth the struggle: a child who redesigns a fallen form learns stretch-and-explore without a speech about grit. An eight- to ten-year-old who builds something that stands, watches it lean, and tries a wider base is doing real sculpture thinking at kitchen scale. A parent who asks “what did the clay do — and what will you try next if it falls?” is teaching process over fridge-ready polish.

Soft spatial play supports this chapter without becoming a formal perspective course. Big and small on one page, overlap of two objects, where things “sit” on a baseline, near and far from a window — these SPACE tries help eyes that will later draw what clay showed the hands. Clay then draw closes a look–make–look loop: form first, then a drawing from one angle.

This is not a ceramics credit. *Art for Homeschooling* can own kiln and harder sculpture later. Here the Hour owns pinch, coil, stamp texture, stand-up redesign, and soft space. Place by skill, not birthday. Short sits beat long melt-downs. Damp wipe for clay dust. Adult present.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

**Everyday picture.** The child’s pinch pot sags. You take it, remake a smooth bowl in ten seconds, and say, “See? Like this.” The child nods. Tomorrow they wait for you to finish every form. Collapse became shame. Your polish became the standard.

**Precise picture.** Same sag. You wait. You ask, “What did the clay do — and what will you try next if it falls?” The child says, “It got thin here.” You: “What could you try?” They thicken the wall — or mash and start a coil — or smile and stamp texture on the pancake that remains. You offer a tip only after struggle, on a demo lump if needed, then return their clay. Their walls stay theirs.

Fernando-Smith’s grain (process and product can both have value, with process default in early years) fits here: a standing coil is lovely; a redesigned fall is the teaching. Do not shame collapsed walls. Do not kiln-shame playdough. Soft is the point.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. **“I’ll just fix it so it looks like a real pot.”** Adult re-smooth before struggle. Reply: “Your walls. What will you try next?” Tip after struggle; demo lump, not takeover.
2. **“We can’t do clay without a kiln.”** Kiln as false gate. Reply: air-dry or playdough AP default. Dry on a board. Paint later if you want — washable. No kiln needed for this book.
3. **“It fell — you ruined it.”** Shame for collapse. Reply: “The clay told us something. What’s next?” Redesign is the skill.
4. **“Let’s cut supports with a craft knife.”** Wrong default tool. Reply: thicker base, shorter height, coil reinforcement; adult-only tips for toothpick after struggle if needed. No craft-knife culture in this Hour.
5. **“Just copy my sample dinosaur so it stands.”** Sample-matched 3-D. Reply: child’s form first; stand-up challenge is their engineering, not your sculpture demo filed as theirs.

**Five-minute parent warm-up.** Alone: roll a ball of air-dry or playdough. Pinch a pot until a wall thins. Let it sag on purpose. Ask yourself the locked opening. Redesign once — wider base or thicker wall. Do not aim for gallery finish. Aim for felt redesign. Wipe the board. You are practicing leave-the-walls-alone and try-next-if-it-falls.

**Precise picture — clay then draw.** Child makes a lumpy animal. From one angle, they draw what they see — not what they invent from memory alone. Looking returns after making. That loop is gold. Put the form on the table; eyes on form; pencil on paper. Short sit.

## How to teach it this week

**Session shape (Art Hour, ~20–40 min).**

1. **Look / notice (3–8 min).** Soften clay together. Notice warm/cool of the lump, cracks, fingerprints. Or look at a mug’s volume before pinching. Or look at two objects overlapping for SPACE soft.
2. **Short make (8–18 min).** Pinch from a ball; or roll coils; or stamp textures; or stand-up challenge. Child’s hands. Demo on demo lump only. If it falls, redesign — do not snatch.
3. **Talk box (3–8 min).** Locked opening. Wait. Follow-ups on what clay did and what’s next.
4. **Name lightly (2–5 min, optional).** *Pinch*, *coil*, *base*, *texture* — after trying. Can wait.
5. **Exit crumb (2–5 min).** Keep or mash with intention; photo of process optional; clay-then-draw crumb; oral “next time I’ll…”

**Exact sentences you can say**

- “Pinch from the ball. Your walls.”
- “What did the clay do — and what will you try next if it falls?”
- “Thin walls tip. What could thicken?”
- “I’ll show a wider base on my demo lump. Then your clay again.”
- “Fallen is information. Mash and redesign, or repair — your choice.”
- “Stamp the slab. What do you see in the print?”
- “Draw what you see from this angle — the clay is still the teacher.”

**First try-it: CLAY-01 Pinch Pot Soft** (band both).

- **Time:** 20–35 min.
- **Materials:** Air-dry clay or playdough (AP); wipe cloth; board or covered table; water drop optional for air-dry.
- **Mess / safety:** Medium; clay dust — damp wipe; no kiln needed; wash hands; adult present; small parts if adding beads — choke check.
- **The fun:** A hollow appears because *their* fingers pinched.
- **The skill:** Pinch from ball; child’s walls. Adult does not re-smooth into “nice pot” unless child asks for a tip after struggle — then demo lump, return tool (hands).

**How to fade help.** Sitting 1: show open-pinch once on demo lump; their ball. Sitting 2: they pinch without demo. Sitting 3: when it sags, you only ask the locked opening. Sitting 4: stand-up challenge or clay-then-draw. Fade praise from “pretty pot” to “you thickened after it leaned.”

**When to stop talking.** After the opening. While fingers work. When they are mid-redesign. Do not narrate worry (“it’s going to fall!”) into the clay. Ask, wait, hint.

**Age-band moves**

- **5–7:** Playdough or very soft air-dry; short sits (match attention); pinch pots; stamp texture with leaf/fork/LEGO; oral redesign talk; mash-and-restart allowed without shame; SPACE-01 big/small same page as dry cousin day; adult may scribe “it fell / I made wider.” Damp wipe. No kiln talk as pressure.
- **8–10:** Firmer air-dry ok; coil snake build; stand-up challenge with redesign; clay then draw; SPACE-02 overlap, SPACE-04 near/far soft as supporting drawing days; optional toothpick/skewer **adult-only** after struggle for a temporary support experiment — not craft-knife armatures. Still refuse adult re-smooth as default. If pinch walls never get a chance before adult fix, stay on 5–7 clay longer even at age nine.

**This-week plan.** Focus: pinch + redesign reflex. Three to five Hours. One SPACE soft day if wet clay is too much. One OUT cousin: sculpture garden walk soft or nature texture collect for stamping. Friday: done-enough — child’s walls/coils; at least one redesign.

**Safety.** AP air-dry or playdough. Damp wipe dust. Wash hands. No kiln default. No craft knives. Adult present for small tools. Food off table. Choke check on add-ons. Read labels. “Non-toxic” ≠ edible.

### Talk box

4. Process: “How did you change it after it fell?” / “Whose hands made these walls?”

### Extended teaching notes for soft 3-D week

**Why gravity is a teacher.** Paint can hide a weak mix under another swirl. Paper can be glued flat. Clay announces imbalance. That announcement is kind if you let it be information. Parents who rush to prevent every lean steal the announcement. Parents who narrate doom (“it’s going to fall!”) add fear. Parents who ask the locked opening turn gravity into curriculum.

**Pinch mechanics without a lecture.** Ball in nondominant hand (or both). Thumbs open a mouth in the center. Fingers support the outside. Rotate a little; pinch a little. Stop and look. Thin rim? Add a coil snake as a lip — child’s choice. You may show rotation on a demo lump once. Then silence.

**Coil mechanics without a lecture.** Roll from the middle outward. Soft coil = thicker, shorter stacks. Firm coil = stronger walls if not cracked. Score-and-slip is studio language you can skip; at this age, gentle pressing together and a dab of water on air-dry (per label) is enough. If coils spring apart, thicker press, shorter tower.

**Stand-up challenge framing.** “Make something that stands for ten slow counts.” If it falls at six, celebrate the data. Redesign. Try again. Ten counts is a game, not a test score. Widening the feet beats secretly inserting a hidden adult wire before the child has tried.

**Clay-then-draw framing.** Place the form at eye level. Child sits in one spot. “Draw the side you see — even if you know the other side has a tail.” Knowing vs seeing is the looking lesson. Blind contour soft from Chapter 3 can visit for one minute if they rush symbols instead of edges.

**Playdough vs air-dry honesty.** Playdough: reusable, great for redesign reps, may not keep a fridge trophy. Air-dry: keeps a form, can crack, needs shelf space. Both are legitimate Art materials in this book. Choosing playdough is not “less than.” Choosing air-dry is not “more artistic.” Process is the effect on the child’s looking and trying.

**Sample dialogue after a fall (illustration).**  
Parent: “What did the clay do — and what will you try next if it falls?”  
Child: “It tipped. The head was heavy.”  
Parent: “What could you try?”  
Child: “Smaller head. Or fatter feet.”  
Parent: “Your choice. I’ll watch.”  
*(Child fattens feet. It stands longer.)*  
Parent: “What do you see that makes you say it’s steadier?”  
Child: “The bottom is wider than the top now.”

**When siblings compare.** “Whose fingerprints?” beats “whose is prettier?” If one child copies another’s dinosaur, allow homage then ask for one change that is theirs — taller legs, stamped skin, different base.

**Outdoor clay?** Soft clay outdoors needs a tray and wind plan; often indoor is kinder. Sculpture walks feed eyes; hands work at home.

**Link to records.** Optional process photo of the lean and the redesign side by side in the dated folder. Label Art. Child’s forms. Not a percentile. Not NAEP.

**Link to assessment later.** Whose-mark and done-enough from Chapter 8 will ask for child’s walls across weeks. Start collecting those walls now. Adult demos stay labelled.

**Emotional weather.** Some children smash when frustrated. Offer: “Intentional mash is allowed. Angry smash at people is not.” Redirect energy into stamp slab. Soften clay. Shorten sit. You are teaching regulation beside redesign — calmly, without shame.

**What you are not teaching this week.** Wheel throwing. Glaze chemistry. Kiln schedules. Lost-wax casting. Craft-knife foam carving as sculpture credit. Formal two-point perspective. Those can wait for older years and other books. Soft 3-D and soft space are enough — and rich.

**Vendor pages and sample kits.** If a kit includes a step sheet that ends in one identical product, use the clay — skip forcing the sample match. Look at the sample once; put it away; child’s form. Fit, not rank, belongs in resources later; authorship belongs at the table now.

**Five-minute emergency Hour.** Only ten minutes free? Soften clay, pinch once, ask locked opening, wipe, done. Micro-doses count when they keep the redesign question alive.

## For the student

Clay is soft on purpose. It bends. It leans. Sometimes it falls. When it falls, you get to try something next — thicker, shorter, wider, or a whole new idea. That next try is part of art.

**Tiny example.** You pinch a ball. The side gets thin. It leans. You push more clay into the thin place — or squash it and roll a coil snake for a new bowl. You did not “ruin” it. The clay told you the wall was thin. You answered.

**Try 1 — Pinch and notice.** Make a pinch pot. Stop once and look at the walls. Point to a thick place and a thin place. Tell what the clay might do next.

**Try 2 — If it falls.** Build something that stands (even a tiny person). If it falls, say the opening question to yourself: what did the clay do, and what will you try next? Do that next thing. Optional: draw your form from one side when you like it.

**Explain it back.** Why might a grown-up want to fix your pot for you? What can you say? Practice: “I want to try next.” Whose walls should be in your Art folder?

**Challenge.** Keep a “failed” tall coil as a pancake and stamp textures into it. Turn the fall into a new piece. Or: big and small versions of the same animal on paper after you make one in clay.

You are allowed to mash and restart. You are allowed to stop while you still want one more pinch. A computer picture of a perfect statue is not your clay. Your fingerprints in the wall count.

**More student tries.** Roll three coils — skinny, medium, fat — and stack them. Which stack stands longer? Stamp a leaf into a slab and look before you name it. Put your finished pinch on the table and draw only what you see from your chair — even if the far side is “missing” on the page.



**If you feel mad when it falls.** Mad is allowed. Hitting people is not. You can mash the clay on purpose and start again. You can take a breath and fatten the feet. You can ask for a tip after you have tried — the tip happens on a demo lump, then your clay comes back to you.

**Explain one more time.** What is the first job — make it look like the sample, or notice what the clay did? What question should a grown-up ask when your tower leans?

## If it isn’t clicking

**Diagnostic 1 — Adult polish reflex.**  
You remake their pot “nice” before they try again.  
Next move: pockets. Locked opening. Demo lump only if they ask after struggle. Fresh ball for them. Label any adult form DEMO. Album honesty: child’s walls.

**Diagnostic 2 — Long sit meltdown.**  
Tears, smash-anger, or shutdown mid-coil.  
Next move: shorten the make. Playdough softer. Stamp texture day. Or SPACE dry day. Mess/dust preview. Damp wipe. End with talk box even if the form is mashed on purpose — intentional mash is still a choice.

**Diagnostic 3 — Dust, dry air-dry frustration, or kiln myth.**  
Cracking, dusty table, or “this isn’t real without firing.”  
Next move: damp wipe; tiny water for air-dry as label allows; softer clay; name playdough as real for this book. Kiln is not the gate. Process is.

**Extra — Shame after fall.** Reframe: “Fallen told us the base was small. That’s useful.” Then redesign. Never “you ruined it.”

**Extra — Generated 3-D as theirs.** Separate: looking at sculpture photos is fine; filing a render as their clay is not.

**When to slow down.** Pinch + stamp + oral redesign until falls are interesting, not catastrophic.

**When to go ahead.** Coils, stand-up, clay-then-draw, overlap/near-far soft.

**When to get a human tutor.** Pain in hands, extreme sensory refusal after soft options, or motor concerns — OT/teacher for tool ideas while keeping child’s authorship. No shame. Looking can continue with SPACE soft and sculpture walks.

Correct without crushing: “Thin isn’t naughty — it’s a clue. What’s your next try?”



### Materials ritual for clay week

Before the first open tub:

1. Check AP / ASTM D-4236 on the package (SAFE-01 grain).
2. Cover or board ready; damp cloth ready; food off table.
3. Mess honesty: “Clay will stick to fingers. We wipe at the end.”
4. Agree where drying pieces live if air-dry (high shelf, labelled).
5. Agree that falls are information — say it out loud once so shame has less room.

These steps take three minutes and save thirty minutes of cleanup fight and polish panic.

### Redesign menu children can choose from

When something leans or falls, offer choices — not commands:

- Thicken the wall or foot.
- Shorten the tall part.
- Add a coil buttress.
- Separate into two smaller standing forms.
- Mash flat and stamp textures (new piece from old attempt).
- Clay-then-draw the fallen view, then rebuild tomorrow.

Choice keeps authorship. Command (“do it my way”) returns adult polish through the side door.

### SPACE soft — one clear week slot

If wet clay every day is too much sensory load, schedule one SPACE soft Hour midweek:

- Monday pinch, Tuesday coil, Wednesday big/small or baseline walk, Thursday stamp or stand-up, Friday clay-then-draw.

Wednesday’s dry looking still belongs to this chapter because size, sit, overlap, and near/far support the same eyes that judge a clay base. You are not abandoning 3-D; you are letting eyes rest while skills transfer.

### What “done enough” is not

Done enough is not a kiln-fired set. Not a store-matched dinosaur. Not a generated render. Not a parent-smoothed bowl with the child’s name taped on. Not a percentile. Not a birthday promotion to “real sculpture.” Done enough is child’s walls, one redesign, soft materials, talk that points at clay.

### Parent fatigue line

If you are tired, soft clay still works in ten minutes. You do not need to perform enthusiasm. You need to ask one real question and wait. The clay supplies the drama. You supply the pause.

# Chapter 7 — Talking and writing about art

![A sticky-note caption on a child’s drawing beside a dated sketchbook margin — still-life of page and note, no people, no logos.](book/art/ch07-opener.jpg){width=100%}

## Why this matters

A child can look at a mug, mix two puddles, tear a collage, and still leave the hour with only your praise in their ear. “Pretty.” “Nice job.” “I love it.” The page sits finished. Tomorrow the talk is thinner. The noticing never became theirs.

Talking about art at 5–10 is one authentic question, a wait, and a finger on the work. Writing about art at 5–10 is a ladder: three words, then a because-I-see caption, then a process sentence, then a title chosen after looking — not assigned before the first mark. It is not an MFA critique. It is not a vocabulary quiz. It is not a blank artist-statement page meant to shame a six-year-old who still needs you to scribe.

This is worth the struggle because talk is how looking becomes shared language, and a caption is how looking lasts past the fridge. A fluent summary from a grown-up, or from a machine, is performance. A sentence that could only come from *this* yellow drip or *this* torn edge is art talk.

Preference is allowed. Joy is allowed. “Pretty” alone is not the craft. Preference reports the speaker. Description reports the work. When the only sentence at the table is preference, the child learns that art talk is a mood check, not a look.

Classroom wait-time research started in elementary *science* lessons. Teachers often waited about a second after a question. When they waited longer — a few seconds — the talk changed. A later digest called the pause *think-time*. Use the pause at your table as craft, not as a kitchen experiment you are failing if Wednesday is noisy. This is a useful study shape, not a promise that every home will see the same result.

Materials alone rarely make discussion. A finished collage is not yet a conversation. You still pose, wait, and point. The child answers you, not a tab.

The locked question for this chapter is the whole craft in one line:

“Tell me about your artwork — not whether it is pretty.”

If they can answer that, with a finger or a caption, the week did its job.

A parent who felt rusty on art often defaults to the praise they received as a child: product compliments. That default is kind. It is incomplete. Specific noticing teaches the child what looking *is*. Process questions teach them that the hand’s try matters. Caption crumbs teach them that their words belong under their marks. None of that requires you to be a studio artist. It requires you to put the page on the table, ask one good question, and wait.

Chapters 1–6 put lookables on the table: notice before name, look at objects and pictures, child’s mark and line, color mix light, collage and print, soft clay. This chapter teaches the mouth and the pencil to stay honest to those looks. Sister English manuals care about oral before heavy print; borrow the kindness, not the phonics scope. Sister thinking manuals care about wait and change-your-mind; borrow the craft, not a playground debate without a page on the table. Then teach art talk. One authentic question beats ten quiz words. One caption that needs the page beats a fluent statement that never looked.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

Here is the everyday picture.

The painting is wet. You say, “That’s so pretty!” The child smiles. You ask, “What is it?” They shrug or invent a title to please you. You fill the silence with more praise. On Friday you ask them to tell you about the work. They say “pretty” back at you. The yellow dots they made never got a sentence.

Here is the precise picture.

The make is done. The page is on the table. You ask them to tell you about the artwork — not whether it is pretty. You wait a slow three. They point at a thick blue edge. You uptake: “You said the blue line is thick where the brush slowed.” For a five-year-old you scribe three words. For an eight-year-old they write one because-I-see caption. The praise can fade. The notice stays.

An authentic question is one you do not already have the exact sentence for. “What is the element of art?” with the vocabulary card facing you is recitation. “Tell me about your artwork — not whether it is pretty” is closer to authentic. You may still know more than they do. You have not prespecified their sentence.

Oral comes before heavy print. Spelling help is allowed. You may scribe. Art looking does not wait for fluent writing. Place by skill, not birthday.

“How did you do that?” turns the child into the teacher for one passage. “Look at all the yellow dots you made” is description as care — the yellow-dots grain from early-childhood process talk. “How do you feel about it?” before your gush lets them own the first report. At 8–10, a soft “already good — how might it be even better?” can invite redesign without shame. None of these is a gallery opening speech.

Studio Habits of Mind name Observe and Reflect among eight habits drawn from high-school studio research. Borrow the light language. Do not claim a kitchen RCT for kindergartners. Visual Thinking Strategies and See–Think–Wonder put observation and evidence before adult interpretation dumps — useful pause craft, not a franchise you must buy.

### Wrong answers you should be able to hear

**1. Empty pretty loop.**  
“Pretty… beautiful… I love it…” and nothing that points.  
*What it usually means:* preference became the only talk.  
*What you say:* point with words. “I notice the thick blue where the brush slowed.” Then the locked opening.

**2. Rapid-fire quiz.**  
“What color is that? What shape? What is the vocabulary word?” No wait. No finger on the page.  
*What it usually means:* talk time became a test.  
*What you say:* put the quiz card away. Ask the locked opening. Count three.

**3. Overwriting the child’s story.**  
They start a caption; you erase and write the “right” artist title or a textbook aim instead.  
*What it usually means:* adult voice replaced child looking.  
*What you say:* stop. Keep their words. Add a second line for your note if you must — labelled as yours.

**4. Title assigned before making.**  
“Today we will make a Happy Tree.” The title lands before the first mark.  
*What it usually means:* name-first colonized the make.  
*What you say:* look and make first. Title after the talk. WRITE-04.

**5. Generated artist statement as the only draft.**  
A fluent machine paragraph sits under a blank or thin page as “what I meant.”  
*What it usually means:* performance replaced looking.  
*What you say:* refuse it. Real two-minute look. Child’s words. Generated statement is not WRITE bank. Point back to the Art Hour AI box.

## How to teach it this week

The Art Hour shape stays. Talk and write are the talk box and the crumb, not a second subject named Little Thinkers Art Writing.

**Session shape (~20–40 minutes)**

1. **Look / notice (3–8 min).** Yesterday’s page, a postcard with caption covered, or today’s object — mug, leaf, window.
2. **Short make (8–18 min).** Child holds the tool. Demo on demo paper only if needed.
3. **Talk box (3–8 min).** Locked opening. Wait. Point with words. One follow-up.
4. **Name lightly (2–5 min).** Optional. A word that fits what was seen — after evidence.
5. **Exit crumb (2–5 min).** Three-word caption, process sentence, or title after look. Adult may scribe at 5–7.

Twenty-minute day: uncover yesterday’s page, locked question, one sticky caption. That is a full hour.

### Exact wording you can say

Opening, locked:

“Tell me about your artwork — not whether it is pretty.”

Then wait.

If they only say pretty: “Pretty is your feeling. Point at one place on the page. What do you *see* there?”

If they freeze: “Point first. Then give the point a word.”

If they shrug at “What is it?”: drop that question. Use the locked opening. “What is it?” often hunts a label you already hold.

If they retell your demo: “Put your finger on *your* mark. What did *your* hand try?”

If siblings talk over each other: “I heard you say ___; the page shows ___. Do you agree — point.”

When you fade help, stop supplying the description. When you stop talking, look at the page.

## Practice that actually builds learning

Named try-its carry time, materials, mess/safety, the fun, and the skill. Talk try-its and write try-its share a table. The library, museum, and fridge gallery are practice. They do not replace the tell-me move.

### Talk box (use every talk/write hour)

**Opening (locked):** “Tell me about your artwork — not whether it is pretty.”

**Follow-ups:**

1. What do you *see* / *notice*?
2. What did your hand / the material do?
3. What do you see that makes you say that?

### Caption coaching details

At 8–10, because-I-see wants a claim that the page can back. “I think the leaf looks bumpy because I see the veins and the torn edge.” Finger required. If they write a claim that only restates a book caption, cover the caption and try again.

Process sentences love clay, collage, and print hours: “First I tore, then I glued the red on top.” “First I pinched, then it fell, then I coiled.” Collapse and redesign belong in the sentence. That is make-before-polish in words.

**What went wrong:** WRITE-05 refuse. Authorship blur. Performance for strangers.

**Rescue:** remove the paragraph. Ask the locked opening. Scribe or write one because-I-see or process sentence from the child’s mouth. Label any adult note as adult. The co-op sees the child’s words or none.

### Mixed-age and co-op notes

In a co-op, talk boxes stay short. One authentic question. Wait. Do not turn the circle into a public critique of a shy five-year-old. Pair share first if the group is large: tell your partner one notice; then two volunteers share out. Adult models yellow-dots once.

At home with a toddler underfoot, talk can be two minutes while the page dries. Dose honesty: a short tell-me counts. You do not need a seminar.

### Connection to look-before-name and make-before-polish

Talk that names vocabulary before anyone has looked is name-first with a microphone. Talk that only praises polish — “so neat,” “so pretty,” “fix the drip” — is polish-before-make with a smile. This chapter’s locked opening protects both locks. Look first. Keep the child’s mark. Then talk. Then write a crumb. Then, lightly, a name that fits.

Studio Structures rename Talking About Art and Showing Art map onto the talk box and display choice — say once, not as weekly franchise posters.


### Parent scripts you can borrow

After a collage hour:

“Tell me about your artwork — not whether it is pretty.”  
*(wait)*  
“What did the paper do that you can point at?”  
“How did you decide what went on top?”  
“You may change your mind about the title tomorrow.”

After a clay hour:

“Tell me about your artwork — not whether it is pretty.”  
*(wait)*  
“What did the clay do — and what will you try next if it falls?”  
“First you ___, then you ___. Did I get that right?”

After a museum sit:

“Tell me about this work — not whether it is pretty.”  
*(wait)*  
“What do you see that makes you say that?”  
“We can leave without naming the artist. Looking was the job.”

These scripts are seeds. Your child’s answers will not match a key. That is the point.

## For the student

Art talk is not a quiz show. It is you saying what you notice on your page.

You may point before you speak. You may change your mind when you look again. Preference is allowed — “I like this part” — and then you still point at what you see. “Pretty” alone is not the whole job.

Writing can be three words. Writing can be “I see ___ because ___.” Writing can be “First I ___, then I ___.” You do not need a long artist statement. You need words that match the look.

### Tiny worked example

You painted thick blue where the brush slowed and left yellow dots near the edge. Someone asks you to tell about the artwork — not whether it is pretty. You say: “The blue is thick here because I slowed the brush. I see yellow dots along the edge.” Your finger is on the dots. That is enough.

### Two tries

1. Cover any adult praise sticky. Look at your page. Say one thing you notice. Then write or dictate three words or a short caption.
2. Say “First I ___, then I ___.” Point at the page when you say what your hand did.

### Explain it back

Teach a stuffed animal or a sibling: “Ask me to tell about my artwork — not whether it is pretty. Wait. Do not answer for me.”

### Challenge

Find a sentence that only says “pretty.” Cross it out gently. Replace it with a sentence that needs your page to make sense.

### One more challenge (optional)

Choose a title *after* you talk — not before you start. Write it on a sticky. You may change the title tomorrow.

### If someone talks for you

If a grown-up or a sibling or a screen answers before you finish, you may say: “I was still looking.” Then point at the page and try your sentence again. Looking first is your job. Waiting is their job.

### Caption ladder at a glance

| Step | What it looks like | Band first home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Three words | Sticky: thick / blue / slow | 5–7 |
| Process sentence | First I ___, then I ___ | both |
| Because-I-see | Claim + because + what I see | 8–10 |
| Title after look | Name chosen after talk | both |
| Refuse generated | Spot FAKE; rewrite from real look | meta / 8–10 |

Climb one rung when the lower rung is easy. Jumping to long artist statements without a finger on the page trains performance.

### Sibling and mixed-age note

A five-year-old and an eight-year-old can share one looking talk about a postcard. The younger child’s sticky holds three words you scribe. The older child’s page holds a because-I-see caption. Both answer the locked question. Neither is Little Thinkers Art I. Both are Art looking with talk.

## If it isn’t clicking

**1. Wait was zero.**  
You ask and answer yourself. They learn to wait for the rescue.  
*Next move:* sit on your hands. Count three out loud once if you must. Look at the page. Practice TALK-01 alone before the child arrives.

**2. Pretty loop only.**  
Every sentence is preference.  
*Next move:* TALK-02 and TALK-03. You model one specific notice. Then locked opening again. Do not outlaw joy — outlaw empty praise as the only talk.

**3. Print load too heavy.**  
Captions become tears; the hour dies.  
*Next move:* oral exit. You scribe. Three words only. Return to writing when the talk is warm. Sister English craft helps: oral before heavy print — without turning this chapter into a phonics scope.

**4. Overwrite habit.**  
You keep “fixing” their caption into textbook language mid-line.  
*Next move:* second line labelled *parent note*. Their line stays. Apologize once if needed. Trust rebuilds.

**5. Generated caption temptation.**  
A tool offers a perfect artist statement.  
*Next move:* refuse. WRITE-05 once. Point back to the Art Hour AI box. The model may prepare *you*; it may not do their looking or their statement when those are the lesson.

When to slow down: if they cannot yet point to the page, stay on TALK-01/02/03 and WRITE-01.  
When to go ahead: if captions point at the work, offer WRITE-02/04 and TALK-05.  
When to get a human tutor: if language delay or anxiety makes every talk sit unsafe — a speech-language or teaching adult can help while you keep art looking gentle. No shame.

Correct a wrong reason without crushing the look. Hearing only “pretty” is information. Ask for the finger. Then teach.

## Tools, including AI

Point back to the Art Hour AI box. Parent holds the account. Ages 5–7: no live open chat as the child’s partner. Ages 8–10: still parent in the room. Unaided first on looking and marking. SCRIPT after the try — questions you will say, not a paragraph for them to copy as their artist statement.

You may ask a tool to explain a named page *to you*, to draft discussion questions you will vet, or to help you design a blank caption sticky with PARENT KEY held by you. You may not write-my-artist-statement as the only draft. You may not generate a “child drawing” and file it as theirs. You may not detector-grade “the blue is thick.” Detectors fail. A child’s caption is not a fraud case.

Optional analog tools: sticky notes for captions, a “talk prompt” card with the locked opening, a sticky to cover postcard captions, sketchbook margin, fridge magnets for display choice.

If you use a voice tool to dictate a caption the child already said, label it as their spoken words you typed. That is scribe help. It is not invent-statement. If the tool invents details they never said — stop. Look again. Write only what they pointed to.

Grids, traces, and projectors still pass through the child’s eye and hand. Generators skip looking and marking. One crutch sentence, labelled OFF-AGE for art hour: a high-school math trial found an unguarded chatbot could make practice look better and leave students worse when the window closed. That paper is not a 5–10 art RCT. The kitchen rule it supports: the model may prepare the adult; it may not do the child’s looking, marking, or artist talk when those are the lesson.

## What “done enough” looks like

Before you move on from this chapter’s focus:

- [ ] Child can answer the locked question with a finger on the page at least once.
- [ ] One caption, three-word sticky, or scribed process sentence exists in *their* words.
- [ ] You waited a slow three at least once without filling the silence with “pretty.”
- [ ] You uptake at least one child phrase accurately (“You said ___”).
- [ ] No generated paragraph is filed as their artist statement.
- [ ] Title, if any, came after looking talk — not before the first mark.
- [ ] One fridge-gallery, library, or museum tell-me happened, or is scheduled.

**Life of the habit (not a ninth chapter).** Revisit next Tuesday: uncover last week’s sticky; ask the locked question again. Mix last week’s mark with a new talk follow-up — yellow-dots on Monday’s collage, how-did-you-do-that on Thursday’s line. Stop while a look remains. A diploma is not in the room. A path through elementary art looking and talking is.

**Placement by skill, not birthday.** A six-year-old with strong oral notices may use 8–10 talk cards while you still scribe. A nine-year-old who freezes at print may stay oral. A “grade 2 art workbook” is a publisher’s scope, not a legal grade, and not a transcript line. The work lives inside **Art**. Never a brand. Never Little Thinkers Art I.


Joy still belongs at the table. Laugh at a funny drip. Celebrate a hard try. Then return to one specific notice. Warm process is the Art Hour voice — notice yellow dots; keep the accidental drip; stop while wanting one more mark. It is not gallery-opening polish and not sample-matched craft cheer.

Done enough is showable talk and a small written crumb — not a critique program, not a percentile, not a culture-war win. The stranger who someday opens the sketchbook should find a child’s sentence that points at a look — not a brand, and not a machine paragraph pretending to be a child’s eyes.

If Friday’s caption still makes sense after a night — they can point to the same yellow dots Monday and say the because-clause again — the talk held. If it does not, next Tuesday is shorter: one page, locked question, scribe only. Revisit is not failure. A short revisit that recovers one notice is worth more than a long lecture that recovers none. Mixing last week’s pinch pot with this week’s process sentence is allowed once each piece works alone.

# Chapter 8 — Short assessments and progress checks

![Three sticky exit notes and a dated folder of pages labelled Art — still-life, no people, no logos.](book/art/ch08-opener.jpg){width=100%}

## Why this matters

A check at ages 5–10 is a skill object, not a score that follows a child around the house.

Can they look at an edge and mark it? Can they name one thing on the page? Can they tell about the work without stopping at “pretty”? Can they say whose mark is whose? Can they choose a page for the folder and give one reason? If Friday’s look still holds after a night, the week taught something. If it does not, you have a next move. That is enough.

This is worth the struggle because the wrong kind of check teaches the wrong kind of art. A vocabulary list trains name-first. A percentile sticker trains identity: “art kid” or not. A public quiz trains performance. An app that awards points for minutes trains sitting, not looking. A generated image filed in a “child work” folder trains dishonesty. A fridge picture treated as the grade trains polish-before-make. None of those is this book’s job.

There is no nationally published homeschool NAEP Arts score for this age band that this book can turn into a kitchen cut score. Some states ask for tests in reading and mathematics, or for a narrative, or for a parent-written list of subjects. Those sittings stay in their lane. They are not this chapter. This chapter does not give legal advice. You read your own statute when you need to. The stranger who later opens a folder wants **Art** work they can see — a dated sketchbook, a looking note, the child’s make, an optional process photo — not a brand, and never a course called Little Thinkers Art I.

Screening a skill is not an identity. A child who cannot yet follow a mug’s rim today is not “not an art kid.” They need a shorter sit, a fatter tool, or one more night. You already know how to reteach a shoe pair. Use that kindness here. Lowenfeld stages are a map of typical graphic behaviors, not a giftedness or delay verdict. Do not diagnose from a fridge picture.

Kitchen skill checks are load-bearing in this book, not garnish. That is why this chapter is dedicated. Exit crumbs still live in every teaching chapter. This chapter gathers the objects, the diagnostic wrongs, the progress charts without percentiles, and the done-enough language in one place.

The locked question for this chapter is how a check should feel:

“What can you show me two ways today — and what still needs practice?”

Two ways means the look or the make is theirs, not a lucky nod. What still needs practice means the next hour already has a job. A diploma is not in the room. A path through elementary looking and making is — and that path is enough for this week.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

Here is the everyday picture.

Friday night becomes a quiz. Ten vocabulary words. Five “name the element” cards. A parent and a child both get tight. The child guesses. The parent hears “not ready.” The folder gets a sad face. Next week the same list, louder. Or the opposite everyday picture: every page on the fridge is graded by how much it looks like the sample. The child’s mark disappears under adult polish. Or a phone JPEG of “kids crayon art” sits in the folder labelled as theirs.

Here is the precise picture.

Friday is short. Look at an edge and mark it from an object already on the table this week. Tell about the work — oral first. Name one notice and one make move. Say whose hand held the tool. If they can show a skill two ways — mark *and* say; sort warm/cool *and* point; choose a page *and* give a reason — you date the sketchbook. If a miss has a reason you can use tomorrow — name-first, adult-filled marks, coloring-book-only week, generated image — you keep the reason. You do not keep a label.

Done enough is local. A five-year-old who looks at a leaf, makes a mark, and says three words about the page is done enough for that check. An eight-year-old who follows a mug rim, writes a because-I-see caption, and says whose mark is whose is done enough for that check. Holding both children to the same MFA rubric is a different product.

When a program you already use includes chapter review pages, you may sit them as *listening and recall* inside that scope. They check whether a lesson was heard. They do not license a national rank. They do not replace the kitchen objects below. Label them as the program’s tools, not as a research trial of your kitchen.

Depth beats a coverage tour on checks as well as on lessons. A long vocabulary list leaves few traces. A small pattern — look, make with own mark, talk once, leave a crumb — built slowly, is what the mind will keep. This is a useful study shape borrowed carefully, not a promise that every home will see the same result.

No CLEP for elementary. No AP Art and Design for a nine-year-old. No NCAA for art at any age in the elementary file. When the child is older and the family needs portfolio depth, open *Art for Homeschooling*. Then return here for the kitchen objects.

### Wrong answers you should be able to hear

**1. Fridge-as-grade.**  
“This one is an A because it looks like the sample.”  
*What it usually means:* product polish replaced process and authorship.  
*What you say:* “The fridge is a display choice. The check is look, make, talk, crumb.”

**2. Percentile identity / “not an art kid.”**  
“This miss means you are behind.” Or: “You’re just not an art kid.”  
*What it usually means:* screening turned into identity — sometimes with a Lowenfeld chart misused as diagnosis.  
*What you say:* “This is a skill we can practice. It is not a name for you.”

**3. Birthday lock.**  
“You’re 8, so you must do blind contour.” Or: “You’re 5, so you cannot do real art yet.”  
*What it usually means:* birthday placement replaced skill placement.  
*What you say:* “We place by what you can show. Looking first if looking is shaky.”

**4. Generated image in the child-work folder.**  
A phone JPEG or “kids crayon style” print sits labelled as Drawing.  
*What it usually means:* authorship blur.  
*What you say:* AS-02. “Whose hand held the tool?” Delete from child work. Start MARK-01 with a real crayon.

**5. NAEP promise or homemade high-stakes battery.**  
A kitchen quiz sold as national readiness.  
*What it usually means:* compliance anxiety or sales language landed on the wrong object.  
*What you say:* “This is a skill check for tomorrow’s teaching. It is not a national score.”

## How to teach it this week

A check week is still an Art Hour week. The try *is* the check. You do not add a second, colder hour titled Assessment.

**Session shape (~20–40 minutes)**

1. **Look / notice (3–8 min).** A familiar object or a close cousin — not a surprise stack of new content.
2. **Short make / check object (8–18 min).** One kitchen object below — not a battery.
3. **Talk box (3–8 min).** Locked opening. One “still needs practice” line.
4. **Name lightly (optional, 2–5 min).** After evidence.
5. **Exit crumb (2–5 min).** AS-01 sticky triad or caption. Adult error-pattern note private.

Twenty-minute Friday: one exit crumb trio *or* one whose-mark check *or* one name-first catch. Two ways on that one object. Stop.

### Exact wording you can say

Opening, locked:

“What can you show me two ways today — and what still needs practice?”

Then wait.

If they freeze: “Show me with the page first. Then show me with words. We can do the second way tomorrow.”

If they perform a memorized definition: “I already know the book sentence. Show me from what you see on *this* page.”

If they ask whether they are good at art: “You can look and make a mark. You are still practicing tell-me talk. That is a map, not a name for you.”

When you fade help, do not supply the second way. When you stop talking, the sketchbook gets one line, not a speech.

## Practice that actually builds learning

Checks are named try-its. They still need time, materials, mess/safety, the fun, and the skill. Fun here is the relief of a finishable task. The skill is showing a look or make two ways.

Composite homes are illustrations, not reported families.

### Talk box (use every check hour)

**Opening (locked):** “What can you show me two ways today — and what still needs practice?”

**Follow-ups:**

1. What do you *see* / *notice* on this page or object?
2. What did your hand / the material do?
3. What do you see that makes you say that?
4. Whose mark is this — and how do you know?
5. You may change your mind — what will you practice next Tuesday?

**How to wait:** After you ask, a slow three. After they stop, wait again. Look at the page. Do not fill silence with a grade face.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the check felt like a test; wait was zero; they fear “not an art kid”; every hour was coloring-book lines; a generated image was treated as theirs; they only know “pretty”; birthday pressure (“you’re 9, you should…”). Soften. Shorten. Point at the page. Name tomorrow’s practice kindly.

## The law is a pattern, not one form

U.S. homeschooling is state law. There is no federal art office. Dates and subject lists change. Read your current department page and the statute it cites. A color-coded chart on a membership site is not your statute. ## What “look-then-make” vs “step-by-step” means at the table

Look-then-make programs (Artistic Pursuits early is the clearest vendor example in this list) put observation and original subject choice near the center. Step-by-step programs (Draw Write Now; many How Great Thou Art early exercises; many video models) put a demonstrated sequence near the center. Both can live in a 5–10 home. Neither is crowned. The house lock still applies: look before name; make before polish; child’s mark on the child’s page. If step-by-step becomes the only hour for three weeks, AS-05 flags the diet — schedule one open MARK/LOOK — without banning the quiet toy or the confidence-building copy page.

Video programs lower parent load and can raise sample-matching pressure. Pause the video. Cover the finished sample if needed. Let the child struggle before rescue. Demo on demo paper. Return the tool. The program is a help. It is not a replacement for authorship honesty.
