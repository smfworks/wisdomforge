# Welcome

*History for Emerging Adults*  
Michael Gannotti

---

You are about to teach history at the kitchen table — or at the library table, or on a courthouse step with a plaque in view — with a fifteen- to eighteen-year-old who can already read dense prose and who still needs help looking before summarizing.

This book exists because high-school history is easy to mistime. One house waits until an AP binder arrives. Another races through an encyclopedia of years. A third treats founding lines as campaign speech. A fourth files a fluent review-book paragraph as “what happened.” None of those is the History Hour this book teaches.

History at 15–18 is a **named founding document on the table before the tidy story**. The student covers the body until they can say who made this, when, for whom, and against what problem. Then they look. Then they talk. Then they try one small move. You ask one good question and wait.

## The focus lock

Across weeks, you walk a **Constitution chain** as documents with makers:

**Declaration → Articles / compacts → Constitution → Bill of Rights → selected Federalist / Brutus edges → amendment / interpretation edges.**

One main excerpt per Hour. A source card. An honesty card (original wording, excerpt, later printing, modernized spelling, Publius pen name). Then-words before now-lectures. Dual voices when the week needs them. Not a campaign speech. Not eighty-five Federalist essays as the month. For a Federalist deep week, point once to *Publius* and keep today’s excerpt on the table.

The chain is the habit of returning to attributable text. Coverage is a map. Depth is the hour. A light tour through mounds of factual information can entertain; the mind still wants pattern built slowly, with repeated passes that go deeper.

If your family already used *History for Young Minds* (ages 11–14), you will recognize Source Before Summary. This book ages that habit up onto denser civic texts and a chain the family can name across weeks. It is not a reprint of that book, of *History for Little Thinkers*, of *History for Homeschooling*, or of *Publius*. One pointer each, then teach 15–18 history looking. It is also not a sequel to *Critical Thinking for Emerging Adults* or *English for Emerging Adults*. Those books own neighboring craft. This one owns the founding document on the table.

## What a good History Hour looks like

Picture thirty to fifty minutes.

You put a short credited founding excerpt on the table with the credit line and honesty card visible. You cover the body for a few minutes. Your student says who made it, when, for whom, and what kind of text it is — or honestly says “we don’t know yet.” You uncover a short stretch of the body. Together you Observe → Reflect → Question. You give a short background or name last week’s chain link and next week’s. You ask one authentic question and wait. They try one thing: a sourcing sentence, a then-word sticky, a claim that points at a named line. You leave an exit ticket. You stop while a look remains.

That is enough for today. Library and museum outings motivate. They do not replace the looking move. A plaque is print about a place. A civic clause is a document. A Federalist paragraph is a newspaper argument. None of those is a team jersey.

## What this book will not do

It will not give you a 180-day worksheet dump. It will not sell you a curriculum. It will not promise a score. It will not title your transcript **Emerging Adults History I**. It will not make APUSH or AP Government the year’s identity — those remain optional edges when the kitchen is ready. It will not turn the Hour into a culture-war pamphlet or a campaign speech. It will not reprint sister manuals with longer words. It will not claim that one well-sourced amendment mints a citizen. Teach looking because that is how history works.

## Two readers

I write to you, the parent or co-teacher, as capable, busy, and willing. Many adults feel rusty on the founding documents they last saw as a list of names and dates. That is normal. This book makes you fluent enough in today’s looking move to hear a summary-first answer, a review-book-as-primary claim, a year-first quiz, a Federalist victory lap with no second voice, a generated “Madison diary” filed as real, or a model summary parked on the table as the source — and to ask a better question without grabbing the answer or filling the silence with the story.

I also write short student pages your adolescent can actually use. Never baby-talk. Never a lecture about their parents. At **15–16** they can cover the body until who-when-audience-purpose is said, run Observe → Reflect → Question with purpose and limitation named, treat Declaration / Articles / Constitution / Bill of Rights excerpts as documents with makers, hold one Federalist *or* Brutus edge with a maker card, interrupt presentism with a their-world prompt, and write a short claim paragraph pointing at a named source with one limitation. At **17–18** they can add Federalist *and* Brutus on one question, critique usefulness for a named inquiry, integrate evidence across two chain links, write a longer claim with warrant and limit, treat a later amendment or interpretation excerpt as another document with makers, and — if they choose — sit an occasional DBQ-shaped stretch that remains their own work.

Place by skill, not birthday. A path through high-school history looking is the promise. A diploma is not. A percentile is not. An AP score is not the only path that counts.

# If you only remember five things

Keep these five on a sticky near the table. Everything else in this book is how to practice them.

1. **Source before summary.** Put the credit line and honesty card in view before anyone absorbs the body as the story. Attribution is the first look, not a footnote after the lecture. Historians look at who made a piece before they trust the body; able high-school students often dive into the text first. Your kitchen can practice the first habit without pretending anyone has become a professional historian.

2. **Ask who made this / when / for whom / against what problem** before the body becomes a slogan. A founding excerpt is a human artifact with makers, audiences, and limits. Cover the body until those are said — or honestly unknown. “Unknown” is a better answer than an invented biography.

3. **Ask one good question and wait.** An authentic question is one you have not already written the sentence for. After you ask, count a slow three. After they stop, wait again. Look at the piece, not at the adolescent, if the silence is hard. Do not fill the quiet with the right year. Stuck silence usually means the question was vague, wait-time was zero, or they are hunting for what you want — not that they “aren’t a history kid.”

4. **The Constitution chain is documents with makers**, not slogans and not a campaign speech. Walk Declaration → Articles / compacts → Constitution → Bill of Rights → selected Federalist / Brutus edges → amendment / interpretation edges as pieces people wrote, contested, amended, and reinterpreted. One excerpt per Hour. Dual voices when the week needs them. For a Federalist deep week, point once to *Publius*; keep today’s paragraph on the table.

5. **Place by skill, not birthday.** A fifteen-year-old who sources before the body may run denser dual-voice work. A seventeen-year-old who treats the review book as “the facts” needs more maker-before-body practice. A path through high-school history looking is the promise. A percentile is not. An AP score is not the only path that counts. Turning eighteen is not a licence to skip practice.

Keep these five clean. AI honesty — a generated diary is not a primary; a model summary is not the source; the parent hosts and stays in the loop — lives once in **The History Hour**. Later chapters point back.

# The History Hour

![A kitchen table with a founding excerpt, an honesty card, and Observe/Reflect/Question stickies — still-life, no people, no logos.](book/art/history-hour-setup.jpg){width=100%}

This chapter says the session once for the whole book. Later chapters point back. You do not need a new ritual every week. You need a shape you can repeat with a new founding excerpt on the table.

History Hour runs about **30–50 minutes**. It is not a museum field trip every day. It is not a silent “read the chapter and write a summary” hour. It is not an AP DBQ every Friday. It is not a civics rally.

## Session shape — five beats

**1. Source before summary (5–10 min).**  
Put attribution in view first: author or maker (or Publius / Anti-Federalist pen name), date, type of text, audience if known, and an honesty card — original wording / later copy / excerpt / translation / modernized spelling. The student names what they notice about the *source*, not yet the full story. Cover the body with a sticky if temptation is high.

**2. Short look at the body (8–15 min).**  
Read a short excerpt aloud together, or silently with scaffolds (glossary sticky; adult reads one archaic paragraph once). Observe → Reflect → Question. One clear historical question the text can actually speak to. Star lines that help; notice lines that do not.

**3. Short background or chain link (5–10 min).**  
Now the narrative — brief. Name people and place. If this week is mid-chain, name the previous link and the next in one breath: “Last week: Confederation requisitions problem. Today: Constitution Article I powers. Next: one Federalist paragraph arguing for energy in government.” Then stop talking.

**4. One try (10–18 min).**  
One try. Not a packet. A corroboration sticky, a claim-with-warrant sentence, a map annotation, a civic-clause paraphrase with then-meaning, a Federalist / Anti-Federalist same-question pair, or a talk-box round. At 17–18 an occasional multi-document stretch may appear — still not the weekly spine.

**5. Exit ticket (2–5 min).**  
Spoken sourcing sentence, claim with a starred source line, same / different / still wonder note, then-word / now-word pair, warrant sentence, or map sticker with a reason. Note one diagnostic error if any. Done-enough is “right, or wrong-with-a-reason we can use tomorrow,” not a percentage.

**Optional sixth beat (siblings / co-op):** after someone answers, wait so each speaker is heard before you evaluate. Whose turn?

### Band forks inside the hour

**15–16:** source card → short look with scaffolds on dense civic prose → short background / previous-and-next chain link → try that may include one Constitution section, one Federalist *or* Brutus paragraph, or two light excerpts → exit as sourcing sentence or claim + one evidence.

**17–18:** source card → more independent look → short background → try that may include Federalist *and* Brutus on one question, a claim paragraph with warrant, an amendment / interpretation edge, or an occasional DBQ-shaped stretch → exit as paragraph or annotated excerpt. Still not APUSH as the year’s identity.

### Dose honesty

School social-studies blocks run long with specialists. A parent hour is not a failed block. If a day only holds thirty minutes: source card + one Observe / Reflect + exit ticket. There is no national history-minutes study that tells you the magic number. A named hour with named moves is the right ambition.

## Talk-box shape (once)

Every teaching chapter carries a talk box. The shape is the same; the locked opening changes.

**Opening.** Exact wording lives in each chapter. It is authentic — about what problem this founding document was trying to solve, who made it, what the next link proposes, what a rights line is trying to do, what two voices stress and fear, what would have to be true in *their* world, what they claim with a named source, or what they can show two ways. Not “did you have fun.” Not “what year was the Constitution ratified.”

**Follow-ups (pick three to five).**  
- Observe: What do you *see* / *read*?  
- Maker / audience: Who made this / for whom / against what problem?  
- Reason tied to the piece: How do you know from *this* line?  
- Check or alternative: What else could be true / what would have to be true in *their* world / what does the other voice fear?  
- Revise: You may change your mind / try another line.

**How to wait.** After you ask, a slow three is a useful convention (classroom think-time research; not a kitchen-table history experiment). After they stop, wait again. If they are mid-look or mid-claim, do not cut them off. Look at the piece, the two voices, or the map — not at the adolescent — if the silence is hard.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the question was vague; wait-time was zero; they are hunting a year because you asked year-first; they are guessing what you want; the piece never landed; they offered a summary-only guess; they think history means “repeat the parent’s story”; they think looking means “the review book is the facts”; archaic prose needs a scaffold before talk; they are bracing for a culture-war trap. Pose, wait, and point back at the sourced line. Do not fill the silence with the right year. Do not turn the table into a fight.

## In-home try-it shape (once)

Every named try-it names **time, materials, safety, the fun, the skill**.

**Time:** usually 15–45 minutes inside an Hour or as a short warm-up.  
**Materials:** a credited founding excerpt; sticky for covering the body; honesty card; optional glossary sticky; dated notebook.  
**Safety:** ordinary kitchen sense — no tasting unknown things, no “test” that involves taking something from a store, no lecture to a stranger’s child.  
**The fun:** uncovering a header before the body; sorting original / excerpt / later printing; hearing two voices on one question without picking a team.  
**The skill:** source before summary; Observe → Reflect → Question; then-words; claim with a named limit.

In-home staples you will meet again: honesty card, attribution flash, textbook is an account, Confederation problem clause, article / section close read, one amendment, Publius card first, Anti-Federalist voice, same question two voices, their-world prompt, claim / evidence / warrant / limit.

## Out-of-home try-it shape (once)

**Library primary hunt.** Find one credited founding or ratification-era piece (or a labelled reproduction). Name maker / date from the catalog or credit before reading the story. Exit: credit line + one Observe.

**Museum label honesty.** Read the object label. Who wrote the label (the museum)? What is object versus interpretation? Exit: object fact vs label interpretation. Hands off restricted objects.

**Courthouse / civic plaque.** Source the plaque. Note install date. If it quotes a founding line, compare plaque wording to document wording. Exit: install date + one wording difference if any. Stay in public space; no trespass.

**Archive appointment (17–18, with adult).** Request one folder or item. Follow handling rules. Source. One Observe. Thank staff.

**Bookstore / used-shop secondary spot.** Find a secondary book on ratification or the Constitution. Source it as secondary. Ask what primary it might point to. Exit: secondary citation + one desired primary type.

Ordinary outing safety: roads, aisle courtesy, photo policy, no cold oral-history quiz of strangers, no photographing strangers without care, family artifacts only with permission.

## AI at the History Hour (ages 15–18)

Say this once. Later chapters point back.

The student looks at the piece-from-then, sources it before the summary, and claims from what they saw. The tool does not write the year or invent the past. **COPPA does not bind** ages 15–18 — the under-13 line is behind them; that is why *History for Young Minds* was stricter on account holding. **The parent still hosts and stays in the loop.** The lock is pedagogical: the crutch, the companion-shaped hole, unaided looking and claim, and — at 17–18 — AP / Common App integrity.

### Ages 15–16 (COPPA off; parent still in the room)

Parent co-holds the account. **Still in the room** for History Hour. Still no unsupervised chatbot as the only partner during the attempt. More document load than 11–14; still not a private historian companion.

**You (the parent) may**, on *your* account (or a co-held account you can see), before or after the student tries:

1. Ask the tool to **explain this week’s idea to you** from a **named** textbook page or credited primary / set — not to the student as a live partner.  
2. Ask for **extra practice** that matches today’s looking (a blank timeline, a map with empty labels, a two-account sheet, a claim frame, an SAQ stem set) with answers only on a **PARENT KEY** page the student never sees.  
3. Ask for a short **SCRIPT** you will say *after* they look and try attribution, corroboration, or a claim — questions that keep *your* voice and the student’s eyes on the credited piece(s).  
4. Ask for **revision questions after a claim or essay draft** — not a rewrite of the student’s paragraph or DBQ.  
5. Ask for a **hint** that is not the finished essay and not a fake diary.  
6. After they finish, show the tool **work they already did** (crop the photo to the paper, map, or packet) and ask what mix-up is likely — then *you* talk to the student.  
7. Ask for **discussion questions** you will vet and ask yourself — the student answers you, not the tab.

### Ages 17–18 (more agency; co-hold; AP / Common App integrity)

Parent co-holds / can audit the account. Still no unsupervised chatbot as the only partner during the attempt when the parent is co-teacher. Turning 17 or 18 is **not a licence** for a private historian companion, live-exam AI, invent-a-diary, or AI substantive prose on a college application.

Same seven allows as above. The student may sit *next to* the parent while the parent uses a tool. They may hear revision questions after they have drafted a claim or essay. They may type their own “I claim ___ because [named source] says ___. A limitation is ___. Another source that corroborates / complicates is ___.” The blanks are the student’s. A model that fills the blanks is still the crutch.

**Integrity add-ons at 17–18:**

- **AP History / AP Government / timed closed assessment:** tools for learning and labelled practice; **not** for live exams. College Board 2026 AP Exam Terms: no AI-generated Test answers or submissions; no accessing ChatGPT, Photomath, or similar during the Test or breaks.  
- **College applications / Common App:** personal statements and short answers that touch history / civics identity must remain the student’s own substantive work. Misrepresenting AI substantive output as one’s own is fraud guidance territory. Revision questions after *the student’s* draft remain allowed; “write my Common App essay” is the ban.

### Must not (both grains)

1. Paste tonight’s worksheet / DBQ packet / map and take the answers (**photo-to-key**).  
2. Ask the tool to **write the student’s history paragraph, essay, DBQ, or LEQ** or “fix this to an A.”  
3. Ask the tool to **generate a diary, letter, or speech from then as if it were a real primary**.  
4. Leave a chatbot running as the student’s only partner during the attempt — not at 15, not at 18 — when the parent is co-teacher.  
5. Put a **model summary** on the table as the source instead of a credited piece-from-then (or set).  
6. Use TTS, narrated rewrite, or a voice-clone toy to **replace looking** at the piece(s) that are today’s lesson. (Parent read-aloud or labelled TTS for a longer survey chapter the student cannot yet hold alone can open *access* — while the looking lesson stays on credited piece(s) on the table.)  
7. Treat a **generated painting** of the past as a primary source (looking together labelled as modern make-believe is fine; filing it as evidence is not).  
8. Invent research or trust a fluent citation without opening the paper.  
9. Detector-grade the student’s claim essay / DBQ and call the score honesty.  
10. Coach live-exam cheating or application fraud.

**One crutch sentence, labelled, then teach:** in a **high-school math** trial, an unguarded chatbot made practice look better and left students worse on the test with the window closed (Bastani et al., 2025). **That is not a 15–18 history study.** **OFF-AGE for literacy / history.** The kitchen-table rule it supports: the model may prepare the adult and the next looking page; it may not do the student’s looking, sourcing, claim, live exam, or college-application essay when those are the lesson.

**One looking sentence:** sources do not by themselves constitute evidence; ask who made this, when, for whom, and why — on a real credited piece, not on a model summary.

A fluent filled blank from a machine is performance, not the student’s history looking. Keep accounts, logs, and keys where the parent can open them. A history tool is not a secret friend. Names that stay separate: Hermes 4 is not Hermes Agent; Grok is not Grok Bot; agents are not chatbots; there is no Grok Bot education SKU for this kitchen.

# Chapter 1 — What history looks like at 15–18

## Why this matters

History at this age is not a waiting room for AP. It is not an encyclopedia slog. It is a named founding document on the table before the parent’s tidy story, attribution before the body, and a **chain** the family can walk across weeks.

That chain is this book’s spine:

**Declaration → Articles / compacts → Constitution → Bill of Rights → selected Federalist / Brutus edges → amendment / interpretation edges** — as documents people wrote, contested, amended, and reinterpreted.

Why struggle for that? Because high school tempts three shortcuts that feel responsible and quietly undo looking.

The first shortcut is **coverage**. A year map that names everything from early settlement to yesterday can look like rigor. Light tours through mounds of factual information may entertain; they leave few traces. The mind wants pattern and form, built slowly, with repeated passes that go deeper. Coverage is a map. Depth is the hour.

The second shortcut is **review-book-as-primary**. Able students — including students preparing for college-level exams — often treat a textbook or review paragraph as “just reporting the facts.” Historians usually rank that kind of later telling last when they ask what a source can show. Your kitchen does not need to distrust all books. It needs to source the review book as later / school audience, then put a credited piece-from-then beside it.

The third shortcut is **AP-as-identity**. College-level survey courses and their document lists can be useful edges. They are not this book’s spine. A kitchen Constitution-chain Hour is not an exam date and not a transcript title that sells a brand.

The Hour’s answer is Source Before Summary aged up from middle-grades civic *edges* into a chain that can carry denser prose, dual voices, and claim-with-evidence writing. If your family used *History for Young Minds*, you already practiced maker-before-body on gentler civic clauses. This chapter does not reprint that bank. It names the high-school object: a chain you can feel across weeks, one main excerpt per Hour.

What the chain is *not* matters as much as what it is. It is not a campaign speech. It is not a virtue machine. It is not a Federalist monograph — for a deep Federalist week, point once to *Publius* and keep today’s excerpt on the table. It is not a reprint of *History for Homeschooling*’s grade map or AP handbook. It is not “Emerging Adults History I” on a transcript. It is Hour teaching: one attributable piece, source card, honesty card, then-words, previous-and-next link named when you are mid-chain.

Parents sometimes ask whether fifteen is “too early” for founding documents. If a student can source before the body with scaffolds, the answer is no. Parents sometimes ask whether seventeen means the kitchen Hour is obsolete. If looking, sourcing, and claim-with-limit are still the lesson, the answer is also no. Skill, not birthday.

Place by skill, not birthday. Gloss the title word “emerging adults”: one common research construct for “emerging adulthood” points roughly at ages 18–25 and is *not* late adolescence still in secondary school. Do not write “now they are emerging adults, so skip practice.” Fifteen-to-eighteen-year-olds can do real history looking. They do not become historians because a parent bought an AP binder, and they do not become fair-minded because a parent read them a morality play. History inquiry also does not automatically mint citizens. Teach looking because that is how history works.

If you finish this chapter able to put an attribution card and honesty card down before the story, name this week’s chain link, and hear a summary-first answer without grabbing the year — you have unlocked the rest of the book.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

### One everyday picture

Imagine a short printed excerpt from the Declaration — one sentence, not the whole parchment — face-up on the table. A sticky covers the body. Only the credit line and an honesty card show: who, when, what kind of text, original wording or later printing. ### One precise picture

High-school history standards maps (C3 Framework pathways for grades 9–12) name moves you can actually touch at the kitchen table: who made this / when / place / audience / purpose; detect limitations in evidence and in later tellings; critique usefulness for a named question; integrate evidence into a reasoned argument; notice how present perspectives shape interpretations of the past. Civics pathways name constitutions, powers, and limits that have changed and are still contested — as disciplinary objects, not as campaign fuel. Those pathways are a **map of moves**, not a content encyclopedia and not a statute for your house. Content choices stay with you and your state’s (or your family’s) map. This book’s job is the Hour on a piece.

Research on historical thinking is blunt about ordinary habits. Sourcing — looking at attribution *before* reading the body — is what historians do almost always and what able AP students often skip (one oft-cited contrast: historians sourced most of the time; AP students far less). Presentism is not a character flaw; it is the mind at rest. When a sixteen-year-old collapses the Constitution into “they wanted freedom,” or imports today’s party map onto 1787, or says “they were just corrupt,” do not scold. Ask what problem *they* were trying to solve, who else was in the room, what no one person decided, and what the document cannot tell us.

Coverage tours leave few traces; the mind wants pattern built slowly. A textbook or review book is one account, written later, for a school or exam audience — useful for some questions, not “what happened, period.” Classroom document-lesson studies in this age band support a *shape* you can borrow: central question, short background, short excerpts, looking moves, talk with facilitation. Materials alone do not produce talk. Your kitchen is not a published classroom coefficient. Borrow the shape. Label the origin. Then teach.

Knowledge still constrains thinking. Give the piece and a tiny story the piece can carry. Do not demand multi-perspective historiography of 1780s finance in week one of age fifteen. Dual perspective grows with practice when prompted; it is not the default even near adulthood. Later chapters will put Federalist and Brutus on one question. This chapter installs the Hour identity first.

This is a useful study landscape, not a promise that every home will see the same result. Then teach.

### Wrong answers you should be able to hear

1. **Summary first.** “Just tell me what happened.” Usually means the body was uncovered before attribution, or the adult filled silence with the story. Repair: cover the body; ask who / when / for whom / against what problem.

2. **Review-book-as-primary.** “The AP book just says the facts.” Usually means a later telling is being filed as the piece-from-then. Repair: source the review book as later / school audience; open one credited primary beside it; ask which questions each can answer.

3. **Year-first.** “What year was it ratified?” as the opening move. Usually means quiz posture. Repair: ask what problem the text was trying to solve before the tidy date. Dates are tools under stories already owned.

4. **Birthday gate.** “They’re fifteen, so they must be ready for the whole Constitution,” or “They’re seventeen, so skip practice.” Usually means placement by calendar. Repair: place by skill — can they cover the body until attribution is said?

5. **Encyclopedia hour / model summary as source / AP unit poster as a primary / “you’re a historian now.”** Usually means coverage crush, machine fluency, or overclaim. Repair: one excerpt; label later telling; keep AP as an edge; aim for serious amateur looking. A generated Founder letter is not a primary. A unit poster is a later teaching tool, not a piece-from-then.

## How to teach it this week

### Session shape

Use the five beats from **The History Hour**: source card → short look (Observe → Reflect → Question) → short background or previous-and-next chain link → one try → exit ticket.

**Warm-up looking (first two minutes).** Hand the honesty card only. Ask: “What does this card already tell us before we read?” Wait.

**Exact wording you can say:**

“Before we hear the tidy story, look only at the credit line and the honesty card. Who made this? When? For whom? What kind of text is it — and is this original wording, an excerpt, or a later printing?”

After attribution is said:

“What problem was this founding document trying to solve — and who made it, for whom — before we hear the tidy story?”

Wait. Count a slow three. Look at the piece, not at the adolescent.

If they answer with a slogan (“freedom”), point back: “Show me a line that makes you say that — or say what we still don’t know.”

### First try-it for the student

Cover the body. Student says maker, date, audience or purpose guess, text type, and honesty-card status — or “unknown.” Uncover one short stretch. Student stars one line that seems to speak to a problem people were trying to solve. Exit ticket: one spoken sourcing sentence + this week’s chain-link name.

### How to fade help

Day 1: you point to each field on the source card.  
Day 2: they fill the card; you only ask “what’s still unknown?”  
Day 3: they run attribution without the card template, then check themselves.  
Day 4: they teach the source-card steps to you in thirty seconds, then you swap seats for one try.  
When they jump to summary, cover the body again without scolding. Hearing a wrong reason, asking a better question, then naming a better move if needed, is teaching. Silence in the face of a review-book-as-primary claim is not kindness; a scolding lecture is not teaching either.

### When to stop talking

After you name last week’s link and next week’s in one sentence. After one authentic question. After a hint that is a question, not the story. Struggle before rescue: ask, wait, hint, then model. Do not grab the answer. Do not fill the silence with the year. Do not paste a machine’s past as theirs.

## Practice that actually builds learning

Library, archive, museum, and kitchen *are* the practice — they do not replace the looking move. Pick one in-home and one out-of-home this week. Blocked practice (one new move) first; mixed practice later in the week when the move is warm.

Ample practice does not mean assigning every named try-it below. A chapter picks what the week needs. Coverage itch is the enemy of looking. Three solid Hours with one attribution drill, one chain card, and one library hunt beat a packet that touches everything once.

Safety stays ordinary across all try-its: roads, aisle courtesy, museum hands-off rules, archive handling rules, no tasting unknown things, no shoplifting a “test,” no lecture to a stranger’s child, no cold public quiz of strangers for “oral history,” no photographing strangers without care, family artifacts only with permission. A plaque is print about a place. It is not a sermon about a city. A civic clause is a document. It is not a culture-war pamphlet.

### Talk box

**Opening (locked):**  
“What problem was this founding document trying to solve — and who made it, for whom — before we hear the tidy story?”

**Follow-ups:**  
1. What do you notice on the credit line?  
2. What kind of text is this — constitution article, newspaper essay, pamphlet, later printing?  
3. Who was it for?  
4. How do you know that from the *source lines*, not only from my summary?  
5. You may change your mind when we look again.

**How to wait:** After the opening, count three. After their first answer, wait again before you evaluate. If they freeze, point at the credit line: “Start with one word you can see.” If they are mid-claim, do not cut them off.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the question felt like a trap; wait-time was zero; they are hunting a year; the piece never landed; they think you want the tidy story; archaic load needs a gloss before talk; they are bracing for a culture-war trap. Repair the question. Point at the sourced line. Lower the stakes. Do not deliver a presentism sermon. Do not turn the table into a fight.

### In-home try-its

**AS-01 Observe Only Minute**  
- **Time:** 10–20 min  
- **Materials:** Credited founding excerpt or image; timer; notebook  
- **Safety:** Ordinary kitchen sense  
- **The fun:** Three minutes of “only what you can point to” — no theory yet  
- **The skill:** Observe before Reflect  
- **Steps:** (1) Set three minutes. (2) List pointable facts only — words, dates, layout, signatures visible. (3) Share. (4) Only then Reflect.  
- **Error patterns:** Jumping to theory; joking instead of noticing  
- **Exit ticket:** Five Observe bullets  

**AS-04 Attribution Flash Soft**  
- **Time:** 10–15 min  
- **Materials:** Five header-only cards (founding / ratification-era credits; include one “unknown”)  
- **Safety:** No speed-trivia cruelty; inventing facts is worse than unknown  
- **The fun:** Micro-drill with permission to say unknown  
- **The skill:** Who / when / type before body  
- **Steps:** Flash each header; student names attribution fields; celebrate honest unknown  
- **Exit ticket:** 4/5 named or honestly unknown  

**AS-08 Model Summary Is Not a Source**  
- **Time:** 15–25 min  
- **Materials:** One later-telling summary (textbook or review-book paragraph) + one credited primary on the same topic  
- **Safety:** Label the summary as later telling from the start  
- **The fun:** Sorting two objects that look “official”  
- **The skill:** Refuse filing a model summary as the primary  
- **Steps:** Source both. Ask which questions each can answer. Refuse swapping them. Reopen the primary before you leave the table.  
- **Exit ticket:** Labelled pair — later telling / primary  

**TIME-02 Chain Chronology Card**  
- **Time:** 25–40 min  
- **Materials:** Cards: Confederation problem → Convention → Constitution text → ratification essays → Bill of Rights  
- **Safety:** Do not crush the whole chain into one day of memorizing with no documents  
- **The fun:** Sorting a spine you can feel  
- **The skill:** Place this week’s excerpt among neighbors  
- **Steps:** Sort cards. Name which card today’s excerpt sits on. Say what came before / after. Optional: leave one card blank for “we haven’t opened this yet.”  
- **Exit ticket:** This week’s link named + neighbors  

**CIV-06 Civic Text Is Still a Source**  
- **Time:** 20–35 min  
- **Materials:** Printed civic excerpt already used this week  
- **Safety:** Keep the Hour from becoming a cynicism seminar (“all civic talk is fake”) or a culture-war rant  
- **The fun:** Spotting a meme-style misuse and repairing it  
- **The skill:** Founding lines stay documents with makers  
- **Steps:** List ways people misuse founding lines (meme, slogan, out of context). Rewrite one misuse as a sourced claim that returns to maker / audience / purpose.  
- **Exit ticket:** One misuse named + one repair (return to source card)  

**TALK-01 Source Before Summary Round**  
- **Time:** 15–25 min  
- **Materials:** Attribution card only at first  
- **Safety:** Do not reveal the body early to “help”  
- **The fun:** The uncover moment  
- **The skill:** Attribution talk with wait  
- **Steps:** Pose “What do you notice about who made this?” Wait 3–5 seconds. Uptake. Only then uncover body.  
- **Exit ticket:** One attribution fact spoken

- **The skill:** Later telling vs piece-from-then

## For the student

History at your age is not waiting until someone hands you an AP binder. It is also not reading a huge pile of facts once and calling it a year.

Here is the idea: **look at a named founding document before you hear the tidy story.** Who made it? When? For whom? Against what problem? What kind of text is it — and is this the original wording, an excerpt, or a later printing?

You are practicing a life of history looking for ordinary high-school challenges: a credit line and honesty card, a chain link you can name, two voices later when the week needs them, a claim that points at a named source with a limit. You are not being asked to become a professional historian this month.

A few things that are *not* the goal this week: memorizing every date on a wall timeline; winning a culture-war argument; finishing an AP packet every Friday; treating a chatbot diary as a letter from 1787; calling yourself a historian because you sourced one header. The goal is simpler and harder: put your finger on the credit line before the story, and say what problem the document seems to be answering before you borrow someone else’s summary.

### Tiny worked example

**Diagnostic 1 — They dive into the body every time.**  
Likely cause: habit plus adult story arriving too early.  
Next move: Physical cover (sticky or paper) until attribution is spoken. Run TALK-01 three days in a row. Celebrate “unknown.” If you keep uncovering “just this once,” the habit cannot form.

# Chapter 2 — Reading a founding document

## Why this matters

A founding excerpt on the table is not a slogan waiting to be recited. It is a source an adolescent can look at — with eyes, a pencil, and a few honest questions.

This chapter owns the looker’s tool grown up for dense civic prose: **Observe → Reflect → Question**, with purpose, audience, and limitation named. The credit line and honesty card are part of the look. They are not a bureaucratic delay before the “real” history. They *are* the start of historical looking.

Library of Congress Teaching with Primary Sources language is plain: primary sources are the raw materials of history — original documents and objects created at the time under study. Analysis can prompt curiosity and move students from concrete observations to questioning and inference. Sources are incomplete snippets. Careful selection and planning matter. That is encouragement, not a certificate that a sixteen-year-old has become an independent historian.

Eighteenth-century periodic sentences need gloss. You may read one hard paragraph aloud. The student still does the sourcing talk and the claim. Adapted excerpts with attribution visible are honest pedagogy, not “dumbing down,” when the looking moves stay intact.

If Chapter 1 installed the Hour identity — founding document before tidy story — Chapter 2 teaches how to *read* that document without skipping Observe, without treating every reproduction as “the real thing,” and without filing a generated Founder letter as a primary.

Why this struggle is worth it: high-school students can decode a page and still never look. They jump to caption, summary, meme, or the parent’s story. Observe → Reflect → Question slows the jump. The honesty card stops a different jump — the jump that treats every old-looking page as equally “primary.” Together they make the Constitution chain readable as human artifacts, not as wallpaper behind a lecture.

This chapter maps the DOC and AS activity banks. Later chapters will reuse the same looking moves on Confederation problem clauses, Constitution sections, rights lines, and Federalist / Brutus edges. If the look is shaky here, those weeks become recitation. If the look is steady, those weeks become history.

Unlock for the week: your student can point to the credit line and honesty card and say maker, date, type, and original-or-later before hearing the summary — then Observe something on the page that is not already the adult’s story.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

### One everyday picture

Two stickies sit on a short Constitution section. The first says **Observe** and holds only what anyone could point to: a list, a date line, a repeated word, a signature block if present. The second says **Reflect** and may only speak when it cites an Observe (“I think this is aimed at state ratifiers because the credit line and the ‘We the People’ opening sit together like a public argument”). A third column waits for **Question** — wonder that could drive a next chain link. That is the look. The lecture waits outside the door.

### One precise picture

Observe → Reflect → Question is a curiosity and observation-to-inference tool. Pair it with sourcing: attribution before the body. High-school pathways ask students to detect limitations in evidence and in secondary interpretations, critique usefulness from maker / date / place / audience / purpose, and pursue further inquiry from questions about multiple sources. Those are ceiling pathways you can touch lightly this week — not a historiography seminar.

Sourcing before the body remains the load-bearing flip, now on denser prose. Cover the meta until who-when-why is said. Practice the honesty card alone before the Hour: original wording, excerpt, translation, later printing, modernized spelling, Publius pseudonym. Barton’s evidence work (sister tradition) reminds us that spontaneous “use of evidence” is not the default; the habit must stay explicit. Fluency is not historical thought. A smooth oral recap of a speech excerpt is not the same as naming audience and purpose.

Print load still matters at 15–18. Sister literacy craft applies: morphology and eighteenth-century syntax can block the look. Scaffold. Do not abandon the piece for a model summary. A model summary is a later telling. It may help after sourcing. It may never be filed as the primary on the table.

What counts as a source this week is practical. A credited public-domain founding excerpt counts. A labelled library reproduction counts when labelled. A textbook paragraph counts as a *secondary / later telling* for some questions — not as the piece-from-then. A museum object counts as an object; the wall label is interpretation. A plaque counts as a later telling with an install date. A generated diary does not count as a primary. A generated painting of the past does not count as a primary (looking together labelled as modern make-believe is fine; filing it as evidence is not).

Publius needs a card even in a reading week: pseudonym, newspaper or book-first venue, date, audience line when accurate. You are not teaching the whole newspaper war here. You are refusing to treat a pen name as a private diary. For a Federalist deep week, point once to *Publius*; keep today’s paragraph on the table.

This is a useful study landscape, not a promise that every home will see the same result. Then teach.

### Wrong answers you should be able to hear

1. **Caption-only guess.** Student reads the modern caption or your spoken gloss and skips the document. Usually means Observe never happened. Repair: cover the caption; three minutes Observe only.

2. **All sources equal / no maker.** “It’s just a document.” Usually means attribution was skipped. Repair: honesty card first; who / when / for whom spoken aloud.

3. **Unmarked reproduction as “the real thing.”** Student treats a modern reprint or aged filter as an original without labelling. Usually means honesty card missing. Repair: AS-05 Reproduction Honesty Sort; labelled reproduction is fine when labelled.

4. **Generated primary filed as real.** Chatbot “Madison diary” or Founder letter on the table as evidence. Usually means AI honesty failed. Repair: label fiction; find a credited LOC / library / public-domain piece.

5. **Skipping Observe / treating Publius as a single person’s diary without a card.** Jump to theory, or collapse a pen name into private confession. Repair: Observe-only minute; Publius card first (pseudonym, venue, date) before body — Chapter 5 deepens dual voices; this week still requires the card.

You can hear generated-primary filing (“Madison wrote this for my notebook”). You can hear Observe skipped (“basically they wanted…”). 1. **Source card + honesty card (5–10).** Attribution before body. Name original-or-later out loud.  
2. **Observe → Reflect → Question (8–15).** Observe first, timed if needed. Reflect only with reasons tied to Observes. Questions last.  
3. **Short background or chain link (5–10).** Brief. What the piece needs — not a chapter dump.  
4. **One try (10–18).** DOC or AS try-it below. One try.  
5. **Exit ticket (2–5).** Starred line + “This helps answer ___ because ___,” or honesty-card fields spoken, or Observe / Reflect / Question three lines.

**Exact wording you can say:**

“Who made this, when, and for whom — and is this original wording, an excerpt, or a later printing?”

Wait. Then:

“What do you *see* or *read* before we decide what it means?”

After Observes land:

“What was the maker trying to do — and what can’t this source tell us?”

If they answer with a modern slogan, try: “Park the slogan. Show me three words on the page that you can point to.” If they treat Publius as a diary, try: “What does the card say about how this was published?” If they ask you to “just tell what happened,” try: “I can give short background after we source and Observe. First — one credit-line fact.”

### First try-it for the student

Point to the credit line and honesty card. Say maker, date, type, and original-or-later before hearing the summary. Then three Observe bullets only. Exit: those three bullets + one honesty-card field.

### How to fade help

Day 1: you run Observe on a shared sticky; they add one bullet.  
Day 2: they Observe alone for three minutes; you only ask “which of these is a Reflect that snuck in?”  
Day 3: they complete Observe → Reflect → Question with you only supplying the historical question card.  
Day 4: they write the honesty card without a template, then check against yours.  
When Reflect arrives without Observe, send them back: “Cite an Observe first.”

**Day A:** Honesty card + AS-01 Observe Only Minute on one Declaration or Constitution excerpt. Locked opening. Exit: five Observes + honesty fields.

**Day B:** DOC-03 Letter Header Before Body (or DOC-01 if you prefer a civic excerpt). Practice revise-without-shame when purpose guesses change after the body appears. Exit: writer / audience / one unknown.

Composite illustration only. Your week may run two Hours or five. Keep one main excerpt per sitting. Prefer a second sitting later in the week to a ninety-minute dump of five documents.

### When to stop talking

After one gloss of an archaic sentence. After the historical question is posed. After a hint that points at a line (“Look at the greeting / the list / the date”). Do not narrate the document’s meaning while they are still Observing. Do not fill silence with the summary you prepared for later.

### Age-band moves

**15–16:** Prefer DOC-01, DOC-03, DOC-07, AS-01, AS-02, AS-03, AS-05. Keep cartoons soft or save for later. Adult reads one hard paragraph aloud once. Exit oral or one short written sourcing / Observe set. One main excerpt.

**17–18:** Add DOC-04 (period cartoon, not live culture-war), DOC-02 newspaper voice, AS-07 two pieces one question, OUT-04 archive soft with appointment. More independence on Reflect with reason. Ask usefulness: “Useful for which question?” Optional: how could we check with one more piece?

## Practice that actually builds learning

Pick one DOC try-it and one AS try-it this week, plus one out-of-home. Do not assign the whole bank. Library and museum practice the same honesty moves; they do not replace Observe on a piece at home.

Ample practical activities are load-bearing in this book — and still a menu. Coverage itch will whisper that you should run every DOC code before Friday. Resist. One question, one excerpt, done well, beats five documents skimmed. Materials alone do not produce talk; you still pose, wait, and point at the sourced line.

Safety across try-its stays ordinary: roads, aisle courtesy, museum hands-off, archive handling, no cold stranger quiz, no photographing strangers without care, family artifacts only with permission. A civic clause is a document. A period cartoon is a period argument on paper — not a licence to import today’s hottest fight as the default seminar fuel.

### Talk box

**Opening (locked):**  
“Who made this, when, and for whom — and is this original wording, an excerpt, or a later printing?”

**Follow-ups:**  
1. What do you *see* / *read*?  
2. What was the maker trying to do?  
3. What can’t this source tell us?  
4. What do you wonder that could drive a next chain link?  
5. How could we check with one more piece? (especially 17–18)  

**How to wait:** After the locked opening, count three. After the first Observe, wait again before you pile on Reflect. If they jump to theory, say: “Park that as a Question. Give me one more Observe first.”

**Stuck silence usually means:** wait-time was zero; the question was vague; archaic prose load; they think you want the tidy story; they offered a caption-only guess; they are bracing for a trap; the piece never landed because the honesty card was skipped. Scaffold reading before demanding talk. Point at one visible word. Do not fill the silence with the answer key.

### In-home try-its — DOC bank

**DOC-01 One Question, One Excerpt**  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** Central historical question on a card; one scaffolded excerpt with attribution visible  
- **Safety:** Ordinary kitchen sense  
- **The fun:** Matching lines to a question the document can actually answer  
- **The skill:** Question-document fit; attribution first  
- **Steps:** (1) Pose question. (2) Source card first. (3) Read. (4) Mark lines that speak to the question; lines that do not. (5) Exit ticket.  
- **Error patterns:** Question the document cannot answer; packet of five documents; skipping attribution  
- **Exit ticket:** Starred line + “This helps answer ___ because ___.”  

**DOC-02 Newspaper Voice (ratification-era optional)**  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** Short historical newspaper excerpt with masthead / date (ratification-era if available; otherwise any period paper kept soft)  
- **Safety:** No live partisan paper as the default object  
- **The fun:** Masthead as a clue  
- **The skill:** Period newspaper as sourced voice, not “neutral camera”  
- **Steps:** Source masthead; who might readers have been?; one claim the paper makes; one missing voice  
- **Error patterns:** “Newspapers then were neutral”; mocking old ads as the whole past; treating masthead as eyewitness  
- **Exit ticket:** Claim + missing voice  

**DOC-03 Letter Header Before Body**  
- **Time:** 25–40 min  
- **Materials:** One letter photocopy with clear header / signature; sticky covering the body at first  
- **Safety:** No unsigned AI letter presented as real  
- **The fun:** The uncover revise  
- **The skill:** Header before body  
- **Steps:** Uncover only header; who / to whom / when; hypothesize purpose; uncover one short body paragraph; revise purpose guess without shame  
- **Error patterns:** Reading body first; inventing biography from a signature; treating unsigned AI letter as real  
- **Exit ticket:** “Writer: ___. Audience: ___. One thing we don’t know: ___.”  

**DOC-04 Political Cartoon Soft**  
- **Band:** 17–18 (careful 15–16)  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** Period cartoon with Observe / Reflect / Question prompts; avoid live culture-war cartoons  
- **Safety:** Soft entry; no graphic harm as default  
- **The fun:** Symbol hunting with brakes  
- **The skill:** Image as sourced argument, not modern party jersey  
- **Steps:** Observe symbols; Reflect audience / purpose; Question; adult supplies minimal context after Observe  
- **Error patterns:** Jumping to modern party labels; choosing inflammatory modern cartoons  
- **Exit ticket:** “Symbol ___ seems to stand for ___ (guess).”  

**DOC-05 Speech Excerpt Soft**  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** One famous speech paragraph with date / place / audience card (founding or later — keep soft)  
- **Safety:** No present-politics dump as the hour  
- **The fun:** Audience-first listening on the page  
- **The skill:** Speech as occasioned text  
- **Steps:** Source audience first; then words; what problem was the speaker addressing *then*?  
- **Error patterns:** Quoting out of context as a meme; present politics dump  
- **Exit ticket:** Audience + one then-problem  

**DOC-06 Image Pair Same Event**  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** Two images of related scenes; captions covered then revealed  
- **Safety:** Hands-off rules if museum prints; no generated painting filed as primary  
- **The fun:** What each includes / leaves out  
- **The skill:** Bridge toward corroboration without “liar” hunt  
- **Steps:** Source each; what each includes / leaves out; captions last  
- **Error patterns:** “Camera never lies”; treating caption as the only truth  
- **Exit ticket:** Included / left out for each image  

**DOC-07 Textbook Is an Account**  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** One textbook paragraph + one short primary on same topic  
- **Safety:** Do not teach universal distrust  
- **The fun:** Two answers to different questions  
- **The skill:** Later telling vs piece-from-then  
- **Steps:** Source the textbook (later writer, school audience) before reading it as “what happened”; then source the primary; ask which questions each can answer  
- **Error patterns:** “Textbook is lying”; “primary is always true”; filing textbook as primary  
- **Exit ticket:** “Textbook can answer ___. Primary can answer ___.”  

### In-home try-its — AS bank

**AS-01 Observe Only Minute**  
- **Time:** 10–20 min  
- **Materials:** Document or image; timer  
- **Safety:** Ordinary  
- **The fun:** Timer as a game, not a test  
- **The skill:** Concrete noticing  
- **Steps:** 3 minutes Observe only (pointable facts); no Reflect yet; then share  
- **Error patterns:** Jumping to theory; joking instead of noticing  
- **Exit ticket:** Five Observe bullets  

**AS-02 Reflect with Reason**  
- **Time:** 15–25 min  
- **Materials:** Same piece after AS-01  
- **Safety:** Ordinary  
- **The fun:** “Because I saw ___” as a ticket to Reflect  
- **The skill:** Inference tied to evidence  
- **Steps:** Reflect guesses must cite an Observe; wait  
- **Error patterns:** Free-floating opinion; adult filling in reasons  
- **Exit ticket:** One Reflect + starred Observe  

**AS-03 Question Column**  
- **Time:** 15–25 min  
- **Materials:** Three-column sticky or notebook (Observe / Reflect / Question)  
- **Safety:** Ordinary  
- **The fun:** Wonder list that might drive next week’s chain link  
- **The skill:** Questions as inquiry fuel, not gotchas  
- **Steps:** After Observe / Reflect, list questions the piece raises; star one that another source might help  
- **Error patterns:** Only “what year” questions; abandoning the piece  
- **Exit ticket:** One starred wonder  

**AS-05 Reproduction Honesty Sort**  
- **Time:** 20–30 min  
- **Materials:** Original scan printout, labelled reproduction, unmarked modern “aged” image (parent-made for teaching)  
- **Safety:** Gotcha-free — teach the rule, don’t humiliate  
- **The fun:** Sort three look-alikes  
- **The skill:** Honesty about what is on the table  
- **Steps:** Sort: real primary / labelled reproduction / not honest as primary; write one rule  
- **Error patterns:** Gotcha on the learner; skipping “labelled reproduction OK if labelled”  
- **Exit ticket:** One honesty rule aloud  

**AS-07 Two Pieces, One Question**  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** Two sources; one question  
- **Safety:** Ordinary  
- **The fun:** Division of labor between sources  
- **The skill:** Usefulness without forcing both to answer everything  
- **Steps:** Which source better answers which part?; bridge to corroboration later  
- **Error patterns:** Forcing both to answer everything; “one is lying” as first move  
- **Exit ticket:** Source A for ___; Source B for ___.  

### Out-of-home try-its

**OUT-02 Museum Label Honesty**  
- **Time:** visit length varies  
- **Materials:** Museum ticket / free day  
- **Safety:** Hands-off restricted objects; aisle courtesy  
- **The fun:** Object vs interpretation detective work  
- **The skill:** Label is later telling; object is object  
- **Steps:** Read object label; who wrote the label (museum)?; what is object vs interpretation?  
- **Error patterns:** Treating label as eyewitness; touching restricted objects

**OUT-03 Courthouse / Civic Building Plaque**

- **Safety:** Public space only; no trespass  
- **The fun:** Compare plaque wording to a founding line if quoted  
- **The skill:** Plaque as later telling with install date  
- **Steps:** Source plaque; compare to Constitution-chain wording if quoted; note install date  
- **Error patterns:** Trespass; treating plaque as 1787  
- **Exit ticket:** Install date + one wording difference if any  

**OUT-04 Archive Soft (appointment)**  
- **Band:** 17–18  
- **Time:** by appointment  
- **Materials:** Archive rules printout; adult present  
- **Safety:** Follow handling rules; no selfie disruption  
- **The fun:** One real folder, one Observe  
- **The skill:** Archive manners + sourcing  
- **Steps:** Request one folder / item; follow handling rules; source; one Observe; thank staff  
- **Error patterns:** Handling without rules; treating the visit as a photo shoot  
- **Exit ticket:** Call number / credit + Observe  

### Blocked, then mixed

Block early: Observe-only, or honesty-card only, or header-before-body only. Mix later: honesty card + Observe + one Reflect with reason in one sitting. When two pieces appear, refuse “one is lying” as the first corroboration move — use same / different / still wonder.

Accounts answer different questions from different standpoints. That Lee-plain habit starts here even before Chapter 5’s dual voices. If a student says one must be lying, ask: “What question is each piece answering — and what does each leave out?” Then return to Observes on both.

Knowledge first still holds: give the piece and a tiny story the piece can carry. Do not demand a multi-document historiography review in week two of age fifteen. One excerpt. One look. One exit ticket. Tomorrow you can look again at the same piece or open the next chain link — still with the honesty card first — every time you sit down to look.

### One incorrect example to diagnose

A student pastes a prompt — “Write a letter from James Madison about the Constitution, dated 1787” — prints the result in old-looking font, and files it in the notebook under “Primary sources.”

**What went wrong:** A generated letter was filed as a real primary. LOC’s definition does not bend: raw materials created at the time under study. A 2026 chatbot was not.

**Repair:** Label the printout as modern fiction / illustration if you keep it at all. Find a credited public-domain excerpt. Run AS-05 and AS-08. Ask the locked opening on the real piece. Unaided looking first next time; tools only on the parent account after the try, per **The History Hour**.

## For the student

A founding document is something you can look at. You are not waiting for a teacher to “tell you what it means” before you are allowed to notice what is on the page.

At 15–16, your job this week is steady: honesty card, Observe before Reflect, one clear limitation. At 17–18, add usefulness (“useful for which question?”) and, when ready, two pieces on one question without declaring a liar. Neither band is a licence to skip looking because a window already summarized the year.

Here is the idea: **Observe → Reflect → Question.** First, what can you point to? Second, what might that mean — with a reason tied to what you saw? Third, what do you wonder, including what this source cannot tell you?

The credit line and honesty card are part of looking. Who made this? When? For whom? Is this original wording, an excerpt, a later printing, modernized spelling, or a pen name like Publius?

Write three Observes (words you can underline). ## If it isn’t clicking

**Diagnostic 1 — Caption-only or summary-only looking.**  
Likely cause: Observe skipped; adult story arrived early.  
Next move: Cover captions and your own gloss. AS-01 three days. Require five Observes before any Reflect.

**Diagnostic 2 — Honesty card feels like busywork; reproductions blur.**  
Likely cause: The card was never tied to a real mix-up.  
Next move: AS-05 once with three clear sorts. Say the rule aloud: labelled reproduction OK if labelled; generated diary not a primary; model summary not the source.

**Diagnostic 3 — Archaic prose freeze or shrug.**  
Likely cause: print load, not “not a history kid.”  
Next move: You read one hard paragraph aloud. Glossary sticky for three words. Student still sources and Observes. Shrink the excerpt. Prefer DOC-03 header work before dense body paragraphs.

**When to slow down:** Observe still collapses into theory after a week. Stay here. Do not add a five-document packet.

**When to go ahead:** Honesty card is routine; Observe bullets appear before Reflect; student can name what a source cannot tell us. Open Chapter 3 (problem → structure on the chain).

**When to get a human tutor:** Persistent decoding barriers even with read-aloud and gloss — literacy support while you keep the history moves. No shame. Correct a wrong reason (“all sources are equal”) without crushing the attempt to look.

**A note on struggle.** Struggle before rescue still applies. Ask, wait, hint (point at a line), then model one Observe. Grabbing the meaning of the document “to save time” teaches that looking is optional. Filling silence with a fluent summary teaches that the adult’s story is the source. Neither is kindness. # Chapter 3 — From problem to structure

![Three chain cards — Declaration / Articles / Constitution — with one excerpt face-up on the table and the other two face-down; honesty card visible; still-life, no faces, no campaign poster.](book/art/ch03-opener.jpg){width=100%}

## Why this matters

The first three links on the Constitution chain are not a morality play and not a semester of Article-by-Article reading. They are a *problem named*, then a *proposed structure* — documents people wrote when they thought something was broken, and documents they wrote when they thought a better frame might hold.

Declaration → Articles/compacts → Constitution. That order is a spine for the History Hour, not a forced march. One main excerpt per sitting. Name last week’s link and next week’s link in one plain sentence so the week feels connected. Leave the tidy adult story for after the looking.

At ages 15–18, adolescents can treat these texts as human artifacts with makers, audiences, and jobs. They can also fall into year-first quizzes, “Articles were stupid” presentism, a whole-Constitution read-aloud that never lands a claim, or the hope that “the Constitution solves everything.” Your job is to hear those turns and ask a better question:

“What problem does this text name — and what structure does the next link propose?”

If they can paraphrase the problem with a finger on the words, and name what the next link is *trying* to structure — without inventing a quotation or filing a campaign speech as history — the week did its job.

Chapter 1 put the chain on the table as the Hour’s identity. Chapter 2 taught credit-line looking and the honesty card. This chapter walks the first three links as problem → structure. Rights lines wait for Chapter 4. Dual Federalist and Anti-Federalist voices wait for Chapter 5. Context, maps, and timelines deepen place next. Claims grow in Chapter 7. Short checks reuse these objects in Chapter 8.

State frameworks sometimes place Declaration, Articles, and Constitution language in high-school maps as historic documents and influences. California’s history–social science framework and Texas TEKS language about grievances addressed by later founding texts are texture for placement — not kitchen pamphlets and not a statute dump. C3 History for grades 9–12 invites change and continuity (“What changed from the Articles to the Constitution? What continued?”) and long-term causes versus triggering events. Wineburg’s coverage-as-map caution still applies: depth is the hour; coverage is the wall card. Nystrand’s warning about recitation still applies: “What year was the Constitution ratified?” is not the structure move.

Knowledge constrains thinking. A student cannot name a Confederation problem from a clause if she has no tiny picture of thirteen states trying to act together without a strong common purse. Give the piece and a two-minute story. Keep quotations sourced. Use public-domain excerpts. Name which text and which year on the honesty card. Access date for this manual’s research: 13 September 2026.

If you used *History for Young Minds*, you met early civic texts as *edges*, not as a chain Hour. One pointer, then teach problem → structure for 15–18. If you used *History for Homeschooling*, you may have seen a fuller high-school survey. One pointer, then teach looking. This book is not *Publius*. Federalist and Brutus edges live in Chapter 5; point to *Publius* for a Federalist deep week.

Process, not snap: Confederation stresses, drafting, ratification fights, and later amendments did not happen in one afternoon. A chain chronology card helps the family feel duration without turning the Hour into a timeline trivia show. Dates attach after the story is owned — TIME bank detail deepens in Chapter 6; here, use the card lightly so this week’s excerpt has neighbors.

The locked question for this chapter is the whole move in one line. Ask it. Wait. Point at the words.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

**Everyday picture.** Someone says, “We fixed the Articles with the Constitution,” the way someone says, “We fixed the leaky roof with a new roof.” The tidy story skips the looking: What problem did *this* text name? What structure did the *next* text propose? At the table you put the documents back in order — one excerpt up, honesty card first — and you ask what the words claim before you tell the repair story.

**Precise picture.** Problem → structure on this chain means three kitchen objects, one at a time:

| Chain link | Kitchen object this week | Refuse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Declaration | One sentence — a rights claim *or* one grievance cluster — with audience (King / “candid world”) and what work the sentence was doing in 1776 | Whole grievance catalog in one hour; morality-play Founders only; treating the Declaration as a statute |
| Articles / compacts | One Confederation-era weakness *as text* (amendment difficulty, no coercive tax power — you select a short public-domain excerpt; Madison *Vices* excerpt allowed with honesty about which text) or a compact edge as a neighbor, not a Little Thinkers reprint | Mythic “Articles were stupid”; inventing quotations; dumping the full *Vices* list |
| Constitution | One Preamble phrase *or* one short structural idea (separated powers; federalism; enumerated powers as *limit* shapes) — who drafted/compromised; ratification as contested | Article-by-Article dump; “the Constitution solves everything”; Federalist 10/51 as this chapter’s spine |

Fill an honesty card every time: which text, which year, original / excerpt / later printing / modernized spelling. Gloss two hard words before the student drowns. Write one authentic question that is not a year quiz. Name the previous link and the next link in one sentence so the week feels a spine — without crushing three documents into one sitting.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. **Year-first civic quiz.**  
   “What year was the Constitution ratified?” as the main move.  
   *What it usually means:* recitation replaced looking.  
   *What you say:* “What problem does *this* text name — and what structure does the next link propose?”

2. **“Articles were stupid” presentism.**  
   A deficit jab that never returns to a clause.  
   *What it usually means:* tidy adult judgment replaced their-world problem-naming.  
   *What you say:* “What problem does *this* text name? What does it not propose yet?”

3. **Whole Constitution read-aloud as the hour.**  
   Coverage pressure; fatigue; no claim.  
   *What it usually means:* map became the work.  
   *What you say:* stop after one section or one Preamble phrase; put the rest on the wall as a map.

4. **“The Constitution solves everything.”**  
   Destiny story with no limit named.  
   *What it usually means:* hope replaced looking at what one line can settle.  
   *What you say:* “What can’t this one line settle? What continued from the Articles — not only what changed?”

5. **Morality-play Founders only / encyclopedia list of all thirteen disputes / inventing quotations.**  
   Heroes-only romance, coverage dump, or a fake line filed as primary.  
   *What it usually means:* story or invention replaced the piece.  
   *What you say:* honesty card; finger on public-domain words; one problem named from *this* excerpt.

Those are adult fluency targets, not a quiz for the adolescent.

## How to teach it this week

The History Hour shape stays (~30–50 minutes). Today’s piece is a chain link — still source before summary.

**Session shape**

1. **Source / honesty card first (5–10 min).** Which text, who/when/for whom as far as you can tell, original vs excerpt vs later printing vs modernized spelling. Name previous-and-next link in one sentence.
2. **Short look (8–15 min).** Observe words. Reflect what problem this text seems to name. Question. Gloss archaic load as access.
3. **Short background or chain link (5–10 min).** Tiny story the excerpt needs (colonies and king; Confederation stresses; convention and ratification era — two minutes, not a semester). Name what the next link will try to structure if this week is a problem link.
4. **One try (10–18 min).** One CIV item: Declaration sentence soft, Confederation problem clause soft, Preamble phrase as chain open, or Article/section close read — plus, on another day, a chain chronology card.
5. **Exit ticket (2–5 min).** Problem named + one limit; or “This section seems designed to ___ (with starred line)”; or phrase + “In the document this seems to aim at ___.”

**Exact wording you can say**

- “What problem does this text name — and what structure does the next link propose?”
- “Who drafted or approved this, as far as we can tell?”
- “What does this word mean *then*?”
- “What power does this line try to limit or name?”
- “What can’t this one line settle?”
- “What continued from the Articles into the Constitution — not only what changed?”
- “You may revise your paraphrase when we look at the words again.”

**First try-it for the student**

**CIV-07 Confederation Problem Clause Soft** (or **CIV-04 Declaration Sentence Soft** if you are opening the chain earlier). Source the excerpt. Ask what problem the text names. Ask what it does not propose yet. Preview that a later Hour may be a Constitution structure link. Exit: problem named + one limit of this text.

**Fade help**

Early week: you gloss; you model one problem paraphrase with a finger on the line. Midweek: they draft the paraphrase; you ask which words support it. Late week: they fill the honesty card; they name previous-and-next link; they name one thing the line cannot settle. At 17–18, add a light change-and-continuity sticky across two chain pieces on separate days — still not a Government course. Fade the adult speech. Keep the excerpt visible.

**When to stop talking**

After the locked question. After they attempt a problem paraphrase — wait. Do not deliver Article I–VII. Do not rescue with “Articles were stupid.” Do not fill the silence with the ratification year. Stop while a look remains.

**Age-band moves**

**15–16.** One chain link per Hour. Heavy scaffold on dense civic prose. Prefer CIV-04, CIV-07, CIV-01, CIV-03, TIME-02. Oral exit OK. Parent may read one hard paragraph aloud as access. Keep Federalist/Brutus out of this chapter’s main try.

**17–18.** Same one-excerpt rule. Add a second day that pairs a Confederation problem clause with a Constitution structure clause on *related* problem types — without claiming 1787 solved 1776 completely. Ask what continued, not only what changed. Still not Federalist 10/51 as this chapter’s spine. Still not an Article-by-Article dump.

Place by skill, not birthday. A seventeen-year-old stuck on year-quiz recitation does problem/structure looking without apology. A fifteen-year-old ready for an Article I §8 opener should not wait.

**Struggle before rescue.**  
Mushy question → fix toward problem/structure on *this* line. Hard prose → adult reads one sentence as access. Presentism jab → return to maker/audience/purpose and then-words. Hint = question or named idea (“What does this clause say the Confederation *cannot* do?”), not the finished essay.

**Anti-dump rule.**  
One main excerpt this Hour. The chain may hang on the wall as a map of names. The hour works one link.

**What change and continuity sound like (C3 kitchen form).**  
After a Confederation problem Hour and a Constitution structure Hour on related themes, ask: “What changed from this problem language to this structure language? What seems to continue?” Require starred lines from *both* excerpts across two days — not a lecture that answers for them. Long-term stresses (weak common action; amendment difficulty) can sit beside a triggering season (convention and ratification) without collapsing history into one date quiz.

**Parent script when they ask “But who won?”**  
“Today we are naming a problem and a proposed structure from the documents. Winning and losing as a morality play can wait. What do the words claim?”


**Knowledge first; working memory honest.**  
One excerpt on the table. Tiny story. Exit as problem sentence or design sentence. If only thirty minutes, honesty card + Observe + locked question + exit.

**Hypothetical week (illustration only — not a calendar)**

Focus move: Confederation problem → Constitution structure.

- **Mon:** CIV-07 Confederation Problem Clause Soft (problem link) + locked talk box.
- **Tue:** CIV-03 Article / Section Close Read (structure link that answers a related problem type).
- **Wed:** WRITE-style civic paraphrase of Tuesday’s starred line (keep short) + one then-word gloss.
- **Thu:** TIME-02 Chain Chronology Card — place this week’s excerpt; name neighbors. Or OUT-03 plaque read if a civic building is on offer.
- **Fri:** Exit ticket CIV paraphrase + one diagnostic repair (modern policy rant → return to CIV-06 later; for this chapter, return to words and honesty card).

Another week may open with CIV-04, then CIV-01, then CIV-03. Sequence the spine; do not crush it into Monday.

## Practice that actually builds learning

Named try-its below carry **time, materials, safety, the fun, the skill**. Library, courthouse plaque, and kitchen table are the practice — they do not replace the looking move.

### In-home try-its

**CIV-04 Declaration Sentence Soft**  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** One Declaration sentence (rights sentence or one grievance — not all); honesty card; pencil  
- **Safety:** Ordinary paper and pencil; public-domain text only; no invented quotation  
- **The fun:** Finding that one sentence was doing work for a named audience in 1776 — not floating as a slogan  
- **The skill:** Source audience (King / “candid world”); what work was this sentence doing then?; separate then-claim from now-feelings; optional chain note toward Confederation/Constitution later  
- **Exit:** “This sentence was trying to ___ for audience ___.”

**CIV-07 Confederation Problem Clause Soft**  
- **Time:** 30–50 min  
- **Materials:** Short public-domain Confederation-era excerpt (Articles clause *or* Madison *Vices* excerpt — honesty about which); chain label “problem link”  
- **Safety:** Label which text is on the table; only lines from the excerpt count  
- **The fun:** Naming a real stress from the words, without a deficit jab  
- **The skill:** Source; what problem does this text name?; what does it not propose yet?; preview next week’s structure link  
- **Exit:** Problem named + one limit of this text

**CIV-01 Preamble Phrase as Chain Open**  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** U.S. Constitution Preamble; phrase cards; honesty card (1787 draft / later printing)  
- **Safety:** One phrase only; rest of Preamble parked  
- **The fun:** Watching one famous phrase become a document line again  
- **The skill:** Source the Constitution as a whole lightly before phrase work; Observe words; Reflect then-purpose guess; Question; name this as chain open if the week continues into articles  
- **Exit:** Phrase + “In the document this seems to aim at ___.”

**CIV-03 Article / Section Close Read**  
- **Time:** 35–50 min  
- **Materials:** One Constitution article/section excerpt (e.g. Art. I §8 list openers; Art. II short clause; Art. V amendment process openers)  
- **Safety:** Do not read the whole Constitution in one sitting  
- **The fun:** Spotting a list, a condition, or a limit shape in the layout itself  
- **The skill:** Source; Observe structure; Reflect what problem this section answers; Question what it does not settle; mark one enumerated item  
- **Exit:** “This section seems designed to ___ (with starred line).”

**TIME-02 Chain Chronology Card**  
- **Time:** 25–40 min  
- **Materials:** Cards: Confederation problem → Convention → Constitution text → ratification essays → Bill of Rights  
- **Safety:** Sort cards; do not crush the whole chain into one looking try  
- **The fun:** Feeling the spine as neighbors, not as trivia  
- **The skill:** Sort; name which card this week’s excerpt sits on; what came before/after  
- **Exit:** This week’s link named + neighbors

**TIME-01 Date After Story (light, this chapter)**  
- **Time:** 20–35 min  
- **Materials:** Today’s excerpt; date cards  
- **Safety:** No date drill before looking  
- **The fun:** Letting the story earn the date  
- **The skill:** Look and talk first; *then* place dates; ask what the date explains  
- **Exit:** Event + date + “the date matters because ___.”

### Out-of-home try-its

**OUT-03 Courthouse / Civic Building Plaque**  
- **Time:** 20–40 min on site (plus travel)

- **Safety:** Roads; aisle/courthouse courtesy; hands off plaques; no photographing strangers without care; no cold quiz of a stranger’s child; plaque is print about a place, not a sermon about a city  
- **The fun:** Catching a founding idea in stone or brass — then testing it against a document

- **Exit:** Plaque claim + “later telling” note + one question for the document

**Library primary hunt (civic excerpt printout)**  
- **Time:** 30–60 min  
- **Materials:** Library card; honesty card blanks; pencil  
- **Safety:** Aisle courtesy; follow library rules; print only what is allowed  
- **The fun:** Walking out with a named text and a year you can defend  
- **The skill:** Find a public-domain Declaration / Articles / Constitution excerpt; fill honesty card (which text, which year, excerpt?); refuse a modern summary as the primary

### Talk box

**Opening question (locked):**  
“What problem does this text name — and what structure does the next link propose?”

**Follow-ups (3–5):**

1. Observe: “What do you *see* / *read* on this line — before the tidy repair story?”
2. Maker/audience: “Who drafted or approved this, as far as we can tell — and for whom?”
3. Reason from the piece: “How do you know the problem from *this* line?”
4. Check / alternative: “What else could be true? What continued from the Articles — not only what changed? What can’t this one line settle?”
5. Revise: “You may change your mind. Try another line.”

**How to wait.** After you ask, a slow three is a convention, not a sacrament (Stahl 1994 think-time, labelled classroom-origin; Rowe 1972 is science-class wait — keep them separate — not a kitchen history RCT). After they stop, wait again. If they are mid-look, do not cut them off. Look at the piece or the chain cards, not at the adolescent, if the silence is hard.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the question was vague; wait-time was zero; they are hunting a year because you asked year-first; they are guessing what you want; the piece never landed; they offered a summary-only guess; archaic prose needs a scaffold; they are bracing for a culture-war trap or a “Articles were stupid” lecture. Pose, wait, and point back at the sourced line — do not fill the silence with the right year.

### Incorrect example to diagnose

**Incorrect:** Parent opens with “The Articles of Confederation were a disaster, so the Founders wrote the Constitution in 1787,” then reads three Articles and two Constitution sections without an honesty card. Student answers the year. Exit ticket is blank.

**Diagnosis:** Summary first; presentism; coverage dump; year-first exit.

**Repair:** Cover the body until who/when/audience/purpose is said. Put one Confederation clause up. Ask the locked question. Exit: problem named + one limit. Put the Constitution section on a *later* day.

### Blocked / mixed practice

**Blocked (new move):** Three days on one link family — e.g. problem naming only — before you ask for structure language.  
**Mixed (when to use it):** After two solid exits, mix a Declaration sentence day with a Constitution phrase day, still one excerpt each Hour, and ask what problem/structure language transfers.


### More on fading and recovery (same week)

If Monday’s Confederation clause landed as a year quiz, do not “catch up” by dumping the Constitution on Tuesday. Repeat the problem link with a *different* short excerpt from the same era, or the same excerpt with Observe-only for two minutes before any Reflect. Recovery is looking, not coverage.

If Tuesday’s Article/section close read turned into a policy rant from one word, return to CIV structure moves: mark the list or condition on the page; ask what problem *this section* answers; ask what it does not settle. Save modern analogies for a later, named conversation — not for the exit ticket.

If the chain chronology card becomes a memorization drill, flip the order: look at this week’s excerpt first, *then* place the card. Dates and order are tools under a story already owned.

## For the student

History at your age is not a waiting room for an exam binder. It is a named document on the table before the tidy story.

This chapter’s move is simple to say and worth practicing: a founding text often *names a problem*. A later text often *proposes a structure*. Your job is not to memorize every Article. Your job is to put a finger on the words and say what problem *this* text names — and what the next link on the chain is trying to structure.

**Tiny worked example (illustration).**  
Honesty card: Articles of Confederation (public-domain excerpt; later printing; year named). You read one short clause about how hard amendment was, or how limited a common purse could be — whatever line is actually on *your* page. You say: “This text names a problem about ___.” You star the words that support that. You do *not* yet say “so they were stupid.” You ask what a later Constitution link might try to structure. That is enough for one Hour.

**Try 1.** Cover the body of today’s excerpt until you can say who / when / for whom / against what problem, as far as the credit line allows. Then uncover. Paraphrase the problem in your own words. Star the supporting line.

**Try 2.** On a different day, take one Preamble phrase or one short Constitution section. Say what it seems designed to do. Name one thing it does not settle.

**Explain it back.** In two or three sentences, tell a parent: What problem does this text name? What structure does the next link propose? What can’t today’s line settle?

**Challenge.** Place this week’s excerpt on a chain chronology card (Confederation problem → Convention → Constitution text → ratification essays → Bill of Rights). Name the neighbors. Leave a blank rather than inventing a quotation — find a public-domain line later.

You are allowed to wait. You are allowed to change your mind when you look again. You are not required to turn the table into a fight about today.

**What “good enough today” feels like.**  
You pointed at a line. You named a problem or a structure aim in ordinary words. You said one thing the line does not settle. You did not need a perfect essay. You needed a sourced look.

**15–16 tip.** One document. One exit. Ask your parent to gloss two hard words before you drown. Oral paraphrase counts.

**17–18 tip.** On a second day, put last week’s problem paraphrase beside this week’s structure paraphrase. Ask what changed and what continued. Keep both honesty cards visible. Still one main excerpt per Hour.

**Watch-outs you can catch yourself.**  
Year-first answer → return to the locked question. “They were stupid” → ask what problem *they* named in *their* words. Wanting to finish the whole Constitution tonight → put the map on the wall and keep one section on the table.

## If it isn’t clicking

**1. They answer with a year, a slogan, or “Articles were stupid.”**  
*Next move:* Cover the tidy story. Put one line up. Ask the locked question. Require a finger on the words before any judgment sentence. Praise the return to the line, not the year.

**2. Dense prose freezes them.**  
*Next move:* You read one sentence aloud as access. Gloss two words. Ask only Observe for one minute. Then Reflect. Oral exit is enough. Do not add a second document tonight.

**3. They want to “finish the Constitution” or list every Confederation failure.**  
*Next move:* Put the chain on the wall as a *map*. Keep one excerpt on the table. Say out loud: coverage is a map; depth is the hour. Schedule the next link for another day.

**When to slow down.** If honesty cards are empty, if paraphrases never touch the page, or if every answer is a modern rant, stay on one link for another week. Looking is the spine.

**When to go ahead.** If they can name problem + limit from a Confederation clause *and* design + limit from a Constitution section on separate days, move toward rights (Chapter 4) or keep deepening structure with a new section.

**When to get a human tutor.** If decoding load is the wall, or if anxiety about “being wrong about America” blocks every try, a local tutor or co-op teacher who will keep documents on the table can help. A chat window is not the tutor during the attempt.

Correct a wrong reason without crushing the look. Hearing “Articles were stupid,” asking a better question, and naming a better move is teaching. Silence in the face of a deficit jab is not kindness. A scolding lecture is not teaching either.

**Extra diagnostic: they treat the Declaration as a statute.**  
*Next move:* Name audience and year on the honesty card. Ask what work the sentence was doing in 1776. Separate then-claim from now-feelings. Optional sticky: “Later chain links propose structures; this sentence is not Article I.”

**Extra diagnostic: they invent a quotation because the excerpt feels thin.**  
*Next move:* Stop. Return to public-domain words on the table. Write “unknown from this excerpt” on the exit ticket rather than inventing. Looking includes naming what you cannot yet see.

**Extra diagnostic: encyclopedia hour.**  
Parent lectures all thirteen disputes; student never looks.  
*Next move:* Two-minute story maximum. Then the piece. Coverage lives on the wall card.

# Chapter 4 — Rights and limits

![One short amendment text with honesty card and a then-word / now-word sticky; still-life, no faces, no partisan poster.](book/art/ch04-opener.jpg){width=100%}

## Why this matters

Rights texts are documents people wrote after a fight about whether a bill of rights was needed. They have makers, dates, audiences, and jobs. They are not sacred slogans. They are not enemies. They are not campaign speeches. At ages 15–18, the honest kitchen object is one amendment — or one later amendment / interpretation *edge* — with a source card, then-meaning of charged words, a paraphrase of the job the line is trying to do, and a clear stop before the week becomes amendment bingo or a Con Law seminar.

Chapter 3 walked problem → structure on Declaration → Articles/compacts → Constitution. This chapter walks the rights link: Bill of Rights as text, then-words, what a line restrains or protects *as written*, and how the chain kept moving through later amendments and interpretation edges. Dual Federalist and Anti-Federalist voices deepen in Chapter 5 — including the argument *about* a bill of rights — but the rights line on the table this week is still a document, not a jersey.

Adolescents this age can paraphrase a rights line with a finger on the words. They can also expand an amendment into a platform, ignore the words on the page, treat the Court as always finding the one true meaning or always betraying the founding, or hope that one Bill of Rights reading will mint a citizen. Your job is to hear those turns and ask a better question:

“What job is this rights line trying to do — and for whom — as a document, not as a slogan?”

If they can paraphrase the job in then-meaning and name one thing the line does *not* settle, the week did its job.

State frameworks sometimes place amendment process and landmark interpretations in high-school civics maps. California’s history–social science framework language about amendment process and changing interpretations of the Bill of Rights and the Fourteenth Amendment is texture — not a kitchen litigation packet. C3 Civics for grades 9–12 notes that constitutions establish powers, responsibilities, and limits that have changed and are still contested. Teach documents as evidence. Historical inquiry skills can move without overall democratic-disposition transfer — a useful limit from civic-education research, not a reason to skip looking. Access date for this manual’s research: 13 September 2026.

LOC’s primary-source definition still applies to founding and later texts: materials from the time under study, incomplete, to be observed and questioned. An interpretation edge is another document with makers — syllabus headnote, opinion excerpt, or later amendment text — not a licence to litigate today’s hottest case as if it were 1787.

If you used *History for Young Minds*, you met civic lines as gentle edges. One pointer, then teach rights and limits for 15–18. This book is not a Con Law course. This book is not *Publius*. Point once for a Federalist deep week; keep today’s amendment on the table.

Soft entry on hard past still holds. Everyday life, tools, tickets, streets and work, and remembrance with family consent are safer default pieces than atrocity images. When a rights line touches exclusions or limits in the past, return to the words and to what the line can and cannot settle — without turning the Hour into a pamphlet from either pole, and without making trauma the week’s default curriculum.

Wilke and colleagues found that historical inquiry skills can improve without overall gains in democratic skills and dispositions. Keep that limit once: teach looking because history works that way. If citizenship moves are a separate aim, name them separately. Do not make this Hour a virtue machine.

The locked question for this chapter is the whole edge in one line. Ask it. Wait. Point at the words.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

**Everyday picture.** Someone quotes a rights line on a shirt or a meme. It feels true. Nobody can say who wrote it, when, for whom, what the next sentence was, or what the line does *not* settle. A civic meme without a source card is a slogan wearing history’s coat. At the table you put the coat back on the document: honesty card first, then the amendment, then the job.

**Precise picture.** Rights and limits in this chapter are kitchen objects:

| Object | Gentle kitchen move | Refuse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One Bill of Rights amendment | Source; paraphrase; name who/what the text restrains or protects *as written*; one then-word | Amendment bingo; modern slogan without text; expanding into a platform |
| Rights word then/now | Two columns: possible then-sense / common now-sense; evidence from surrounding lines | Dictionary as the only historical method; lecturing the teen for using now-sense |
| Civic text is still a source | Name a misuse (meme, slogan, out-of-context); rewrite as a sourced claim | Cynicism (“all civic talk is fake”); purity lecture; culture-war seminar |
| Bill of Rights argument edge (FED-06) | Short excerpt arguing for or against a bill of rights; compare to amendment text *as ratified* without declaring destiny | Presentism (“they were wrong about everything”); ratification as morality play |
| Later amendment or interpretation edge | Another document with makers; how the chain kept moving | Full Con Law course; “Court always right” / “Court always betrays” as the hour’s thesis; today’s hottest case as if it were 1787 |

Fill an honesty card every time: which text, which year, proposal / ratification era / later printing / modernized spelling. Gloss two hard words. Write one authentic question that is not a slogan quiz. If you use an interpretation edge, treat it as another document with makers — who, when, what kind — before any verdict about today.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. **Amendment bingo.**  
   Ten amendments in five days; fatigue; no looking.  
   *What it usually means:* coverage pressure.  
   *What you say:* stop after one; put the rest on the wall as a map.

2. **Campaign speech / modern slogan without text.**  
   The line becomes a platform for today’s fight; or a paraphrase filed as the document.  
   *What it usually means:* document edge became pamphlet or meme.  
   *What you say:* “Back to maker, audience, purpose. What do the words on the page try to restrain or protect?”

3. **Virtue-machine hope.**  
   “If we read the Bill of Rights, they’ll be good citizens.”  
   *What it usually means:* looking was asked to do disposition work it may not transfer.  
   *What you say:* teach the document as evidence; point to separate civics aims if wanted. Inquiry skills can move without overall disposition transfer — then teach the line anyway.

4. **Court destiny story.**  
   “The Court always correctly finds the one true meaning” or “The Court always betrays the founding” as the hour’s thesis.  
   *What it usually means:* interpretation edge became a sermon.  
   *What you say:* source the later document; ask how it continues or changes the chain — without appointing the adolescent as final judge of today’s hottest case.

5. **Erasing limits/exclusions without looking — or turning the hour into a pamphlet from either pole.**  
   Gotcha without return to the clause; or a culture-war lecture that never opens the text.  
   *What it usually means:* pamphlet replaced the piece.  
   *What you say:* honesty card; then-words; what the line can and cannot settle — soft entry on hard past; no trauma seminar as default week.

**Change and continuity (light).**

Do not collapse the story into “then liberty, now liberty” as a slogan — and do not collapse it into “then oppression, now purity” as a pamphlet either. Looking holds both change and continuity when the pages support them.


**What fluency sounds like for you this week.**  
You can cut any rights packet to one amendment. You hear bingo and stop. You hear a campaign speech and return to the words. You can gloss a then-word without delivering a semester. You can treat an interpretation edge as another document with makers. You can say, in one plain sentence, that looking at a rights line is not a promise of citizenship — then teach the line anyway.

## How to teach it this week

The History Hour shape stays (~30–50 minutes). Today’s piece is a rights link — still source before summary.

5. **Exit ticket (2–5 min).** Paraphrase + then-word; or job + what it does not settle; or writer’s worry + related amendment line.

## Practice that actually builds learning

### In-home try-its

**CIV-02 One Amendment, One Hour**  
- **Time:** 30–50 min  
- **Materials:** One Bill of Rights amendment text; honesty card (proposal / ratification era)  
- **Safety:** Public-domain or clearly attributed text; no invented quotation  
- **The fun:** Watching a famous line become a document again  
- **The skill:** Source; paraphrase; name who/what the text restrains or protects *as written*; one then-word  
- **Exit:** Paraphrase + one then-word note

**CIV-05 Rights Word Then/Now**  
- **Time:** 25–40 min  
- **Materials:** One civic excerpt containing “rights,” “liberty,” “people,” “necessary and proper,” or “militia”; two-column sticky  
- **Safety:** Do not lecture the teen for using now-sense; invite revision  
- **The fun:** Catching a word that shifted without a gotcha  
- **The skill:** Then-sense / now-sense columns; evidence from surrounding lines; wait  
- **Exit:** Then-sense guess with a starred line

**CIV-06 Civic Text Is Still a Source**  
- **Time:** 20–35 min  
- **Materials:** Printed civic excerpt already used this week  
- **Safety:** Keep the repair calm; no purity lecture; no culture-war seminar  
- **The fun:** Catching a meme and putting the coat back on the document  
- **The skill:** List ways people misuse founding lines; rewrite one misuse as a sourced claim  
- **Exit:** One misuse named + one repair (return to source card)

**FED-06 Bill of Rights Argument Edge**  
- **Time:** 30–50 min  
- **Materials:** One short excerpt arguing for or against a bill of rights (Federalist or Anti-Federalist); the amendment already studied  
- **Safety:** Historical stakes; no team jerseys; no modern party labels  
- **The fun:** Hearing that the rights text arrived after an argument  
- **The skill:** Source the argument excerpt; what problem did the writer see?; compare to amendment text *as ratified* without declaring destiny  
- **Exit:** Writer’s worry + one line from the later amendment that relates (or does not)

**CTX-03 Word That Shifted (support)**  
- **Time:** 20–35 min  
- **Materials:** Charged word from this week’s amendment; surrounding lines  
- **Safety:** Soft; no shame for now-sense  
- **The fun:** Lexical detective work tied to *this* page  
- **The skill:** Then-meaning anchored in context lines  
- **Exit:** Word + then-sense + starred support

**CTX-06 Separate Understanding from Approving (17–18; careful 15–16)**  
- **Time:** 20–35 min  
- **Materials:** Today’s rights paraphrase already drafted  
- **Safety:** Soft; no forced confession of values; no pamphlet  
- **The fun:** Noticing that understanding a past line is a different job from approving every later use  
- **The skill:** Say one sentence of understanding tied to the text; optionally name approval as a *separate* question for another conversation  
- **Exit:** “I understand this line to claim ___ (starred). Whether I approve later uses is a different question.”

**TIME-03 Change and Continuity Soft (support week)**  
- **Time:** 25–40 min across two days’ cards  
- **Materials:** Founding amendment card + later amendment or interpretation card  
- **Safety:** One main excerpt per Hour; compare cards without dumping both bodies at once  
- **The fun:** Seeing the chain move without a destiny sermon  
- **The skill:** One change + one continuity with evidence lines  
- **Exit:** One change + one continuity with lines

### Talk box

**Opening question (locked):**  
“What job is this rights line trying to do — and for whom — as a document, not as a slogan?”

**Follow-ups (3–5):**

1. Observe: “What do you *see* / *read* on this line — before the slogan?”
2. Maker/audience: “Who drafted or approved this, as far as we can tell — and for whom?”
3. Reason from the piece: “How do you know the job from *this* wording?”
4. Check / alternative: “What does this line restrain or protect *as written*? What can’t it settle? (17–18) How does a later edge continue or change the chain without a destiny sermon?”
5. Revise: “You may change your mind. Try another line or another then-word guess.”

## Why this matters

Selected Federalist and Anti-Federalist excerpts — Brutus and neighbors — are **dual documents with makers**. They are not a moral victory lap. They are not a monograph dump. They are newspaper and pamphlet arguments written to move audiences in a ratification fight. At ages 15–18, the honest kitchen object is one question on a card, two short paragraphs with honesty cards, and a stop before the week becomes “Federalists were right, end of story,” a team-jersey seminar, or a chapter-by-chapter march through eighty-five essays.

Chapter 3 walked problem → structure. Chapter 4 put rights lines on the table as documents. This chapter adds the aisle: Publius card first; Anti-Federalist voice with pen name / printer / date; same question, two voices; corroboration without a liar hunt. Point to *Publius* for a Federalist deep week — circulation honesty, newspaper-war narrative, full essay sequence live there. This chapter owns excerpt grain. *Publius* owns depth. FED-07 is the explicit try-it that names what this Hour will *not* finish.

Adolescents this age can star a claim in each paragraph and write “On question ___, A stresses ___; B stresses ___.” They can also scorekeep for a team, map voices onto modern parties, treat Publius as a diary, dump all of Federalist No. 10 without sourcing, abandon the Hour for a monograph binge, or feel shame for not “doing all eighty-five.” Your job is to hear those turns and ask a better question:

5. **Exit ticket (2–5 min).** On question ___, A stresses ___; B stresses ___. Or shared concern + divergent remedy. Or deferred question + today’s sourced claim.

**When to stop talking**

After the locked question. After they attempt a stress/fear pair — wait. Do not deliver a victory lap. Do not fill silence with modern party labels. Do not open essay No. 51 “just to finish the thought” unless it is today’s cut excerpt. Stop while a look remains.

## Practice that actually builds learning

### In-home try-its

**FED-01 Publius Card First**  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** One short Federalist paragraph; card: Publius / newspaper or book-first / date / “To the People of the State of New-York” when accurate  
- **Safety:** Public-domain text; no invented diary  
- **The fun:** Watching a famous pen name become a newspaper persona again  
- **The skill:** Source pseudonym and venue before body; what anonymity does; claim in the paragraph  
- **Exit:** Venue + one claim in the paragraph

**FED-02 Anti-Federalist Voice Soft**  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** One short Anti-Federalist excerpt with pen name / printer / date card  
- **Safety:** No caricature; no culture-war ventriloquism  
- **The fun:** Hearing a fear the victory lap usually skips  
- **The skill:** Source; fear or problem named; what this excerpt can answer that Publius alone cannot  
- **Exit:** Fear/problem named + audience guess

**FED-03 Same Question, Two Voices**  
- **Time:** 35–50 min  
- **Materials:** One Federalist paragraph + one Anti-Federalist paragraph; question card  
- **Safety:** Historical stakes; no team jerseys; no modern party labels  
- **The fun:** Holding two smart worries at once  
- **The skill:** Source each; mark answers to the same question; refuse “one is lying”  
- **Exit:** “On question ___, A stresses ___; B stresses ___.”

**CORR-04 Federalist vs Anti-Federalist Soft**  
- **Time:** 35–50 min  
- **Materials:** Pair from FED-03  
- **Safety:** No scorekeeping  
- **The fun:** Finding a shared concern under divergent remedies  
- **The skill:** Same / different / still wonder; shared concern + divergent remedy  
- **Exit:** Shared concern (if any) + divergent remedy

**FED-04 Faction Paragraph Soft (17–18; stretch 15–16)**  
- **Time:** 35–50 min  
- **Materials:** Short excerpt from Federalist No. 10 (or adjacent); glossary sticky  
- **Safety:** Do not dump the whole essay; no culture-war examples  
- **The fun:** Defining faction from *their* words  
- **The skill:** Source; faction-in-text; remedy-in-text *then*  
- **Exit:** Faction-in-text + one proposed remedy-in-text

**FED-05 Circulation Honesty Soft (17–18)**

- **The skill:** If circulation was thin, what questions can we still ask of the *text*?  
- **Exit:** “Text can still teach ___ even if circulation was ___.”

**FED-07 Point to Publius, Return to Hour**  
- **Time:** 15–25 min  
- **Materials:** Sticky: “Deeper Federalist week → *Publius* stream”; today’s excerpt stays on the table  
- **Safety:** No shame; no binge that cancels the exit  
- **The fun:** Permission to go deeper later without failing today  
- **The skill:** Name what this Hour will not finish; choose one follow-up question for a deep week; finish today’s exit on the excerpt  
- **Exit:** One deferred question + today’s sourced claim

**ARG-03 Counter Soft / ARG-06 Audience and Purpose (support)**  
- **Time:** 20–35 min  
- **Materials:** Today’s two-voice notes  
- **Safety:** Soft counter; no prosecution tone  
- **The fun:** Trying on the other voice’s best point  
- **The skill:** Audience/purpose in argument; light counter before claim  
- **Exit:** “The other voice’s best point is ___ because ___.”

### Talk box

**Opening question (locked):**  
“On this one question, what does this Federalist voice stress — and what does this Anti-Federalist voice fear?”

**Follow-ups (3–5):**

1. Observe: “What do you *see* / *read* in each paragraph — before the victory story?”
2. Maker/audience: “Who is Publius as a newspaper persona — and who is this Anti-Federalist voice as a pen name? What audience was each trying to move?”
3. Reason from the piece: “How do you know the stress or fear from *this* line?”
4. Check / alternative: “What matches across the two accounts — and what does each leave out? What would count as success *then*?”
5. Revise: “You may revise without calling either a liar. Try the other side’s best point.”

**How to wait.** After you ask, a slow three is a convention, not a sacrament (Stahl 1994 think-time, labelled classroom-origin; Rowe 1972 is science-class wait). After they stop, wait again. Look at the two cards or the question card, not at the adolescent, if the silence is hard.

**Stuck silence usually means:** vague question card; zero wait; hunting a winner because you asked who won; guessing what you want; piece never landed; bracing for a culture-war trap; archaic prose needs a scaffold; they think history means repeating the parent’s jersey. Pose, wait, point back at both sourced lines — do not fill the silence with a victory lap.

## Context, maps, and timelines at high school

## Why this matters

What would have to be true in *their* world — and where and when does this belong on the chain?

That is not a capitals quiz. It is not a wall of centuries. It is not a scolding sermon about how people in the past were “dumb” or “corrupt.” It is the question that turns background into a tool under a founding document already on the table. A Confederation clause about delay only makes sense if you can feel how long a letter took. A ratification essay about New York only makes sense if you can point at a harbor. A date on the Bill of Rights only helps after the rights line has a job and a maker.

If your family used *History for Young Minds*, you already practiced maps and timelines under owned stories. This chapter ages that habit up. At 15–18 the piece is denser, the chain is named, and “their world” is the interrupt when presentism sits down first. Presentism is the mind at rest — including for able students who can already recite years.^1 A period map is a source with a maker. A modern ratification map is a later telling of votes. Dates attach after the story is owned. Simultaneous elsewhere, route and port constraint, and process-not-snap deepen place without drowning the Hour.

What this idea unlocks is contextualization — space and time under Constitution-chain stories the student already owns. Wineburg’s contextualization heuristic includes both.^2 Classroom work with sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds found sourcing and corroboration easier to move than contextualization; do not pretend one “their world” prompt installs period expertise.^3 That is a useful study pattern, not a promise that every home will see the same result. Then teach.

You do not need to be a geographer. You do need to put one map that belongs to this week’s chain link on the table, ask what would have to be true in *their* world, and add one date only under a story already owned. Hearing a deficit-past jab, an atlas slog with no chain story, or a modern ratification map treated as the whole ratification fight is teaching. Filling the silence with a forty-minute lecture is not.

This week you can learn to script the their-world prompt, cut background to what the piece needs, annotate one place that belongs to this week’s link, and add one date under an owned story. Today the student can answer the their-world prompt out loud once and place this week’s excerpt on the chain chronology card.

Picture two Fridays. In one, a continent wall hangs over the table and no one has pointed at the harbor the Federalist paragraph actually names. In the other, a simple coast map sits under a credited excerpt; one sticky marks “near here”; a chain card names “ratification essays — after Constitution text, before Bill of Rights”; the student says what would have to be true about distance and delay for the argument to make sense. Both rooms look busy. Only one built tools the student can reuse next Tuesday.

This remains a history Hour book. Geography appears as maps under stories. The C3 Framework folds Geography under its inquiry map; this chapter borrows the tool, not a full course.^4 A 2016 parent survey found geography taught that year to 38 percent of homeschooled ninth-through-twelfth-graders, while social science, history, or social studies sat at 81 percent.^5 Quote those as flags, not as minutes. Fold place into **U.S. History**, **World History**, or **Government** honestly. Do not invent a separate continent wall to “catch up.”

## For the parent: understand it yourself

Many adults remember maps as a Friday quiz and timelines as a strip of names they were supposed to keep forever. Rust on ratification order is normal. It is not a character flaw. Five to eight minutes of this section, then the warm-up, is enough for tomorrow. The student still places the sticky and answers the their-world prompt.

**Everyday picture.** You have a short credited excerpt — a Confederation problem clause, a Constitution section, or a ratification paragraph — already sourced this week. The student snaps: “They were just corrupt” or “Why didn’t they just text?” You ask: “What would have to be true in *their* world for this to make sense?” You wait. You point back at the text. Then you put a simple map of the places named on the table, or a chain chronology card. The student marks where and when this belongs on the chain. The map did a job. The date did a job. Neither replaced the piece.

**Precise picture.** *Contextualization* here means reconstructing conditions in *their* world — distance, delay, audience, word meanings, who was and was not at the table — without drowning the excerpt in a lecture and without a deficit-past sneer.^6 A *period map* is a source: someone made it, for a purpose; what it includes and leaves out is part of the look. A *modern ratification map* is a later visualization of votes — useful, labelled as later telling, never the whole story of floor deals and essays read. A *chain chronology card* is a short line of owned Constitution-chain links (Confederation problem → Convention → Constitution text → ratification essays → Bill of Rights), not a generated “complete world timeline.” *Process not snap* means ratification and drafting took months; no one person decided in one heroic afternoon. *Separate understanding from approving* (especially at 17–18) means one sentence that reconstructs reasons and a separate sentence that keeps present judgment — without forcing endorsement and without forbidding any moral language forever.

Wineburg’s familiar-versus-strange warning still holds: even strong students default to now-sense.^1 Lee’s deficit-past caution licenses the repair without a scolding lecture.^6 Willingham’s knowledge point licenses a tiny story before you demand multi-perspective historiography of 1780s finance.^7 This is a useful study pattern, not a promise that every home will see the same result. Then teach.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. *Deficit past.* “They were dumb / primitive / just corrupt.” Repair: the their-world prompt; one condition stated without sneer; point back at the line.

2. *Cynicism as the only repair.* Every founder reduced to a villain, so looking stops. Repair: reconstruct the problem the text names; dual voices when the week needs them (Chapter 5); still source before the sermon.

3. *Individuals-only constitutional change.* “One hero wrote it in a weekend.” Repair: CTX-05 Process Not Snap; TIME-05 Process Duration; name months.

4. *Drowning the piece in a forty-minute lecture.* Background ate the look. Repair: cut to what *this* excerpt needs; two minutes of place and one neighboring chain link.

5. *Atlas slog / modern ratification map as the whole story / map as truth from nowhere.* Coloring with no reason; treating a later vote map as ratification itself; no maker. Repair: honesty card on the map; one pattern the map shows; one question maps cannot answer; annotate one place the piece names.

A sixth, quieter miss: *generated “complete world timeline” as curriculum,* or *date flashcards before any looking.* Three to seven owned chain cards. One new date under a story already looked at. Dates help after the story has a face.

**Five-to-eight-minute parent warm-up**

Do this alone, with tomorrow’s chain piece, before anyone sits down.

Minute 1. Cover any date on the piece. Say the small story in two sentences without the year. Name this week’s chain link (problem / structure / rights / ratification voice / amendment edge).

Minute 2. Write one their-world condition the piece needs (delay, distance, audience in a state assembly, a then-word). Practice asking it aloud.

Minute 3. Find one map that belongs to this week’s link — period map if credited, or a simple modern map of a then-place, or a labelled ratification visualization. Note the maker if known. Honesty: original / reproduction / later telling.

Minute 4. Place one chain chronology card only under a link already owned. If nothing is owned yet, the card waits beside this week’s excerpt.

Minute 5. Write the locked opening on a sticky. Cut any second continent you do not need.

Minute 6. Decide the exit ticket: one their-world condition without sneer, *or* place + reason tied to text, *or* this week’s link named + neighbors.

Minute 7. Practice the wait. Ask the opening aloud. Count three. Do not answer yourself.

Minute 8. Put the pencil down. You are ready. The student answers their-world and places the card.

## How to teach it this week

The History Hour chapter holds the full session shape. Here is the same five beats scaled to context, maps, and timelines. Keep about thirty to fifty minutes. If a day only holds thirty, run the source card, one their-world prompt or one annotation, and the exit ticket.

**1. Source before summary (5–10 min).** Put the piece and the map (or the chain cards) in view. Attribution first: who made the *piece*, and — if it is a period map — who made the *map*. Honesty card: original / excerpt / reproduction / modern map of a then-place / later telling of votes. The student names what they notice about the source and the map’s credit line before anyone tells the tidy story.

**2. Short look at the body (8–15 min).** Read a short excerpt. Observe → Reflect → Question. One historical question the map, the cards, or the their-world prompt can actually speak to: where, when, how long, what else was nearby, what word might mean something different then.

**3. Short background or chain link (5–10 min).** Two minutes of place names and one neighboring chain link already owned. Not a continent lecture. If a date will matter, preview that dates help *after* the story has a face. If presentism appears, run the their-world interrupt here — then return to the piece.

**4. One try (10–18 min).** One named try-it from the CTX, MAP, or TIME list below. One try. Not a packet of blank maps.

**5. Exit ticket (2–5 min).** One their-world condition; or place + reason; or ordered chain cards plus “this week’s link sits between ___ and ___.” Done-enough is right, or wrong-with-a-reason you can use tomorrow — not a percentage.

Exact wording you can say:

- “What would have to be true in *their* world — and where and when does this belong on the chain?”
- “What word might mean something different then?”
- “Who else was in the room — and who was not at the table?”
- “What took years, not one day?”
- “What would a scolding ‘they were dumb / corrupt’ miss?”
- “Who might have made this map — and what can a ratification map *not* answer?”
- “You may revise your first judgment.”
- “You may move one card.”

**First try-it for the student.** After the source card and a short look, ask the locked opening. Wait. The student states one their-world condition without sneer, then places this week’s excerpt on the chain chronology card. That is CTX-01 plus TIME-02. It is enough for the first sitting.

**How to fade help.** First you point at the harbor or the chain neighbor with your finger while you wait. Then you point at the map or the cards and stay quiet. Then you sit back. Stop talking when they are mid-look. Look at the piece, the map, or the cards — not at the adolescent — if the silence is hard.

**When to stop talking.** After you ask. After they stop, wait again. If they are hunting a year because you asked year-first, fix the question. If they offer a summary-only guess or a deficit jab, return to the piece and the their-world prompt. Do not fill the silence with the right year or a lecture.

**Age-band moves**

*15–16.* One their-world condition from the text. One sticky with a reason tied to the piece. Chain chronology card: this week’s link + neighbors. Dates after story. Century as a shelf, not a personality. Oral exit is honest. Scaffold archaic place-names; they still place the sticky.

*17–18.* Add MAP-06 Border Line Soft when a boundary change belongs to an owned unit. CTX-06 Separate Understanding from Approving on a careful excerpt: understanding sentence + separate judgment sentence. MAP-03 with a sharper “what maps cannot answer.” TIME-03 Change and Continuity across two chain links. Still one focus. Still not a continent wall. Still not a historiography seminar.

Place by skill, not birthday. If a seventeen-year-old still treats the atlas as the hour, stay on one sticky and a their-world sentence. If a fifteen-year-old already asks what a ratification map cannot answer, grow that look.

## Practice that actually builds learning

Named try-its below carry **time, materials, safety, the fun, the skill**. Library, museum, courthouse plaque, walking tour, and kitchen table *are* the practice. They do not replace the looking move. Pick one in-home and one out-of-home this week. Coverage itch is the enemy of looking.

Safety is ordinary on every outing: roads; aisle courtesy; museum hands-off rules; archive handling rules; no tasting unknown things; no shoplifting a “test”; no lecture to a stranger’s child; no cold public quiz of strangers for “oral history”; no photographing strangers without care. A plaque is print about a place. It is not a sermon about a city. A civic clause is a document. It is not a culture-war pamphlet.

### In-home try-its — context (CTX)

**CTX-01 Their World Prompt**
- **Time:** 20–35 minutes.
- **Materials:** One excerpt already sourced this week; timer for wait.
- **Safety:** Table only. No live controversy as the “presentism bait.”
- **The fun:** Catching your own now-sense and turning it into a detective question.
- **The skill:** What would have to be true in *their* world — without sneer.
- **Steps:** After a presentist snap, ask the locked half-question. Wait. Point back at the text. One condition aloud.
- **Error pattern:** Presentism lecture; replacing one morality play with another.
- **Exit ticket:** One “their world” condition stated without sneer.

**CTX-02 Technology / Distance Gap Without Deficit**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Map or travel-time note for the 1780s (letters, coaches); excerpt mentioning delay or distance.
- **Safety:** Estimate labelled *estimate*. No outdoor navigation homework.
- **The fun:** Feeling how long news took.
- **The skill:** A decision shaped by delay or distance — without “primitive” sneer.
- **Steps:** How long to send news? What decisions look like under delay? Star a line that names distance, delay, or place.
- **Error pattern:** Deficit past; tech-mockery as history.
- **Exit ticket:** One decision shaped by delay/distance.

**CTX-03 Word That Shifted**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Civic or Federalist excerpt with a charged word (rights, people, union, liberty — keep soft).
- **Safety:** Dictionary as helper, not as gotcha.
- **The fun:** Hearing then-sense from surrounding lines.
- **The skill:** Then-sense versus common now-sense; what changes if we swap.
- **Steps:** Then-sense from surrounding lines; common now-sense; what changes if we swap senses.
- **Error pattern:** Dictionary-only method; shaming now-sense.
- **Exit ticket:** Then-sense + starred supporting line.

**CTX-04 Who Else Was in the Room**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Convention or ratification excerpt that names a few agents.
- **Safety:** Soft entry. No trauma deep-dive without readiness. No forced guilt sermon.
- **The fun:** Noticing absences without turning the hour into a rally.
- **The skill:** Named agents + one absent group we still need sources for.
- **Steps:** After narrative, ask who else was affected / not at the table. Keep soft. Exit names one need for later sources.
- **Error pattern:** Forced guilt sermon; erasing absences without care.
- **Exit ticket:** Named agent + one absent group we still need sources for.

**CTX-05 Process Not Snap**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Timeline cards for a multi-month drafting or ratification slice.
- **Safety:** Table only. Ordinary process — not atrocity sequence.
- **The fun:** Seeing that “an event” was often months.
- **The skill:** What took months; what no one person decided.
- **Steps:** Sort cards. Circle slow cards. Name one process.
- **Error pattern:** Collapse into one heroic day; scolding the learner for loving a clean story.
- **Exit ticket:** One slow process named.

**CTX-06 Separate Understanding from Approving** *(17–18; careful 15–16)*
- **Time:** 20–35 minutes.
- **Materials:** Excerpt with a practice the learner rejects — still a soft founding/civic object, not a live fight.
- **Safety:** Model the split. Do not force approval. Do not forbid all moral language forever.
- **The fun:** Holding two sentences without collapsing them.
- **The skill:** Reconstruct reasons without endorsing; keep judgment separate and honest.
- **Steps:** Adult models one understanding sentence + one present-judgment sentence. Learner writes both.
- **Error pattern:** Forcing approval; forbidding any moral language forever.
- **Exit ticket:** Understanding sentence + separate judgment sentence.

### In-home try-its — maps (MAP)

**MAP-01 Annotate Where**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Blank or outline map; excerpt naming places.
- **Safety:** Table only.
- **The fun:** Planting a flag on a harbor the text actually names.
- **The skill:** Place + reason tied to text.
- **Steps:** Source card first. Mark places named. One annotation explaining why place matters to the question.
- **Error pattern:** Pretty coloring with no reason; modern borders assumed blindly.
- **Exit ticket:** Place + reason tied to text.

**MAP-02 Period Map as Source**
- **Time:** 30–45 minutes.
- **Materials:** Period map reproduction with maker/date if known; honesty card.
- **Safety:** Screen with an adult if online. No generated “old map” filed as a period source.
- **The fun:** Spotting what the old map bothers to draw — and what it skips.
- **The skill:** Source the map; Observe; Reflect purpose; Question; what the map cannot show.
- **Steps:** Attribution first. Look. Purpose guess. One limit.
- **Error pattern:** “Old map = accurate photo.”
- **Exit ticket:** Maker/purpose guess + one limit.

**MAP-03 Ratification Map Soft**
- **Time:** 30–45 minutes.
- **Materials:** Simple state ratification order map (labelled secondary visualization if modern).
- **Safety:** No culture-war color coding. Soft pattern talk only.
- **The fun:** Noticing a pattern — then naming what the map cannot tell.
- **The skill:** Modern map as later telling of votes; pattern + limit.
- **Steps:** Honesty: later telling. What pattern do you notice? What questions does a map *not* answer (floor deals, essays read)?
- **Error pattern:** Treating map as the whole ratification story.
- **Exit ticket:** One pattern + one question maps can’t answer.

**MAP-04 Route / Port Constraint**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Map of a port, river, or road relevant to the excerpt (New York harbor light if the week’s voice is ratification — keep light; point to *Publius* for a deep week).
- **Safety:** Geography as constraint, not destiny lecture.
- **The fun:** Seeing why a place argument had teeth.
- **The skill:** One geographic constraint + starred text line.
- **Steps:** How does geography constrain the political argument in the text? Star the line.
- **Error pattern:** Geography determinism lecture.
- **Exit ticket:** One geographic constraint + starred text line.

**MAP-06 Border Line Soft** *(17–18)*
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Map showing a historical boundary change relevant to an owned unit.
- **Safety:** Soft ordinary consequences. Not a live border fight.
- **The fun:** Noticing a line on paper changed someone’s walk or market.
- **The skill:** What changed; who drew the line; what sources beyond the map we need.
- **Steps:** Source the map. Name the change. Name one needed source type.
- **Error pattern:** Present borders as eternal.
- **Exit ticket:** Change named + one needed source type.

### In-home try-its — time (TIME)

**TIME-01 Date After Story**
- **Time:** 20–35 minutes.
- **Materials:** Excerpt; date cards.
- **Safety:** No shame for a wrong year.
- **The fun:** Story logic first, then the flip.
- **The skill:** Dates attach after the story is owned; dates then help you revise.
- **Steps:** Look and talk first; *then* place dates; ask what the date explains.
- **Error pattern:** Date drill before any looking; birthday lock.
- **Exit ticket:** Event + date + “the date matters because ___.”

**TIME-02 Chain Chronology Card**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Cards: Confederation problem → Convention → Constitution text → ratification essays → Bill of Rights (add amendment edge when owned).
- **Safety:** Table only.
- **The fun:** Parking this week’s excerpt on a chain you can name.
- **The skill:** This week’s link named + neighbors.
- **Steps:** Sort. Name which card this week’s excerpt sits on. What came before/after.
- **Error pattern:** Memorizing order with no documents; crushing chain into one day.
- **Exit ticket:** This week’s link named + neighbors.

**TIME-03 Change and Continuity Soft**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Two excerpts years apart on a related theme (two chain links, or rights line + later amendment edge).
- **Safety:** Soft civic objects.
- **The fun:** Holding “something changed” and “something stayed” at once.
- **The skill:** One change + one continuity with lines.
- **Steps:** What changed? What stayed? Evidence lines.
- **Error pattern:** “Everything changed”; “nothing changed.”
- **Exit ticket:** One change + one continuity with lines.

**TIME-04 Century as Tool**
- **Time:** 15–25 minutes.
- **Materials:** Timeline strip.
- **Safety:** None special.
- **The fun:** Parking documents on a long shelf and noticing how little the shelf says.
- **The skill:** Century placement + one soft co-temporal note — no dump.
- **Steps:** Place this week’s documents in century; one co-temporal note kept soft.
- **Error pattern:** Isolated floating dates; century as personality.
- **Exit ticket:** Century placement + one co-temporal note.

**TIME-05 Process Duration**
- **Time:** 20–35 minutes.
- **Materials:** Start/end dates for a drafting or ratification slice.
- **Safety:** Table only.
- **The fun:** Counting months and feeling debate as duration.
- **The skill:** Duration + one implication for reading essays.
- **Steps:** Count months. What “debate” means when it lasts that long.
- **Error pattern:** Snap-decision myth.
- **Exit ticket:** Duration + one implication for reading essays.

**TIME-06 Memoir Written Later Flag**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Memoir excerpt about an earlier founding event; two dates visible (event vs writing).
- **Safety:** Soft remembrance. Family consent if a family memoir.
- **The fun:** Catching the later pen.
- **The skill:** Useful-for / weak-for.
- **Steps:** Event date vs writing date; useful for / weak for.
- **Error pattern:** Discarding memoirs; treating as same-day diary.
- **Exit ticket:** Useful-for / weak-for.

- **Time:** 30–50 minutes (may include OUT).

- **Safety:** Same as MAP-05. Looking at a building and a public display — not a protest and not a campaign.

### Talk box

**Opening (locked):** “What would have to be true in *their* world — and where and when does this belong on the chain?”

**Follow-ups (use three to five):**

1. Observe: “What do you *see* / *read* — a place name, a delay, a blank on the map, a word that might have shifted?”
2. Maker / audience: “Who made this / for whom / against what problem — and who might have made this map?”
3. Reason tied to the piece: “How do you know from *this* line, not from a guess about now?”
4. Check or alternative: “What else could be true in *their* world? What took years, not one day? What can a ratification map *not* answer?”
5. Revise: “You may revise your first judgment. You may move one card.”

**How to wait.** After you ask, a slow three is a convention, not a sacrament. Classroom wait-time research is mostly science-class in origin (Rowe) and later discussion craft (Stahl); borrow the pause; do not treat those studies as a kitchen history trial.^9 After they stop, wait again. If they are mid-look, do not cut them off. Look at the piece, the two voices, or the map — not at the adolescent — if the silence is hard.

**What a stuck silence usually means.** The question was vague. Wait-time was zero. They are hunting a year because you asked year-first. They are guessing what you want. The piece never landed. They offered a summary-only or deficit guess. They think history means “repeat the parent’s story” or “the review book is the facts.” Archaic prose needs a scaffold before talk. They are bracing for a culture-war trap. Your job is to pose, wait, and point back at the sourced line — not to fill the silence with the right year, and not to turn the table into a fight.

**TALK-03 Their World Round** (talk protocol). Use CTX-01 as talk: one speaker; wait after response before evaluation. Exit: one their-world condition.

### Blocked, then mixed

**Blocked (new move).** One their-world prompt *or* one map annotation *or* one chain card sort. CTX-01, MAP-01, or TIME-02 alone. No second continent. No extra century chant “for enrichment.”

**Mixed (when the move is stable).** Pair MAP-03 with TIME-02. Pair CTX-02 with MAP-04. Pair a plaque outing with a home sticky and a their-world sentence the same week. Pair CTX-05 with TIME-05.

### One incorrect example to diagnose (illustration)

**Labelled illustration, not a reported family.** A parent prints a generated “complete world timeline, 4000 BCE–present,” colors the 1780s, and asks, “What year was the Constitution ratified?” The student chants a year and cannot point at the harbor the Federalist paragraph names, cannot say what would have to be true about distance, and treats a modern red-blue ratification poster as “what happened.” The map is decoration. The year is a performance. The chain is missing.

**Repair.** Put the paragraph down. Cover the year. Ask the locked opening over a simple coast map and the chain cards. One sticky. One their-world condition. Flip the date after the story is said. ## For the student

A map is a tool, not a poster you are supposed to memorize. A timeline is a short line of Constitution-chain stories you already know, not a test of every year. “Their world” is a question that stops you from judging first and looking second. Your job today is to say what would have to be true *then*, to put one mark that belongs to the piece in front of you, and to park this week’s excerpt on the chain.

**What this idea is.** History happened somewhere. It happened in a when. People wrote under delays, distances, audiences, and word meanings that are not yours. The map helps you say *where* without guessing from the street you live on now. The chain timeline helps you say *when* after you already know the story. Some maps are sources — a person made them, for a reason, and left things out. A modern ratification map is a later picture of votes. It is useful. It is not the whole fight.

**Tiny worked example (illustration).** You have a short ratification-era paragraph that names a port city and worries about trade. A simple map of that coast is on the table. You cover the year. You ask: what would have to be true in *their* world? You say: news and goods moved slowly; a city’s port mattered to the argument. You put a sticky near the named harbor because the paragraph names it. You place the card on “ratification essays — after Constitution text, before Bill of Rights.” Then you flip the date. The date helps you say the debate lasted months, not an afternoon. That is enough for one sitting.

**Try 1.** On this week’s piece, answer the their-world prompt out loud once without sneer. Star the line that licensed your condition.

**Try 2.** Place this week’s excerpt on the chain chronology card. Name the neighbor before and the neighbor after. On a map, place one sticky. Label *guess* or *known*. Write “Near ___ because this line names it.”

**Explain it back.** In your own words, tell someone at the table what a their-world question can do that a summary cannot, and what a date can do only after you own the story.

**Challenge.** If you are ready (often 17–18): look at a period map and a modern map of the same place — or a labelled ratification visualization. Name one thing the map can show and one question it cannot answer. Or take a walk with an adult to a local plaque. Copy one sentence. Who put the plaque up, if it says? Compare wording to a founding line if quoted. Park that sentence on the kitchen map later. Write one understanding sentence and one separate judgment sentence if the piece asks for that split.

You may change your mind when you look. You may write “I don’t know yet” on a maker line. You do not have to chant centuries. You do not have to finish a wall of the world. You do not have to pretend a modern vote map is 1788 speaking.

## If it isn’t clicking

Three diagnostics. No shame. A wrong reason is information.

**1. Presentism or the atlas ate the hour.** Symptom: deficit jab, cynicism-only, coloring capitals, or a generated world timeline — and no one has pointed at the piece. Next move: close the extra maps. Run CTX-01 + MAP-01 only. One their-world condition. One sticky. One reason. If archaic place-names are the blocker, scaffold the reading, then return to the looking move. Slow down here. Do not add MAP-06 to “enrich” a stuck Monday.

**2. Year-first freeze or year-first performance.** Symptom: silence while they hunt a year, or a fluent century with no chain story. Next move: cover every date. Run TIME-01 then TIME-02. Order by chain logic. Flip last. If they are guessing what you want, say: “I do not have a year I am waiting for. I want a their-world condition or a chain place you can show.”

**3. Map as truth from nowhere, or ratification map as the whole story.** Symptom: “The map just shows it” with no maker; or a modern vote map treated as ratification itself. Next move: MAP-02 for maker talk, or MAP-03 with pattern + “what maps can’t answer.” Ask one their-world question. Then stop. Do not scold “they weren’t stupid.”

**When to slow down.** One sticky and a their-world sentence are not stable. Archaic prose is the blocker — scaffold access, then return. Living-memory or family-artifact work needs consent you do not yet have — skip TIME-06 family versions until you do.

**When to go ahead.** They state a their-world condition without sneer, place a sticky with a reason from the line, park the excerpt on the chain, and attach a date after the story. Then MAP-02, MAP-03, CTX-05, or TIME-03 is fair. Border-line soft and separate-understanding-from-approving wait until that floor is honest.

**When to get a human tutor.** Place-name or dense civic reading is so heavy that every hour becomes decoding, and a reading teacher would help access. Or a family wants a geography *course* — that is a different title. Point at a Geography year if the work is actually that. This chapter will not become that course.

Hearing a wrong reason, then asking a better question, then naming a better move if needed, is teaching. Silence in the face of a wallpaper map is not kindness. A lecture about every capital is not teaching either.

## Talking and writing civic history

## Why this matters

What do you claim — which named source shows it — and what does this claim not settle?

That is not a timed Advanced Placement essay. It is not a request for a fluent paragraph a machine could have written. It is the question that turns looking at a founding document into a sentence another person can check. A claim without a source is a vibe. A source named with no claim is a caption. A warrant that only restates the claim is a shrug in fancy clothes. A claim with no limit pretends one excerpt finished the fight. History talk at this age is a claim that points. History writing at this age is the same claim, slower, on a dated page — with a warrant and a limit.

If your family used *History for Young Minds*, you already practiced “I claim ___ because [source] shows ___.” This chapter ages that habit up. At 15–16 the exit is a sourced paragraph with warrant and limit. At 17–18 it grows toward multi-paragraph argument and, occasionally, a DBQ-shaped stretch — still not Monday–Friday identity. *English for Emerging Adults* holds sentence craft and literacy argument. *Critical Thinking for Emerging Adults* holds judgment on ordinary present stakes. One pointer each, then teach civic history: a spoken or written claim about a *past* founding piece, with the maker in the sentence.

What this idea unlocks is the high-school writing exit the C3 Framework names in ordinary language: integrate evidence from multiple sources into a reasoned argument.^11 Digital Inquiry Group’s classroom materials put the same spine in teacher language — back up claims with evidence from original documents.^12 Copy the shape. Label the classroom origin. Do not import an AP packet as the weekly spine.

Regular writing alone does not install those features. Monte-Sano and Thomson found that students who wrote regularly still needed instruction aimed at argument and historical thinking — reading, evidence analysis, and claim development taught together, not a stack of summaries hoped into discipline.^13 That is a useful study, not a promise that every home will see the same result. Then teach. Dual-perspective *talk* grows from dialogic practice when prompted; unprompted dual-function argument is not the default even near adulthood.^14 The parent models one claim sentence that names the source, one warrant stem, waits, and asks for a limit and the other voice’s best point before the conclusion.

You do not need to be a historian. You do need to hear a claim with no source, a source with no claim, a warrant that only restates the claim, a chatbot paragraph filed as the student’s, a Federalist victory lap with no second voice, and a disagreement that never points at a line — and to ask the locked question, then wait. Hearing those wrong turns is teaching. Filling the silence with your own perfect paragraph is not.

This week you can learn to model one claim sentence that names the source, one warrant stem (“This evidence supports the claim because ___”), one limit sentence, and a prompt for the other voice’s best point. Today the student can write: “I claim ___ because [source name / maker] shows ___. This evidence counts because ___. This claim does not settle ___.” At 17–18 they add a light “Brutus would say…” or counter sentence when the week holds two voices.

Picture two Fridays. In one, the notebook holds a tidy summary that could have come from any review book — or from a window. In the other, a paragraph names a Constitution section or a Federalist paragraph, points at a starred line, warrants why that line counts, and admits what the claim does not settle. Both pages look like writing. Only one is civic history looking on paper.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

Many adults remember history writing as “five paragraphs about causes,” as a worksheet blank, or as no writing at all — only talk that drifted into a lecture. Rust on essay formulas is normal. It is not a character flaw. Five to eight minutes of this section, then the warm-up, is enough for tomorrow. The student still writes the blanks.

**Everyday picture.** A short credited founding excerpt is on the table. You have already done the source card. You ask: “What do you claim — which named source shows it — and what does this claim not settle?” You wait. The student says, “I claim this Confederation clause names a problem of insufficient power to compel contribution, because Article ___ (maker, date) says ___. This evidence counts because the clause states the failure in the Confederation’s own words. This claim does not settle how the later Constitution fixed it.” They point. If they say “because the Articles were stupid” with no piece, you send them back: “Which named source shows it?” That pointing is the move.

**Precise picture.** A *claim* here is a sentence about the past that could be wrong and that the piece can speak to. *Evidence* is a starred line — something another person can find. A *warrant* ties the evidence to the claim: why *this* line counts for *that* claim (not a restatement of the claim). A *limit* is one honest sentence about what this source cannot tell or what the claim does not settle. At 15–16 the exit is one paragraph: source sentence; claim; evidence; warrant; limit. At 17–18 it is multi-paragraph when ready: intro claim; evidence weave; counter soft; limit — still not a Document-Based Question every week. An occasional DBQ-shaped stretch (three to four short excerpts, outline before draft) may appear when sourced paragraphs with warrant are already stable.^15

Talk is not a luxury before writing. Nystrand’s work on authentic questions — questions where the asker has not already scripted the answer — is the spirit of the talk box.^16 Materials do not discuss themselves; even well-designed document lessons can produce little text-based talk unless someone asks and waits.^17 Accountable Talk, from a professional-development checklist, names three debts: to the people at the table (turn-taking), to knowledge (how do you know?), and to rigorous thinking (a reason, not a vibe).^18 Borrow the three as craft. Do not treat the checklist as a history trial. Stahl’s think-time is a convention for waiting after a question and after an answer; Rowe’s original wait-time work is science class.^19 Borrow the pause. Do not mash those names as a kitchen RCT.

Monte-Sano and De La Paz’s disciplinary-literacy work is the writing neighbor: cognitive apprenticeship (I do / we do / you do); choose evidence that would matter to a skeptic; name who and where the evidence comes from.^20 Copy the shape. Do not paste a classroom coefficient onto the kitchen table.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. *Claim with no source.* “They wanted liberty.” No clause, no maker. Repair: “Which named source shows it?” Star a line or return to the source card.

2. *Source named but no claim.* A perfect attribution paragraph and then “so that’s the document.” Repair: “What do you claim *about the past* from this piece?” One sentence.

3. *Warrant that only restates the claim.* “This supports my claim because my claim is true.” Repair: “Why does *that line* count for *that claim*?” Model one warrant stem.

4. *Chatbot claim / model summary as source / five-paragraph empty ritual.* Fluent encyclopedia voice; summary filed as primary; empty formula with no lines. Repair: close the window; WRITE-06; student writes from the piece in view; tool only after a real draft, on your account, as revision questions — not a rewrite.

5. *Own-side-only fluent paragraph / disagreement that never points at a line / filling silence with the parent’s paragraph.* Federalist victory lap; vibe winner; you narrated the claim. Repair: “What’s the other voice’s best point?”; “Show me the line”; wait; do not rescue the claim.

A sixth: *quote dump* — three copied lines and no sentence of their own. Repair: sentence 1 names writer, date, type; then one claim; then one support; then warrant; then limit.

**Five-to-eight-minute parent warm-up**

Do this alone, with tomorrow’s piece.

Minute 1. Write one model claim sentence that names the source. Put it in your pocket. You will not read it first.

Minute 2. Star the line that would support that claim. Write one warrant stem you could model later. Star one thing the piece cannot tell.

Minute 3. Write one revision question that targets evidence or warrant, not polish: “Where is the claim in your sentence? Point to the evidence. Why does that line count?”

Minute 4. Plan the wait after the locked opening. Count three aloud once, now, so you remember.

Minute 5. Decide the band exit: sourced paragraph with warrant and limit (15–16) or multi-paragraph / counter soft (17–18). Do not assign ARG-05 before a sourced paragraph with warrant is stable.

Minute 6. If two learners will sit, plan one uptake prompt: “Add one idea to what ___ said, tied to a *different* line.”

Minute 7. Cut any second excerpt you do not need for a first claim. One piece is enough for Monday. Two pieces wait for WRITE-04 / ARG-04.

Minute 8. Put the model sentence away. You are ready. They write the blanks.

## How to teach it this week

The History Hour chapter holds the full session shape. Here is the same five beats scaled to talk and a short civic claim. Keep thirty to fifty minutes. If a day only holds thirty, run the source card, one point-to-the-line talk, and a paragraph exit.

**1. Source before summary (5–10 min).** Attribution card first. The student names maker, date, type, audience if known, honesty flag, against-what-problem if the chain week needs it. No claim yet. No summary yet.

**2. Short look at the body (8–15 min).** Observe → Reflect → Question. Adult scaffolds archaic lines if needed. One question the piece can actually answer.

**3. Short background or chain link (5–10 min).** Two minutes of story the piece can carry — previous-and-next chain link named. Then the talk box — not a lecture that writes the claim for them.

**4. One try (10–18 min).** One TALK try-it or one WRITE / ARG try-it. One. A spoken claim can be the whole try on Monday. A written claim–evidence–warrant–limit can be Wednesday.

**5. Exit ticket (2–5 min).** “I claim ___ because [named source] shows ___. This evidence counts because ___. This claim does not settle ___.” At 17–18, add a light counter or “Brutus would say…” when two voices are on the table. Date the page. File under **History**, **U.S. History**, **World History**, **Government**, or **Civics**.

Exact wording you can say:

- “What do you claim — which named source shows it — and what does this claim not settle?”
- “Where is the claim in your sentence?”
- “Point to the evidence in the piece.”
- “What’s the warrant — why does that line count?”
- “What can’t this source tell us?”
- “What’s the other voice’s best point?”
- “Whose turn to add one idea tied to a line?”
- “You may revise the claim without erasing the look.”
- “I do not need a pretty paragraph. I need a named source, a warrant, and a limit.”

**First try-it for the student.** After the source card and a short look, ask the locked opening. Wait. Every claim must point to a starred line. That is TALK-02, Point to the Line, growing into ARG-01, Claim from a Starred Line. If they are new to writing a warrant, let the first sitting stay oral on claim + line; ARG-02 Warrant Sentence can be tomorrow.

**How to fade help.** You model one claim–warrant pair *after* they have tried, or you write yours on a card they see only if they are stuck after a real attempt. Then a frame: “I claim ___ because [name] shows ___. This evidence counts because ___. This claim does not settle ___.” Then the frame goes away. Stop talking when they are mid-sentence. Do not finish it for them.

**When to stop talking.** After you ask. After they answer, wait again before you evaluate. If they are hunting the sentence they think you want, say so: “I do not have a hidden claim. I want one you can show from this piece.” If they jump to a summary, return to the source card. If they file a model summary as the source, run WRITE-06.

**Age-band moves**

*15–16.* Source sentence first. Claim from a starred line. One warrant. One limit. Talk accountable to the text. Oral claim counts as a try; written paragraph is the week’s exit when ready. One Federalist *or* Brutus edge already owned may supply the claim — not both yet unless corroboration is stable.

*17–18.* Two-source paragraph or mini-argument (WRITE-04 / ARG-04). Counter soft. Audience and purpose in argument (ARG-06). Multi-paragraph soft (WRITE-05). Occasional DBQ-shaped stretch (ARG-05) only when paragraphs with warrant are stable — labelled occasional, not weekly identity. Seminar soft at home scale (TALK-05). Still not APUSH as the year’s identity. Still point to *Publius* for a Federalist deep week; keep the Hour on one or two excerpts.

Place by skill, not birthday. If a seventeen-year-old still writes vibes, stay on claim + starred line + warrant. If a fifteen-year-old already warrants and limits cleanly, grow toward two sources.

## Practice that actually builds learning

Named try-its below carry **time, materials, safety, the fun, the skill**. Library, museum, family-permission artifact, and kitchen table *are* the practice. They do not replace the claim-that-points move. Pick one talk try-it and one write/arg try-it this week when you can. Coverage itch is the enemy of looking.

Safety is ordinary: roads; aisle courtesy; museum hands-off; archive rules; family artifacts only with permission; no cold public quiz of strangers; no photographing strangers without care. A civic clause is a document. A Federalist paragraph is a newspaper argument. Neither is a team jersey.

### In-home try-its — talk (TALK)

**TALK-01 Source Before Summary Round**
- **Time:** 15–25 minutes.
- **Materials:** Attribution card only at first.
- **Safety:** Table only.
- **The fun:** Detective work before the body appears.
- **The skill:** Attribution spoken before claim or summary.
- **Steps:** Pose “what do you notice about who made this?”; wait 3–5s; uptake; only then uncover body.
- **Error pattern:** Filling silence; revealing body early.
- **Exit ticket:** One attribution fact spoken.

**TALK-02 Point to the Line**
- **Time:** 20–35 minutes.
- **Materials:** Excerpt; sticky flags.
- **Safety:** Do not interrupt mid-search.
- **The fun:** Making every claim show its homework.
- **The skill:** Claim restated with flagged line.
- **Steps:** After any claim, “show me the line”; wait; if no line, revise claim.
- **Error pattern:** Accepting vibe answers; interrupting mid-search.
- **Exit ticket:** Claim restated with flagged line.

**TALK-04 Add-One Protocol**
- **Time:** 20–35 minutes.
- **Materials:** Shared excerpt; siblings or co-op partners if present.
- **Safety:** No debate-club dunking.
- **The fun:** Building a shared look without a fight.
- **The skill:** Two lines used total.
- **Steps:** Speaker A claim+line; Speaker B adds one idea tied to a *different* line; adult waits.
- **Error pattern:** Dunking; ignoring text.
- **Exit ticket:** Two lines used total.

**TALK-05 Seminar Soft (home-scale)** *(17–18; stretch 15–16)*
- **Time:** 30–45 minutes.
- **Materials:** One stimulating historical question; one/two excerpts; timer.
- **Safety:** No live controversy as fuel. Not a branded franchise.
- **The fun:** A real conversation accountable to lines.
- **The skill:** Opening → claim with text → challenge/add → warrant → closing write.
- **Steps:** Opening question → claim with text evidence → challenge/add → warrant → closing write (2 min).
- **Error pattern:** Opinion-without-text; live controversy; franchise kit as RCT.
- **Exit ticket:** Closing write: claim + line + warrant.

**TALK-06 Stuck Silence Repair**
- **Time:** 10–20 minutes.
- **Materials:** None.
- **Safety:** Kind diagnosis, not sarcasm.
- **The fun:** Fixing the question instead of blaming the silence.
- **The skill:** Restated clear question + one attempt.
- **Steps:** Diagnose: mushy question vs hard prose vs fear of trap; fix question or scaffold one sentence; retry wait.
- **Error pattern:** Longer silence with the same mushy question.
- **Exit ticket:** Restated clear question + one attempt.

**TALK-07 Accountable to the Text Soft**
- **Time:** 20–30 minutes.
- **Materials:** Excerpt; optional “line or revise” sticky.
- **Safety:** Politeness without bullying; evidence without cruelty.
- **The fun:** A house rule that makes talk fair.
- **The skill:** One turn that cited a line.
- **Steps:** Norm: accountable to community, knowledge, rigorous thinking (Accountable Talk spirit — PD document, not history RCT); enforce “line or revise.”
- **Error pattern:** Politeness without evidence; bullying.
- **Exit ticket:** One turn that cited a line.

### In-home try-its — write (WRITE)

**WRITE-01 Source Sentence First**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Excerpt; notebook.
- **Safety:** Table only.
- **The fun:** Opening like a person who looked.
- **The skill:** Opening source sentence + claim.
- **Steps:** Sentence 1 must name maker/date/type; then claim; then evidence.
- **Error pattern:** Summary first; no attribution.
- **Exit ticket:** Opening source sentence + claim.

**WRITE-02 Quote vs Paraphrase Soft**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Excerpt.
- **Safety:** Short quotes only — no page dump.
- **The fun:** Two honest ways to use a line.
- **The skill:** Quote + paraphrase pair labelled.
- **Steps:** One exact quote (short); one paraphrase without copying syntax; label each.
- **Error pattern:** Patchwriting; quote-mining without claim.
- **Exit ticket:** Quote + paraphrase pair labelled.

**WRITE-03 Civic Clause Paraphrase**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** One CIV excerpt (clause, amendment line, preamble phrase).
- **Safety:** Document job, not platform rant.
- **The fun:** Putting then-words into your own mouth carefully.
- **The skill:** Paraphrase + document-job sentence.
- **Steps:** Paraphrase in own words; then “in the document this seems to ___”; then-word note.
- **Error pattern:** Platform rant; ignoring words.
- **Exit ticket:** Paraphrase + document-job sentence.

**WRITE-04 Two-Source Paragraph**
- **Time:** 35–50 minutes.
- **Materials:** Two short excerpts.
- **Safety:** Model → guided → independent. Not AP length on day one.
- **The fun:** Holding two named voices in one paragraph.
- **The skill:** One paragraph with two named sources; warrant; limit.
- **Steps:** Model → guided → independent; claim; A; B; warrant; limit.
- **Error pattern:** Practice alone with no model; AP length on day one.
- **Exit ticket:** One paragraph with two named sources.

**WRITE-05 Multi-Paragraph Soft** *(17–18; stretch 15–16)*
- **Time:** 40–50 minutes (or two days).
- **Materials:** Outline from ARG-04.
- **Safety:** Not five-paragraph empty ritual.
- **The fun:** Building a short argument that still points.
- **The skill:** Multi-paragraph draft + one revision note.
- **Steps:** Intro claim; body with evidence weave; counter soft; limit; revise once for warrants.
- **Error pattern:** Empty ritual; no sources.
- **Exit ticket:** Multi-paragraph draft + one revision note.

**WRITE-06 Model Summary Is Not a Source**
- **Time:** 20–35 minutes.
- **Materials:** Chatbot or textbook summary labelled “later telling” + one primary.
- **Safety:** Label clearly. Do not trick the learner.
- **The fun:** Catching a smooth fake primary.
- **The skill:** “Summary = later telling; primary = ___.”
- **Steps:** Compare; refuse filing the summary as primary; rewrite opening using primary attribution.
- **Error pattern:** Using summary to skip the primary forever.
- **Exit ticket:** “Summary = later telling; primary = ___.”

**WRITE-07 Revision Pass — Evidence Weave**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Prior draft.
- **Safety:** Target evidence, not only spelling.
- **The fun:** Upgrading one weak sentence into a pointed one.
- **The skill:** Before/after of one upgraded sentence.
- **Steps:** Highlight claims without evidence; insert starred lines or cut claims; one warrant upgrade.
- **Error pattern:** Only spelling edit; expanding rant.
- **Exit ticket:** Before/after of one upgraded sentence.

### In-home try-its — argument (ARG)

**ARG-01 Claim from a Starred Line**
- **Time:** 30–45 minutes.
- **Materials:** One excerpt; highlighter.
- **Safety:** Table only.
- **The fun:** Star first, claim second.
- **The skill:** Claim + starred line quoted.
- **Steps:** Star two lines; write one claim the lines can support; scrap claims the lines cannot support.
- **Error pattern:** Claim with no line; line with no claim; rant.
- **Exit ticket:** Claim + starred line quoted.

**ARG-02 Warrant Sentence**
- **Time:** 30–45 minutes.
- **Materials:** Yesterday’s claim+evidence; blank warrant stem (“This evidence supports the claim because ___”).
- **Safety:** None special.
- **The fun:** Saying the “because it counts” out loud.
- **The skill:** Full claim–evidence–warrant trio.
- **Steps:** Model one warrant; learner writes one; parent asks “why does that evidence count?”
- **Error pattern:** Restating the claim as warrant; skipping warrant.
- **Exit ticket:** Full claim–evidence–warrant trio.

**ARG-03 Counter Soft**
- **Time:** 30–45 minutes.
- **Materials:** One opposing short excerpt or a parent-supplied counter-sticky labelled secondary if needed.
- **Safety:** No culture-war venting. Steelman, not caricature.
- **The fun:** Giving the other side its best point — then answering with a source.
- **The skill:** Counter in one sentence + reply with a named source.
- **Steps:** Steelman the counter in one sentence; reply with evidence, not insult.
- **Error pattern:** Caricature; abandoning sources.
- **Exit ticket:** Counter in one sentence + reply with a named source.

**ARG-04 Two-Source Mini-Argument**
- **Time:** 35–50 minutes.
- **Materials:** Two short excerpts; one historical question.
- **Safety:** Half-page, not five-document packet.
- **The fun:** Outline that becomes a short argument.
- **The skill:** Half-page mini-argument with two named sources.
- **Steps:** Outline: claim; evidence from A; evidence from B; warrant; limit.
- **Error pattern:** Five-document AP packet; summary without claim.
- **Exit ticket:** Half-page mini-argument with two named sources.

**ARG-05 DBQ-Shaped Stretch (occasional)** *(17–18)*
- **Time:** 45–50 minutes (or split across two days).
- **Materials:** 3–4 short excerpts max; central question; timer optional.
- **Safety:** Label as *occasional stretch*, not weekly spine. No unscaffolded eight-document dump. No model summary as source.
- **The fun:** A stretch day that still starts with sourcing.
- **The skill:** Outline + one revised paragraph OR full short essay if time.
- **Steps:** Source each briefly; outline before draft; draft short essay; one revision target (evidence weave or warrant).
- **Error pattern:** Making this Monday–Friday identity.
- **Exit ticket:** Outline + revised paragraph or short essay.

**ARG-06 Audience and Purpose in Argument**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Federalist or civic excerpt.
- **Safety:** Rhetoric as craft, not “lying” sermon.
- **The fun:** Asking who the writer was trying to move *then*.
- **The skill:** Audience + then-purpose + one rhetorical move named.
- **Steps:** Who was the writer trying to move?; what would count as success *then*?; how does that shape the rhetoric?
- **Error pattern:** Treating rhetoric as “lying”; modern audience only.
- **Exit ticket:** Audience + then-purpose + one rhetorical move named.

**ARG-07 Limit Your Claim**
- **Time:** 20–35 minutes.
- **Materials:** Learner’s recent claim paragraph.
- **Safety:** Limit is strength, not weakness.
- **The fun:** Ending with honesty.
- **The skill:** Limit sentence added.
- **Steps:** Add one sentence: “This claim does not settle ___ because ___.”
- **Error pattern:** Overclaim from one line; refusing any limit as “weak.”
- **Exit ticket:** Limit sentence added.

### Talk box

**Opening (locked):** “What do you claim — which named source shows it — and what does this claim not settle?”

**Follow-ups (use three to five):**

1. Observe: “What do you *see* / *read* on the page before you claim?”
2. Maker / audience: “Who made this / for whom / against what problem?”
3. Reason tied to the piece: “Point to the evidence. How do you know from *this* line?”
4. Check or alternative: “What’s the warrant — why does that line count? What’s the other voice’s best point? What can’t this source tell us?”
5. Revise: “You may revise the claim without erasing the look. Whose turn to add one idea tied to a line?”

**How to wait.** After you ask, a slow three is a convention, not a sacrament.^19 After they stop, wait again. If they are mid-claim, do not cut them off. Look at the piece or the two voices, not at the adolescent, if the silence is hard.

**What a stuck silence usually means.** The question was vague. Wait-time was zero. They are hunting the sentence they think you want. The piece never landed. They offered a summary-only guess. They think history means “repeat the parent’s story” or “the review book is the facts.” Archaic prose needs a scaffold before talk. They are bracing for a culture-war trap. Your job is to pose, wait, and point back at the sourced line — not to fill the silence with your paragraph, and not to turn the table into a fight.

### Blocked, then mixed

**Blocked (new move).** TALK-02 or ARG-01 alone. One piece. Claim + line. Warrant tomorrow. Limit the day after.

**Mixed (when the move is stable).** Pair WRITE-01 with ARG-02 and ARG-07 in one paragraph. Pair ARG-03 with a Chapter 5 two-voice week. Pair WRITE-04 with TALK-05 at 17–18. Pair library hunt with a claim card the same week.

## For the student

History writing at your age is not “sound smart.” It is a claim another person can check. You name a source. You point at a line. You say why that line counts. You say what your claim does not settle. Talk is the same move out loud.

**What this idea is.** A claim is a sentence about the past that could be wrong. Evidence is a line someone else can find in the piece. A warrant is the bridge: why *that* evidence supports *that* claim. A limit is honesty: one excerpt does not finish every question. Dual voices — when your week holds them — mean you can say the other side’s best point without surrendering your sourced claim.

**Tiny worked example (illustration).** You have a short credited Constitution section already sourced. You write: “According to Article I, Section __ (1787 Constitution text, excerpt), Congress is given power to ___. I claim this section tries to solve a Confederation-era problem of weak common action because the section names a power the earlier compact lacked in practice. This evidence counts because the clause assigns the power in the Constitution’s own words. This claim does not settle how later amendments changed the balance, and it does not settle every ratification fear.” You star the line. That is enough for one sitting.

**Try 1.** Write: “I claim ___ because [source name / maker] shows ___. This evidence counts because ___. This claim does not settle ___.” Star the line.

**Try 2.** Say the same claim out loud to someone at the table. When they ask “how do you know?”, point to the line without looking at your paragraph first. Then check whether your written warrant still matches.

**Explain it back.** In your own words, tell someone the difference between a summary, a claim, a warrant, and a limit.

**Challenge.** If you are ready (often 17–18): add one sentence that steels the other voice’s best point (“Brutus would fear ___ because [named Anti-Federalist line]…”), then reply with your sourced warrant. Or revise a prior draft with WRITE-07 — upgrade evidence weave, not only spelling. Or, when your parent labels an occasional stretch day, outline a short multi-source argument before you draft — still your looking, still not a weekly identity.

You may revise without erasing the look. You may write “I don’t know yet” on a maker line. You do not have to sound like an encyclopedia. You do not have to win a culture-war argument. You do have to point.

## If it isn’t clicking

Three diagnostics. No shame. A wrong reason is information.

**1. Claim with no source — or source with no claim.** Symptom: vibes, or perfect attribution and then nothing about the past. Next move: TALK-02 + ARG-01. Star first. Claim second. One sentence. If archaic prose is the blocker, scaffold one sentence of *access* (read a line aloud). Do not rescue the *claim*.

**2. Warrant missing or restating; limit missing; chatbot smoothness.** Symptom: “because my claim is true”; overclaim from one line; fluent window prose. Next move: ARG-02 with the stem on the table; ARG-07; WRITE-06 if a summary was filed as primary. Close the window during the attempt.

**3. Talk that never points — or parent paragraph fills the silence.** Symptom: combat without lines; seminar that is only opinion; you narrated the answer. Next move: TALK-07 “line or revise”; TALK-06 if the question was mushy; put your model claim back in your pocket and wait.

**When to slow down.** One starred claim is not stable. Warrant is new — stay on ARG-02 before WRITE-04. Dual voices are still Chapter 5 stretch — do not demand ARG-03 every week.

**When to go ahead.** Claim + line + warrant + limit holds after a night. Then WRITE-04, ARG-03, and (at 17–18) WRITE-05 are fair. ARG-05 waits until those are honest.

**When to get a human tutor.** Writing motor work or reading access is the wall — a writing or reading teacher helps *access* while you still keep the history claim as the student’s. Or the family wants an AP course as a *course* — that is an edge (Chapter 8), not this chapter’s weekly identity. This chapter will not become an AP binder dump.

Hearing a wrong reason, then asking a better question, then naming a better move, is teaching. “Never correct the adolescent” is not this book’s rule. A scolding lecture about honesty is not teaching either.

## Tools, including AI

Optional helpers for you. The History Hour chapter already holds the full AI box for ages 15–18. Point back.

You may, on *your* account, after they have tried — or before, for your prep — ask a tool to explain this week’s claim idea from a *named* lesson or credited primary; to make extra claim frames, warrant stems, or SAQ-shaped stems with a **parent key** the student never sees; to give you a SCRIPT of questions to say after they draft; to offer *revision questions* after a real draft (not a rewrite); to hint with a question or a named idea, not a finished essay; or to diagnose a cropped photo of the page. You hear the diagnosis. You talk to the student.

You may not ask the tool to write the student’s paragraph, essay, DBQ, or LEQ, or “fix this to an A.” You may not generate a diary, letter, or speech as if it were a real primary. You may not put a model summary on the table as the source. You may not park an unsupervised chatbot as the only partner — not at 15, not at 18 — when you are co-teacher. You may not detector-grade a claim. Detectors fail often enough that a score is not honesty.^21 Fabricated citations are a parent-side risk; if you asked a tool for a paper on primary sources, open the Library of Congress Teaching with Primary Sources page or the C3 Framework PDF instead.^22 At 17–18, integrity add-ons still hold: no AI-generated AP Test submissions; no AI substantive Common App prose filed as the student’s own — revision questions after *their* draft remain allowed.

At 15–16 you stay in the room. At 17–18 you still co-hold; turning 17 or 18 is not a licence. The student may sit next to you while you use a tool. They may hear revision questions after they have drafted. They may type their own blanks. A model that fills the blanks is still the crutch.

The same off-age caution as in the History Hour: a high-school math trial showed unguarded chat helping practice and hurting the closed-window test.^23 The kitchen rule: the model may prepare you; it may not write the claim when the claim is the lesson.

Paper tools that earn their keep: star pencils; claim / evidence / warrant / limit boxes; dated index cards; a two-source T-chart; the Accountable Three card used once and put away; a “later telling” sticky for model summaries. A voice-to-text accommodation is access if writing motor work is the wall — labelled, you in the room — while they still choose the claim and the line. Audio that replaces looking at today’s piece is a substitute, not an accommodation.

## What “done enough” looks like

Not a perfect paragraph. Not an A. Not a diploma. Not an AP score.

**This week’s checklist (place by skill, not birthday)**

- [ ] The student can say or write a claim that names a source and points at a line.
- [ ] A warrant says why that evidence counts — not a restatement of the claim.
- [ ] A limit sentence names what the claim does not settle.
- [ ] Attribution happened before the claim.
- [ ] One talk try-it and one write/arg try-it happened; one outing or a homebound library-screen equivalent if you can.
- [ ] One revision targeted evidence or warrant, not polish alone.
- [ ] At 17–18 when ready: a light counter or second voice acknowledged without abandoning sources.
- [ ] The page lives in the dated notebook under **History**, **U.S. History**, **World History**, **Government**, or **Civics** — never a brand, never Emerging Adults History I.
- [ ] You heard one wrong turn (no source, no claim, warrant restates, chatbot smoothness, combat, your own paragraph) and asked a better question.

**Band exits**

- *15–16:* “I claim ___ because [source name / maker] shows ___. This evidence counts because ___. This claim does not settle ___.” One paragraph. Oral claim counts as practice; written paragraph is the week’s show.
- *17–18:* Multi-paragraph soft or two-source mini-argument with counter soft and limit. Occasional DBQ-shaped stretch only when that floor is stable. Still not AP as weekly identity.

**Life of the habit (not a ninth chapter).** Revisit next Tuesday: read last week’s claim aloud and ask whether the star and warrant still hold. Mix last week’s two voices if you have them. Name this week’s chain link before the new claim. Stop while a look remains. A path through high-school history looking is the promise. A diploma is not. A percentile is not. An AP score is not the only path that counts.

**When to move on.** The claim–evidence–warrant–limit page holds after a night. They can show the same skill two ways — spoken and written, or two different pieces. If they only repeat your model sentence, the piece was decoration. Rerun with your model in your pocket and their pencil on a fresh line.

A “grade 11 U.S. History workbook” essay prompt is a publisher’s scope, not a legal grade, and not a transcript line. Several short written arguments across a term, saved, are enough for an honest sample. That is not a national minutes study. Chapter 8 turns these exits into kitchen checks you can reuse. Chapter 9 files them where a stranger can read the title.

## Short assessments and progress checks

## Why this matters

What can you show me two ways today — and what still needs practice?

That question is the spirit of kitchen assessment for ages 15–18. Assessment here means a dated notebook, a claim that names a source, two short accounts set beside each other, a short written argument with warrant and limit, an exit ticket, one explained miss, and a done-enough checklist by skill. It does not mean a percentile as a personality. It does not mean NAEP. It does not mean a homemade high-stakes battery. It does not mean “not a history kid.” It does not mean an AP score as the only honest path.

Families need two truths at once. First: every History Hour already assesses. The source card, the starred line, the their-world sticky, the claim–warrant–limit paragraph — those are information. Second: a dedicated Friday rhythm keeps the week honest when hope, coverage itch, or a publisher’s quiz would skip the look. Screening a skill is a teaching signal. It is not an identity. Formal evaluation, when a family seeks it for reading or writing access, is a different column. Kitchen checks decide Tuesday’s piece.

Wineburg reviewed a century of “kids don’t know history” stories built on fact tests. Light tours leave few traces; more encyclopedia hours are the usual panic, and they still leave few traces.^24 This chapter will not panic you into a binder of years. National Assessment scores for U.S. History exist for schools. They are not a sixteen-year-old’s census, and the federal statistical agency has never published a nationally representative *homeschool* U.S. History NAEP score.^25 Passing or missing a state’s reading-and-math sitting does not prove a history year worked. A publisher’s review booklet checks recall inside a scope. It does not license a national percentile.

What this idea unlocks is calm evidence you can hold in a notebook a stranger could read: they can source before the body; they can point to a line; they can say what matches across two accounts; they can write a short argument that names a source, a warrant, and a limit; they can place this week’s excerpt on the Constitution chain. You will know what to reteach Tuesday. You will know when a skill holds after a night. You will know when to grow from a paragraph to a multi-paragraph stretch — by skill, not birthday.

APUSH, AP World History: Modern, AP U.S. Government and Politics, CLEP history and government exams, and dual enrollment sit in this chapter as **labelled edges**. They are useful signals when the student and the kitchen are ready. They are never the default kitchen check and never this book’s weekly identity.^26 A full timed AP DBQ may appear as an occasional stretch for 17–18 when paragraphs with warrant are stable. It is still not Monday–Friday.

You do not need to be a psychometrician. You need a dated notebook, a pencil, three kitchen objects you can rotate, the locked question, and — if an edge is in view — the honesty to label it as an edge.

This week you can learn to run one diagnostic wrong explained and a done-enough checklist on Friday. If an edge is in view, label it as an edge — title the credit **U.S. History** / **World History** / **Government**, and record “AP … exam, score n” as a separate signal. Today the student can show the same skill two ways — oral and written, or two different pieces — and name one thing still in practice.

Picture two Fridays. In one, a printout of a score sits on the fridge and no one can say what the student can *do* with a fresh founding excerpt. In the other, a chart with skill names — source, corroborate, claim+warrant, chain placement — has a date and a note: “holds on a new piece; warrant still shaky; AP practice labelled practice.” Both rooms feel like they measured something. Only one told you what to put on the table next week.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

Many adults remember assessment as a secret test, a red number, and a hallway conversation. Others remember no checks at all — only “we finished the chapter” or “we bought the AP binder.” For a History Hour, assessment is frequent, short, kind, and useful. Five to eight minutes of this section, then the warm-up, is enough for Friday. The student still shows the skill.

**Everyday picture.** Friday, about twenty-five to thirty-five minutes inside a History Hour. You put a *fresh* short founding excerpt on the table — not Monday’s. Source card (5 minutes). One oral claim that points, with warrant (5 minutes). The same claim in a short written paragraph with limit (10 minutes). You ask what still needs practice (2 minutes). You tick two done-enough boxes and write Tuesday’s hold (3 minutes). The student leaves knowing what worked. You leave knowing whether the skill transferred.

**Precise picture — four columns**

| What to teach Tuesday | Recall and listening inside a purchased scope | AP / CLEP / dual enrollment when fit | Compliance or documentation in *that* lane |

| Parent-run; skill-based; screening ≠ identity | Not a national percentile; not a kitchen trial | Never the default weekly spine; CLEP ≠ NCAA core | Not replaced by this book; not a proof the history year “worked” |

Keep the columns separate until they stick.

**Three kitchen objects (rotate; one Friday can hold two if you stay short)**

1. **Source a claim.** Credit line, honesty card, letterhead. Who made this, when, for whom, against what problem — “I don’t know yet” allowed. Then one claim that points. Write it with a warrant.
2. **Corroborate two sources.** Two short texts, or a Federalist and an Anti-Federalist paragraph on one question, or a text and a map. Where they agree, where they differ, what that does to the claim. First move is not “one is lying.”
3. **Short written argument with warrant.** A dated paragraph — at 17–18, toward a page or two — with evidence from the sources, a warrant, and a limit. Not a five-chapter thesis. Not a timed Advanced Placement essay as the weekly check. Occasional DBQ-shaped stretch only when ready.

Add crumbs: daily exit tickets (2–5 minutes); one diagnostic wrong explained each Friday; AS-06 Further Investigation Mini as a retest after a night; a progress chart with skill names, not percentages; chain chronology placement as a fourth crumb when the week’s link matters.

A 2016 parent survey found social science, history, or social studies taught that year to 81 percent of homeschooled ninth-through-twelfth-graders.^27 You are not inventing a subject. You are checking the looking move inside a subject most families already name. There is no national study of daily homeschool history minutes. Do not write one into your notebook. Regular narrative or document work, a map habit most weeks, and several short written arguments across a term are an honest dose.

Virginia lets the parent write the list and show evidence of progress. File the kitchen work under **History**, **U.S. History**, **World History**, **Government**, or **Civics** — Geography or Economics only when the work is actually that. Never a brand. Never Emerging Adults History I.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. *Birthday gate.* “You’re only fifteen, so we don’t do documents” or “You’re seventeen, so you must do AP packets every Friday.” Repair: run the three objects at this week’s grain. Adjust length. Place by skill. Label AP as an edge if you choose it.

2. *Percentile identity / NAEP panic.* “You’re a 40th-percentile history student” or a news story becomes an encyclopedia year. Repair: translate to a skill — sourcing before the body is shaky; corroboration still jumps to a villain; warrant still restates the claim. One piece, one question, one move.

3. *“Not a history kid.”* A miss filed as a type of person. Repair: screening ≠ identity. Shorter sources. Clearer honesty card. One claim only. Retest after a night.

4. *Vendor quiz or AP packet as the only measure.* The pretest booklet or a weekly DBQ stands in for looking. Repair: keep the booklet if it helps recall; keep occasional AP-shaped practice labelled *practice*. Add a sourced claim on a real piece the booklet never saw. Refuse AP as every Friday.

5. *CLEP as a substitute year of U.S. History for NCAA — or Emerging Adults History I as a credit.* Repair: CLEP ≠ NCAA core; title stranger-readable courses; never brand the credit.

A sixth: *no checks — just the next lesson.* Hear abandoned transfer. A skill that only works on Monday’s familiar clause is not done-enough.

Minute 3. Write the repair move in one sentence.

Minute 4. Practice the locked opening aloud.

Minute 5. Choose today’s object: source-a-claim, corroborate, or short argument — or a two-ways pair (oral + written on the same skill). If an edge is in view this term, write one line: “Edge = optional; kitchen = spine.”

Minute 6. Print or sketch a done-enough checklist with *skill names*, not percentages.

Minute 7. Choose a fresh piece. Transfer dies if Friday reuses Monday’s excerpt with the answers still in the air.

Minute 8. Put the pencil down. You are ready. They show you two ways.

## How to teach it this week

Assessment is not a separate subject titled Emerging Adults History I. It lives inside History / U.S. History / World History / Government / Civics as short probes. Friday often carries the longer sweep. Daily exit tickets stay tiny. The History Hour’s five beats still hold. The “one try” *is* the check.

**Band 15–16 weekly rhythm**

- Daily: 2–5 minute exit ticket matched to the sit (sourcing sentence; their-world condition; claim + line; chain placement).
- Mid-week: one WRITE-01 / ARG-02 page *or* one two-account sticky (match / differ / still wonder).
- Friday: one kitchen object on a *fresh* piece; one diagnostic wrong explained; done-enough checklist; note Tuesday’s hold.
- Across a term: several short written arguments with warrant saved. Not a battery every Friday. Not AP as weekly identity.

**Band 17–18 weekly rhythm**

- Daily exit may be a limit sentence, a warrant upgrade, or a two-source note.
- Mid-week: two-source paragraph or counter soft when voices are ready.
- Friday: kitchen object on a fresh piece; optional labelled AP-shaped practice *as practice*; done-enough; Tuesday’s hold.
- Across a term: multi-paragraph soft samples; occasional ARG-05 stretch when paragraphs are stable — still labelled occasional. Optional external edge recorded separately.

**Five beats on a check day**

1. **Source before summary (5–10).** Fresh piece. Attribution + honesty card. No claim yet.
2. **Short look (5–10).** Observe → Reflect → Question enough to attempt the object.
3. **Short background (2–5).** Only what this check needs. Name the chain link if placement is part of the check.
4. **One try = the check (10–20).** Source-a-claim, corroborate, or short argument — or two ways on one skill.
5. **Exit + done-enough (3–5).** What still needs practice? Tick boxes. Date the page.

Exact wording you can say:

- “What can you show me two ways today — and what still needs practice?”
- “Can you source before the body on a fresh piece?”
- “Can you point to a line when asked how you know?”
- “Faced with two voices, what’s your first move?”
- “Faced with a civic clause, do you rant before sourcing?”
- “Can you write a claim that names a source, a warrant, and a limit?”
- “Can you place this week’s excerpt on the Constitution chain?”
- “Screening a skill is not a label about who you are.”

**First try-it for the student.** Show the same skill two ways on a fresh piece: oral claim that points, then the same claim written with warrant and limit. That is enough for the first assessment Friday.

**How to fade help.** First Fridays, you may remind them of the frame after they attempt. Later, the frame stays in the notebook as a look-up, not as something you read aloud first. Stop talking when they are mid-look. Do not nod them into the answer.

**When to stop talking.** After you ask. After they show, wait before you evaluate. If they freeze, run TALK-06: mushy question vs hard prose vs fear of trap. Fix the question or scaffold access. Do not turn Friday into a lecture that writes the claim for them.

## Practice that actually builds learning

Named try-its below carry **time, materials, safety, the fun, the skill**. The check *is* the practice. Pick one kitchen object and one diagnostic this Friday. Out-of-home transfer uses a new object for the same skill.

Safety is ordinary: roads; aisle courtesy; museum hands-off; no cold quiz of strangers; family artifacts only with permission. A check is looking. It is not a gotcha and not a campaign.

### Kitchen objects (in-home)

**Object A — Source a claim (check form)**
- **Time:** 20–35 minutes.
- **Materials:** Fresh credited founding excerpt; honesty card; notebook.
- **Safety:** Fresh piece. No Monday answer key in the air.
- **The fun:** Proving the look transfers.
- **The skill:** Attribution before body; claim + warrant (+ limit at band).
- **Steps:** Cover body → attribution → short look → oral claim that points → written claim–evidence–warrant–limit.
- **Error pattern:** Summary first; review-book voice; no warrant.
- **Exit ticket:** Dated paragraph + “still needs practice: ___.”

**Object B — Corroborate two sources (check form)**
- **Time:** 25–40 minutes.
- **Materials:** Two short excerpts (or text + map); one historical question.
- **Safety:** Soft civic objects. First move is not “one is lying.”
- **The fun:** Same/different/still wonder without a villain hunt.
- **The skill:** Agree / differ / what that does to the claim.
- **Steps:** Source both. Match. Differ. Still wonder. One claim that survives both — or a claim that must shrink.
- **Error pattern:** One-must-be-lying; ignoring the weaker-fit source.
- **Exit ticket:** Match / differ / still wonder + revised claim if needed.

**Object C — Short written argument with warrant (check form)**
- **Time:** 30–45 minutes.
- **Materials:** One or two excerpts already lightly sourced in the sitting; outline sticky.
- **Safety:** Not a timed AP exam unless you have labelled an edge practice day.
- **The fun:** A page another person can check.
- **The skill:** Claim; named sources; warrant; limit; optional counter soft at 17–18.
- **Steps:** Outline → draft → one revision target (warrant or evidence weave).
- **Error pattern:** Five-paragraph empty ritual; chatbot smoothness; no limit.
- **Exit ticket:** Dated argument + revision note.

**Object D — Chain placement crumb**
- **Time:** 10–15 minutes (add-on).
- **Materials:** Chain chronology cards.
- **Safety:** None special.
- **The fun:** Parking the fresh piece on the chain.
- **The skill:** This excerpt sits on ___ between ___ and ___.
- **Steps:** Sort; place; name neighbors.
- **Error pattern:** Memorized order with no document.
- **Exit ticket:** Link + neighbors.

### Talk box

**Opening (locked):** “What can you show me two ways today — and what still needs practice?”

**Follow-ups (use three to five):**

1. Observe: “What do you *see* / *read* on this fresh piece before you claim?”
2. Maker / audience: “Who made this, when, and for whom — honesty card?”
3. Reason tied to the piece: “Point to the line. How do you know from *this*?”
4. Check or alternative: “Faced with two voices, what’s your first move? Can you warrant and limit? Where does this sit on the chain?”
5. Revise: “You may change what still needs practice after you show the second way.”

**How to wait.** After you ask, a slow three is a convention, not a sacrament.^33 After they stop, wait again. If they are mid-look or mid-claim, do not cut them off. Look at the piece, not at the adolescent, if the silence is hard.

**What a stuck silence usually means.** The question was vague. Wait-time was zero. They think Friday is a trap. The fresh piece never landed. They are hunting the answer from Monday. They think assessment means a percentile. Archaic prose needs a scaffold before the check. Your job is to pose, wait, and point back at the sourced line — not to fill the silence with the score story, and not to turn the table into a fight.

**Blocked (new check habit).** One object. Two ways. One diagnostic. Done-enough. Stop.

**Mixed (when Friday is fluent).** Object A + chain crumb. Or Object B after a Chapter 5 two-voice week. Or Object C after Chapter 7 paragraphs are stable. Edge practice on a *different* day, labelled.

## For the student

Assessment in this house is not a secret about whether you are “a history person.” It is a chance to show a skill two ways and to name what still needs practice. Fresh piece. Your eyes. Your pencil. Your voice.

**What this idea is.** Done-enough means you can show the same skill two ways and it still works after a night. Source a claim. Set two sources beside each other without rushing to “one is lying.” Write a short argument that names a source, says why the evidence counts, and admits what the claim does not settle. Place a founding excerpt on the Constitution chain. Optional AP, CLEP, or college courses are edges — useful when you choose them — not the only way to prove you looked.

**Tiny worked example (illustration).** Friday. A short credited Bill of Rights amendment is on the table — not Monday’s. You cover the body, say who made it and that it is an excerpt, look, then say: “I claim this line tries to restrain ___ for ___ because the amendment text says ___. This evidence counts because the restraint is in the document’s own words. This claim does not settle later court readings.” You write the same thing. You place the card on “Bill of Rights — after ratification essays.” You say warrant still needs practice on a two-source day. That is a good Friday.

**Try 1.** On a fresh piece, show source-a-claim two ways: oral, then written with warrant and limit.

**Try 2.** On another day, show corroboration: two short sources, match / differ / still wonder, then one revised claim if needed.

**Explain it back.** Tell someone at the table what “showable two ways, holds after a night” means — and what a percentile is *not*.

**Challenge.** If you are ready (often 17–18): write a multi-paragraph soft argument from two named sources with a counter soft and a limit. Or sit an occasional DBQ-shaped stretch your parent has labelled as stretch — still your outline, still your looking. Or prepare for an optional AP or CLEP sitting as an *edge*, while your transcript still says U.S. History / World History / Government for the year’s work.

You may name what still needs practice without shame. You may write “I don’t know yet” on a maker line. You do not have to be a percentile. You do not have to treat an AP score as your only honest path.

## If it isn’t clicking

Three diagnostics. No shame. A wrong reason is information.

**1. Transfer failure — works on Monday’s piece only.** Symptom: fluent on the familiar excerpt; freeze on a fresh one. Next move: shorter fresh piece; same object; after-a-night crumb. Keep the frame visible *after* the attempt, not before.

**2. Identity spiral or edge confusion.** Symptom: “I’m bad at history”; or AP/CLEP panic as the only measure; or CLEP filed as NCAA U.S. History. Next move: translate to a skill note on the chart; return to kitchen objects; label edges as edges; open the Toolkit if athletics are in view.

**3. Check became a ceremony or a packet dump.** Symptom: Friday lasts two hours; or every Friday is an eight-document dump; or no checks at all. Next move: one object, twenty-five minutes, done-enough. Blocked before mixed. Put the binder away until the kitchen spine holds.

**When to slow down.** Source-a-claim two ways is not stable. Stay there. Do not add ARG-05 or an AP practice test to “motivate.”

**When to go ahead.** The three objects work on fresh pieces and hold after a night. Then lengthen the argument. Then, if the student chooses, label an edge.

**When to get a human tutor.** Reading or writing access is the wall — seek help for access while keeping history looking as the student’s. Or the family wants a formal evaluation for documentation — that is a different column from kitchen checks. Or AP Course Audit / coordinator questions need a school’s AP coordinator, not this book inventing a rule.

Hearing a wrong reason, then asking a better question, then naming a better move, is teaching. Silence in the face of a percentile story is not kindness. A scolding lecture about effort is not teaching either.

## Records

The history *work* can be as ordinary as a library survey chapter and as serious as any classroom hour. The *credit* is a family claim until a stranger can use it — a college admissions reader, an NCAA Eligibility Center staffer, a dual-enrollment office, a receiving counselor, or the high-school transcript you will someday sign. ## What you are making

You are making a **transcript line** plus a **dated notebook** (or course folder) of **History**, **U.S. History**, **World History**, **Government**, or **Civics**, with the task named, plus a **sourced claim**, plus a **two-source corroboration**, plus a **short written argument**, plus optional publisher review when you use a purchased program, plus optional external edges when they fit. You are not making a national diploma. You are not making Emerging Adults History I.

When a stranger asks, print those ordinary titles — and **Geography** or **Economics** only when the year’s work was that. Never a brand. Never Exploring America. Never OpenStax. Never McGraw Hill. Never Reading Like a Historian. Never All American History Volume II. Never DIG. Never **Emerging Adults History I**. The dated notebook and the kitchen checks live *inside* those titles.

There is no federal homeschool diploma and no national history credit a registrar already files.^35 Texas, North Carolina, and New York do not issue one. Pennsylvania’s supervisor or approved-organization diploma is a Pennsylvania high-school object. Virginia’s list of subjects is a list, not a transcript. A parent-issued diploma can be a real piece of paper. It is not a registrar’s national history credit.

Three readers, if anyone outside the kitchen asks. (1) The **state**, in a thick jurisdiction: IHIP, affidavit, portfolio, evidence of progress — with history samples where the statute named history. (2) A **college admissions** office: transcript, counselor letter (often the parent), optional scores. (3) The **NCAA Eligibility Center**, if athletics are in view: Toolkit packet, Core-Course Worksheets for social science, proof of graduation. State compliance is not a college transcript. Neither fact is a reason to skip the past — or to fake a credit.

A family that invents “Emerging Adults History I, 1.0, A” for a sixteen-year-old has created a slogan. A receiving counselor cannot map it. NCAA will not file it.

As of the 2016 Parent and Family Involvement subject tables, among grades 9–12-equivalent homeschoolers, social science, history, or social studies was taught *that year* to **81 percent**; geography to **38 percent**. Subjects *ever* taught among 9–12: history/social studies **92 percent**; geography **72 percent**.^36 Those are the high-school rows. They are not a curriculum preference poll and not a mandate to finish an AP year. Informal learning does not arrive as “U.S. History, 1.0, B+.” Name the task, date it, keep a sample, grade on a stated scale if a credit is claimed.

## Titles a stranger can read

Print these and only these as high-school names, if a name is needed at all:

**History.** **U.S. History** / **American History** (only when the year’s work is that). **World History** (only when the year’s work is that). **Government** / **U.S. Government** / **Civics** / **Participation in Government** (only when the work is actually that). **Geography** or **Economics** (only when the work is actually that). English / Literature / Composition. Mathematics. Science. Art, Music, Physical Education.

Those names travel. The NCAA sample homeschool transcript uses ordinary titles in the same family: Geography, World History, American History, Government, Western Civilization, Economics. The Core-Course Worksheet says the course name on the worksheet should be the same as the title on the transcript.^37 **American History** on that sample is the same object as **U.S. History** on this list. Pick one title and stay with it.

Never a brand. Never Emerging Adults History I. The brand, if used at all, sits in a parenthetical: “U.S. History (Exploring America; American Voices documents; two looking lessons; museum visit notes; graded essays).” Combining does not mint a new title.

A publisher’s “grade 11 U.S. History workbook” is a scope, not a legal grade. A year that mixed history, English, and Bible still splits: **U.S. History** or **World History** or **Government**; English for literature and writing; a Bible or religion line if the family wants one.

Here is an illustration, labelled as such, not a reported family:

> 13 September 2026. U.S. History. Sources: a short excerpt from a 1765 newspaper and a later textbook paragraph on the Stamp Act. Task: source a claim; corroborate two sources; write a one-to-two-page argument with warrant. Student: named the printer on source A; noted source B was written after the war; essay arguing the tax fight was also about who got to consent. Grade recorded on transcript scale.

That is a record a parent, a Pennsylvania evaluator, a New York quarterly narrative, a college reader, or an NCAA worksheet can actually use. It is not NAEP.

## The law is a pattern, not one form

U.S. homeschooling is state law. There is no federal history office. Read your current department page and the statute it cites. Five official-page examples show the range. **None of them requires “Emerging Adults History I.”** History / social studies is sometimes named, sometimes left to the parent’s list, and sometimes neither named nor tested.^38 This chapter is not legal advice.

**Texas.** The Agency does not regulate home-school programs. *Leeper*, restated on TEA’s Alternative Schooling page, requires a bona fide written curriculum: reading, spelling, grammar, math, and a course in **good citizenship**. History is not named as a word. Good citizenship is not a Government credit. The state does not award a homeschool diploma. For colleges and NCAA, the parent builds a transcript with stranger-readable titles. Transfer is treated like an unaccredited private school.

**North Carolina.** Notice of Intent. Nine calendar months. Annual nationally standardized test in **English grammar, reading, spelling, and mathematics** — not history. The home school, not the State, issues any diploma. A five-hour day is a recommendation, not law. Do not name the homeschool after a curriculum brand. Career and College Promise on the DNPE page is **North Carolina** dual enrollment with pathway gates — state-variant, not a national right.

**Pennsylvania.** Secondary **shall include** geography and **social studies, to include civics, world history, history of the United States and Pennsylvania**. Graduation social studies is **three years**. Put the work under World History, U.S. History, Civics, or Geography as it matches. **990** hours secondary are whole-program hours. A supervisor (or approved-organization) diploma is Pennsylvania’s high-school object.

**Virginia.** Notice, a parent-written **list of subjects**, and evidence of progress (fourth stanine, or SAT/ACT/PSAT equivalent, or evaluation / college transcript). History is not named as required; the parent writes the list. **U.S. History**, **World History**, **Government**, or **Civics** is the ordinary word to write.

**New York.** Letter of intent, IHIP, quarterly reports, annual assessment. Grades **9–12** require **social studies (four units)** including American history, participation in government, and economics. A unit is 6,480 minutes. **990** hours for grades 7–12. A brand name is a **material**, not a required unit title. Home instruction does not yield a local or Regents diploma. For NCAA proof of graduation, the Toolkit notes New York (and Hawaii) paths that are not a local diploma — open the Toolkit.

If you live in none of those states, open your department page.

## What a short check is

Five objects, not a battery — and a sixth when an edge fits.

1. **A dated notebook.** Date, task named, what the student read or saw, one sentence of what happened — under **History**, **U.S. History**, **World History**, **Government**, or **Civics** if a title is needed.

2. **Source a claim.** Who made this, when, and for whom — from the credit line — and against what problem when the week is a founding excerpt. Compare the claim to the piece on the table.

3. **Corroborate two sources.** Say where they agree, where they differ, and what that does to the claim. Refuse “one must be lying” as the first move. On a Federalist week, two voices on one question is the same habit.

4. **A short written argument.** Claim + named source + warrant + limit. A paragraph at 15–16; toward multi-paragraph or an occasional DBQ-shaped stretch at 17–18 when fit. Revised once when you can. Kept in the notebook.

5. **Optional publisher review** when you use a purchased program — to check reading and recall inside a scope. IES does not certify them as randomised trials. They do not license a national percentile. They generally do not satisfy North Carolina’s annual battery by themselves.

6. **Optional external edge** — an AP score, a CLEP score, a dual-enrollment grade — labelled as an edge. Edges sit on top of the kitchen spine. They are not the spine.

**Screening is not an identity label.** A miss on corroboration is a teaching signal, not “this adolescent is not a historian.” Reteach with shorter excerpts or one claim only.

Bands: **15–16** — survey with scaffolds; source a claim; two manageable sources; a short essay. **17–18** — more independent pace; multi-source essays; optional AP / CLEP / dual-enrollment edge when fit.

## What a short check is not

NAEP. A percentile promise. Homeschooling was 3.4 percent of K–12 students in 2022–23 (~1.765 million); the 9th–12th cell was **3.1 percent**. Instruction at home at 5.2 percent — and 6.6 percent in the high-school band — is a different bucket.^39 There is no nationally representative homeschool NAEP U.S. History score. There is no opened national study of daily homeschool history minutes.

SAT Subject Tests were discontinued — last international sittings June 2021.^40 One sentence. Stop.

## Edges: AP, CLEP, and dual enrollment

**AP United States History**, **AP World History: Modern**, **AP United States Government and Politics**, **CLEP** history and government exams, and **dual enrollment** are edges — useful when the student and the kitchen are ready. They are not the default year and not a requirement of this book.^41

Safer transcript practice when an AP exam is sat: title the credit **U.S. History**, **World History**, or **Government**, and record “AP … exam, score n” as a separate signal. Homeschoolers typically need a participating school’s AP coordinator. Confirm fees and local costs with that coordinator. Credit is institution-specific.

CLEP may become college credit. It is **not** NCAA core. Audited, CLEP, and credit-by-exam courses are not NCAA-approved core courses. Each college sets its own policy.

Dual enrollment is a college course taken during high school. Access is local — North Carolina’s Career and College Promise has pathway gates; New York generally keeps home-instructed students out of the district program; Virginia will accept a college transcript as one form of evidence of progress. Map the course to **U.S. History**, **World History**, or **Government**, with the college named. NCAA wants dual-enrollment coursework designated on the homeschool transcript with college name and location, plus a separate official college transcript.

A student who only “CLEPs out” without course lines may look thin to admissions — and NCAA will not count the CLEP as core. Edges sit on top. The kitchen method is still the piece on the table, the unaided claim, and the dated notebook.

## NCAA, if athletics are in view

If Division I or Division II sports might be in the picture, social-science planning starts in ninth grade. **Social science is a core area.**^42

Division I asks for **sixteen** NCAA-approved core-course credits from the start of ninth grade, including **two years of social science**. Ten of the sixteen, including seven in English, math, or science, must be finished before the seventh semester — the 10/7 rule. Minimum core-course GPA is **2.3**. Proof of graduation is required. If Division II is in view, open the current Division II sheet for that year’s year-count.

The homeschool packet includes a transcript (with a ninth-grade start date and an administrator signature), an administrator statement that instruction followed *state* law, a Core-Course Worksheet for each core course, and proof of graduation. Evaluation begins after those documents are in **and** an NCAA school has placed the student on an Institutional Request List. CLEP and credit-by-exam do not count as core.

Hawaii and New York have a graduation-proof rule: the Eligibility Center cannot accept proof of graduation from a diploma issued by a homeschool in those states. A district or state letter, or a state-recognized equivalency exam after the class graduation date, is the toolkit’s stated path.

Point to the current NCAA Home School Toolkit and Core-Course Worksheet. Do not invent rules from blogs. If athletics are not in view, the same titles still serve colleges and transfers. The honesty is the point either way.

## Library of Congress Teaching with Primary Sources — looking neighbor

Primary sources are the raw materials of history — original documents and objects created at the time under study; secondary sources retell, analyze, or interpret at a distance — as the Library describes it. Observe the source; think about the response; ask about the creator’s purpose; ask whether the source agrees with other sources; demonstrate learning by writing an essay, delivering a speech, or creating a museum display. Primary Source Analysis Tool and teacher’s guides are named.^50

**Fit.** Parent load: low if one short source and the analysis tool; high if a full set is treated as a year. **Looking neighbor**, not a curriculum franchise. Pairs with DIG or with a naked library hour. A Constitution-chain excerpt or a short speech can be the day’s source when the student can name who made it.

**Transcript.** Materials under History / U.S. History / Government: “Library of Congress primary-source analysis.” Never “LOC TPS” as the credit.

Founders Online (National Archives) collects papers of the Founding generation as searchable public texts. A Constitution week, a Federalist week, a letter with an attribution line: those sit here at no cost. Public-domain is a copyright status, not a quality stamp. Check that the text in front of you is the document you meant to assign. A generated “Madison diary” is fiction with a costume. A credited Founders Online letter is a source.

## Combining honestly

Need: $0 start → library + museum.  
Need: narration-to-argument habit without a brand → living books + written argument.  
Need: Christian independent survey with assigned documents → Notgrass Exploring America / World / Government.  
Need: secular school-shaped survey → McGraw Hill or a used basal.  
Need: free college-density survey → OpenStax *U.S. History*.  
Need: Christian two-year U.S. already in house → All American History Volume II (Volume I as the earlier half).  
Need: looking move with a document → DIG lesson adapted, or LOC TPS analysis tool.  
Need: outside teacher of record → dual-enrollment History / Government when gates allow.  
Need: Federalist deep week → *Publius* once; keep the Hour on one or two excerpts.

A family may combine. Common honest combinations: library shelf + Notgrass or OpenStax spine; one DIG-style or LOC two-source lesson after a museum label; dual enrollment as a senior Government or U.S. History I release valve. Combining does not authorize a franken-course title. The transcript still says **U.S. History**, **World History**, **Government**, or **Civics**. The description may say “narrative spine X; looking lessons adapted from primary-source sets; Constitution as an object.” A stranger can file that.

Middle-grades *Story of the World*, History Quest, Beautiful Feet 4–6, and Mystery of History volumes belong to *History for Young Minds* — one pointer, then stop. Do not file *Story of the World* Volume 4 as the default high-school World History year unless the actual work matches a high-school survey — which that publisher’s own band does not claim as AP World. Grades 1–12 survey handbook length belongs to *History for Homeschooling*. One pointer; then stop.

A student who cannot yet source a claim is not “behind in DIG”; they are still in a scaffolded survey year. A looking-heavy path that assumes dense primary fluency will not teach survey reading to a fifteen-year-old still consolidating stamina. That is a fit warning, not a slur.

Put the kitchen checks from Chapters 8 and 9 next to whatever spine you choose: date the work; source a claim; corroborate two sources; write a short argument. The program supplies the story. The notebook supplies the stranger-readable line.

## What “done enough” looks like

You can say, in one sentence, why this year’s history stack fits *this* student: parent load you can actually carry, a spine the student can follow, a religious or secular frame the house chose on purpose, a high-school credit shape only where the work is a high-school survey, library or museum $0 first when money is tight, and placement by skill.

The programs have names. The transcript has titles a stranger can read. The two lists are not the same list. Fit chooses. Ranking does not.
