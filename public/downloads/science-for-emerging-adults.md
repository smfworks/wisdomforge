# Welcome

This book is for you and the fifteen- to eighteen-year-old at your table.

You do not need to be a scientist. You do need to put a system on the table with a blank notebook, ask what they measured, and wait for an answer that points at that notebook. That is the work. The rest of these pages teach the moves.

## Why this book exists

At fifteen through eighteen, science can mean looking at a named system — a jar garden, a body subsystem, a reaction you can stop and record, a change of motion — and leaving the hour with a claim that points at evidence from *this* trial. The claim gets denser than it was at eleven to fourteen: a warrant that names the idea, a limitation or an alternative explanation, a notebook a stranger could almost reconstruct without watching.

Many adults feel rusty. The science they remember was a vocabulary list, a laminated “scientific method” poster, and a year that never left the textbook. That is ordinary. It is not a character flaw. This book makes you fluent enough in today’s looking move to hear a force-as-fuel jab, a cells-as-tiny-mouths claim, a confounded “proof,” neat invented rows, a generated gel filed as the student’s result, a model summary sitting on the table as the observation, a demo with no student’s record, an isolated lab-day with no talk, a coverage lecture with no system on the table, a kitchen picture sold as Chemistry credit, a chatbot CER with no trial, method-poster theater, and AP-as-weekly-spine — and to ask a good question without grabbing the answer.

The student is a person, not a percentile. At fifteen and sixteen they can draw a system boundary with help, keep a stranger-readable notebook, redesign a confounded fair test, write denser CER with an explicit warrant and one limitation or alternative, and use Biology or Chemistry or Physics as this week’s vehicle. At seventeen and eighteen they can revise a systems model against data, integrate evidence across linked trials, name alternative explanations *and* limitations, use denser precision talk, sit an occasional FRQ-shaped stretch or an AP or CLEP edge that remains their own work, and plan a lab-path neighbor when the try needs real glassware. Place by skill, not birthday.

## What a good Science Hour looks like

Thirty to fifty minutes. Five beats you will meet again in the next section: open with a system or question on the table (and a RAMP check if heat, chemicals, glass, living specimens, or outdoor water are coming); a short look or prior-idea card without dumping the tidy answer yet; one try — measure and record; a talk box and denser CER; a short exit ticket. The student looks. You ask one authentic question and wait. Biology, Chemistry, or Physics is the vehicle for the week, not an encyclopedia year. Coverage is a map. Depth is the hour. AP is an edge, not the spine.

The work lives in the home and outside it. A system boundary on a jar. A stranger-readable lab notebook. A fair-test redesign sticky. A denser CER three-box with a warrant line. A museum one-exhibit systems sketch. A park transect. A night-sky log with an adult. A lab-path neighbor bench when the try needs supervised glassware. Shared looking and outings motivate. They do not replace the systems-and-claim move.

## What this book will not do

It will not give you a 180-day worksheet dump. It will not sell a curriculum franchise. It will not promise a score, a diploma, or a percentile. It will not reprint *Science for Young Minds* (ages 11–14 fair test with notebook and CER light), *Science for Little Thinkers* (ages 5–10 observe-before-explain), or *Science for Homeschooling* (grades 1–12 survey and transcript architecture). One pointer each, then teach fifteen-to-eighteen science. It is not a sequel to *Critical Thinking for Emerging Adults*, *English for Emerging Adults*, *History for Emerging Adults*, or *Math for Emerging Adults*. One pointer each, then teach looking at a named system and claiming from *this* notebook.

It will not invent a course titled Emerging Adults Science I. It will not treat a kitchen vinegar-and-soda picture as Chemistry credit. It will not make AP Biology, Chemistry, or Physics the year’s identity. It will not file invent-data neat rows, a generated gel, or a model summary as the student’s looking. It will not turn every Thursday into an FRQ packet. It will not add a ninth teaching chapter called “life of the habit.” The habit lives in the Science Hour rhythm, in done-enough checklists, and in the records you keep.

## How the twin spines sound in ordinary words

**Systems looking.** Where is the boundary? What are the parts and flows? What does the model leave out? How would a change inside or at the boundary matter?

**Lab claim with evidence (CER denser).** What do you claim? Which numbers or observations in *this* notebook support it? Why would that measurement count as evidence for *this* scientific idea — that is the warrant? What else could explain these numbers? What can’t this dataset tell us?

You will practice those spines on safe objects: a jar, a notebook, a redesign sticky, a photosynthesis inputs–outputs picture, a change-of-motion card, a RAMP card beside goggles, a kitchen honesty card labelled *not Chemistry credit*. Soft entry on living things: care, return organisms, wash hands. Home vertebrate dissection is optional, never required. Mold cultivation is refused by default. Curiosity is not a permit for unauthorized mixing.

## Encouragement that is specific

This week you can learn to name today’s system in ordinary words before any vocabulary list, and to hear coverage panic without grabbing a unit calendar. Today they can draw one boundary and point at one notebook row that could support a claim.

You are allowed to feel rusty and still teach well. They are allowed to wait, to revise, and to change their mind when the table disagrees with the tidy idea. Put the system down. Open the notebook. Ask the question. Wait.

Then teach.

# How to use this book this week

You do not need to finish the book before you begin. You need one Science Hour shape, one focus move, and one try the student can see.

## The honest this-week plan

1. Choose one spine emphasis: **systems looking** or **lab claim with evidence** (often both lightly).
2. Choose one named try-it from the chapter you are in — for example a system-boundary sketch, a stranger-notebook repair, a fair-test redesign, or a denser CER three-box.
3. Choose one vehicle for the week: Biology *or* Chemistry *or* Physics (Earth soft when the question asks for it). The vehicle carries the spine. It is not a survey dump.
4. Run a RAMP card if heat, chemicals, glass, living specimens, outdoor water, or electricity beyond a battery toy are coming. Stop if you cannot minimize the risk. Adult present for the whole try.
5. Run three to five Science Hours of the named shape (~30–50 minutes each).
6. Add one out-of-home or lab-path try-it when the week needs it — museum one-exhibit systems, park transect, night sky with adult, or a supervised neighbor bench.
7. On Friday: one exit ticket, one diagnostic wrong sticky, and a done-enough check (showable two ways; still holds after a night).

That plan is smaller than “units” and “tests.” It is the honest lesson plan this book promises. Coverage itch is the enemy of looking. One system question and one claim move done well beat three topics with no notebook.

# If you only remember five things

1. **Systems looking and denser CER are the twin spines.** Put a named system on the table — a jar, a body-subsystem card, a reaction you can stop, a change of motion. Leave the hour with a claim that points at *this* notebook, names a warrant (why that measurement counts for *this* idea), and can say what the data cannot tell you. Biology, Chemistry, and Physics are this week’s vehicles — not encyclopedia years. AP is an edge, not the weekly spine. Coverage is a map. Depth is the hour.

2. **The student looks, measures, and records first.** You ask one authentic question and wait. Struggle before rescue. Point at the materials, the table, the system diagram, or the notebook — not at the tidy vocabulary list — if the silence is hard. Hearing a wrong reason (force-as-fuel, cells-as-tiny-mouths, a confounded “proof”), then asking a better question, is teaching. Grabbing the answer or filling the silence with the explanation is not.

3. **A stranger-readable notebook is the high-school record.** Question, units, table before graph, uncertainty crumb, claim pointer. Invented neat rows are not data. A generated gel or micrograph is not a result. A model summary is not the observation. Kitchen change-of-matter pictures stay pictures — not Chemistry credit. Drawing alone was for younger bands; CER light was middle grades; raise the warrant and the limitation here.

4. **Safety is ordinary and non-negotiable.** Adult present for heat, chemicals, glass, living specimens, outdoor water, and any kitchen chemistry picture. RAMP before the try: Recognize, Assess, Minimize, Prepare. No unauthorized mixing. Stop is always allowed without penalty. Lab-path neighbors exist when the try needs real glassware. Soft entry on living things: care, return, wash hands; refuse home vertebrate dissection as default; mold cultivation refused by default.

5. **Place by skill, not birthday.** Unaided first on looking, measuring, and recording. The tool is parent-hosted, not a secret friend. COPPA does not bind ages 15–18; you still host and stay in the loop — in the room at 15–16; co-hold with AP and Common App integrity at 17–18. A path through high-school science looking is the promise. A diploma is not. A percentile is not. A culture-war win is not.

Keep these five on a sticky near the notebook. When the week frays, return to them before you reach for another packet.

If you forget everything else, remember this: a named system on the table, a stranger-readable page, a denser claim with a warrant, a RAMP card when heat is coming, and skill-based placement. That is enough to begin tomorrow.

# The Science Hour

![A kitchen lab table with a RAMP safety card, systems-boundary card, denser CER card, and dated lab notebook — still-life, no people, no logos.](art/science-hour-setup.jpg){width=100%}

This is the session shape for the whole book. Learn it once. Later chapters point back here in a sentence.

Science Hour is thirty to fifty minutes. It is not a museum field trip every day. It is not a silent “read your AP chapter” hour. It is not activity mania. It is not inventing neat rows to finish a graph. It is a repeating five-beat rhythm: open a system or question; take a short look; try, measure, and record once; talk and build denser CER; exit with a small ticket. The student looks. You ask one good question and wait.

## Five beats (~30–50 minutes)

**1. Open / system or question on the table (3–7 min).**  
Name today’s outcome in ordinary words. If this is a systems week, sketch or reveal a system — a jar, a body-subsystem card, a watershed map, a household-safe circuit. If this is a lab or fair-test week, pose a question that could come out otherwise. Run a RAMP check if heat, chemicals, glass, living specimens, outdoor water, or electricity beyond a battery toy are coming. Adult present for the whole try.

**2. Short look or prior-idea card (3–8 min).**  
What do you already think will happen? What does the model leave out? Hold the textbook answer for later. Optional: one labelled secondary figure with an honesty card — *not your data*.

**3. One try (15–25 min).**  
One fair test, one focused observation with measurement, one systems diagram plus one probe, or one denser-CER build from an existing notebook page. One try. Not a packet. Not five cookbook stations. Record in a notebook a stranger could almost read.

**4. Talk box + denser CER (5–10 min).**  
One authentic question about the evidence; wait three to five seconds; uptake to the table or diagram; optional revoice; “what would change the claim?” Build claim + notebook pointer + warrant (because-the-idea). Prompt one limitation *or* one alternative at 15–16; both at 17–18 when ready.

**5. Exit ticket (2–5 min).**  
Spoken CER crumb, systems boundary plus one flow, “can we tell for sure?” sentence, RAMP hazard named, or one diagnostic-error sticky. Done-enough is “right, or wrong-with-a-reason we can use tomorrow,” not a percentage.

Optional sixth beat for siblings: post-response wait so each speaker is heard. Lab-path days may stretch the try; still end with CER or systems exit.

### Band moves inside the hour

**15–16.** System boundary scaffolded by the adult → short look → try that may include one fair-test repair, one simple measurement table, or one denser CER with two evidence bullets and an explicit warrant → exit as oral CER or labelled diagram. One limitation *or* alternative prompted. Biology or Chemistry or Physics as *this week’s vehicle*. Adult present. AP labelled only.

**17–18.** More independent system model (what the model leaves out) → try that may include multi-trial comparison, uncertainty talk, or CER with explicit reasoning to a named idea → occasional lab-path neighbor for regulated techniques → exit as short paragraph or annotated graph. Alternative explanations *and* limitations. Integrate evidence across linked trials when the week asks. Occasional FRQ-shaped stretch — still not AP as weekly spine. Still refuse invent-data.

### Dose honesty

School science blocks run long with specialists and fume hoods. A parent hour is not a failed block. If a day only holds thirty minutes: RAMP (if needed) + system or question card + one measurement or one CER from yesterday’s table + exit ticket. One named move done well beats a rushed medley. Lab-path neighbors are scheduled honesty, not guilt. A second sitting later in the week beats one long sit that frays.

### Hour debug table

| Symptom | Likely cause | Move |
|---|---|---|
| Hour is only worksheets / AP FRQs | Missing look + talk | One real try or notebook page; one oral CER; one wait |
| Hour is only demos that “show the point” | Record and claim skipped | Learner records; exit ticket claim from *their* numbers |
| Learner invents numbers to fit the hypothesis | Invent-data fail | Stop; label honesty; redesign or use labelled secondary data |
| Learner files AI gel / perfect curve as result | Generated-result fail | Label fiction; reopen real photo or skip that try |
| Learner freezes when asked | Wait-time 1 was zero / question unclear | One clear question; count three |
| Design confounded; claim still confident | Fair-test rule not taught | Show confound vs fair; “can we tell for sure?” |
| Kitchen fizz filed as Chemistry credit | Path honesty fail | Label picture; point to lab-path neighbor for credit path |
| Session becomes AP dump every week | Weekly spine wrong | One system or one claim move; save FRQ for occasional stretch |
| Materials out, no talk | Facilitation skipped | Pose, wait, point at notebook row |
| Unsafe mixing / heat without plan | RAMP skipped | Stop; SAFE card; do not continue until minimized |
| Everything is a “system” with no boundary | SYS slogan without diagram | Draw boundary; name what is outside |
| Two trials → “prove the textbook” | Confirmation bias | Ask what result would falsify the claim |

## Talk-box shape (once)

Exact opening question: authentic, about what system we are looking at and what claim the notebook could support before the tidy idea — or what a stranger would need to reconstruct the trial, what changed and what stayed the same and whether we can tell for sure, where the system boundary is and what the model leaves out, what is happening in this living system with notebook evidence, what changed in matter or motion and how we know safely, what they claim with evidence and warrant, what they can show two ways. Not “did you have fun.” Not “what is the AP vocabulary word.”

Each teaching chapter locks one opening. Follow with three to five follow-ups: one for observe (“what do you *see* / *measure*?”); one for record (“what does the notebook / table show?”); one for reason tied to the try (“how do you know from *this*?” / “what is the warrant?”); one for check or alternative (“what else was different / can we tell for sure? / what does the model leave out? / what else could explain these numbers?”); one that lets them revise (“you may change your mind” / “try another look” / “redesign once”).

**How to wait.** After you ask, a slow three is a convention, not a sacrament (Rowe 1972 elementary *science* wait; Stahl 1994 think-time, labelled classroom-origin). After they stop, wait again. If they are mid-measure or mid-claim, do not cut them off. Look at the materials, the table, the system diagram, or the notebook, not at the adolescent, if the silence is hard. This book owns Rowe as science class — still. This book claims no kitchen wait coefficient.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the question was vague; wait-time 1 was zero; they are hunting a vocabulary word because you asked name-first; they are guessing what you want; the materials never landed; they offered a book-only guess; they think science means “repeat the parent’s explanation”; they think looking means “say what the screen already told me”; they invent neat rows because the trial was messy; they are bracing for an AP rubric sentence. Pose, wait, and point back at the notebook — do not fill the silence with the right vocabulary, and do not turn the table into a fight.

## In-home try-it shape (once)

Every teaching chapter names try-its with **time, materials, safety, the fun, the skill**. At least one try-it lives inside the home: system boundary on a jar; stranger notebook test; confound vs fair side-by-side; denser CER three-box; photosynthesis inputs–outputs picture; change-of-motion card; RAMP card before heat; kitchen picture honesty card labelled *not Chemistry credit*; conservation weighing. Pick one named card per hour from the banks (SAFE, LAB, REC, FAIR, CER, SYS, BIO, CHEM, PHYS, and the rest). Do not assign all eighty.

**How to pick this week:** choose one spine emphasis (systems looking or lab claim-with-evidence, often both lightly); choose one bank + one ID; one idea per hour; match band 15–16 or 17–18; run RAMP if the card says so; end with the exit ticket; note one diagnostic error.

## Out-of-home try-it shape (once)

At least one try-it lives outside unless the chapter is purely definitional — and even then, give a walk-sized instance: museum one-exhibit systems; park or yard transect; stream clarity soft; grocery produce structure–function; night sky with adult; lab-path neighbor bench session. Library, museum, and nature motivate. They do not replace the systems-and-claim move. Lab-path neighbors (community college open lab, supervised co-op, museum bench, field station day) are labelled as neighbors — not as “the kitchen is Chemistry.” Schedule honesty, not guilt.

## RAMP / safety spine (once)

ACS RAMP: **Recognize** hazards; **Assess** risks; **Minimize** risks; **Prepare** for emergencies. School ACS / NSTA / Flinn / Roy documents are the **ceiling**, not a DIY license.

Before any try with heat, chemicals, glass, sharp tools, living specimens, outdoor water, or electricity beyond battery toys:

1. Recognize hazards on today’s materials list.
2. Assess who could be harmed and how.
3. Minimize (lowest concentration or volume; substitute; PPE; clear space).
4. Prepare (what if spill / burn / broken glass — adult actions).
5. Decide go / redesign / lab-path neighbor / skip.

Adult present for the entire try. Stop is always allowed without penalty. No unmarked bottles. No bleach mixes. No drain cleaners. No alcohol flames. No sealed pressurized fizz bottles. No lasers in default banks. Living things — no harm, return, wash hands; mold cultivation refused by default; home vertebrate dissection refused as default; dissection optional never required. A vinegar-and-soda fizz is a change picture. It is not a Chemistry credit. Curiosity is not a permit for unauthorized mixing. Regulated chemicals, burners, and electrophoresis belong on a lab-path neighbor, not in a default home bank. If PPE is missing, do not run.

Keep a house RAMP card and a stop-authority phrase where everyone can see them. Walk the emergency mini-map once (extinguisher / water shutoff / adult phone / first-aid) before heat or chemical weeks. Practice the stop phrase on hypothetical cards — never with a live unsafe demo.

## AI box for ages 15–18 (once)

The student looks at a real trial, measures, and records what happened. The parent may use a tool. The student does not sit alone with an open chat as the only partner when the parent is co-teacher. Say this box once; later chapters point back in one sentence.

### Ages 15–16 (COPPA off; parent still in the room)

Parent co-holds the account. **Still in the room** for Science Hour. Scripts and parent–student talk. **Not** live open chat as the student’s only partner. Unaided first on looking, measuring, and recording. SCRIPT after the try. COPPA’s under-13 line is **behind them** — name that once as why *Science for Young Minds* was stricter at 11–12. The lock is still pedagogical. Turning 15 is not a licence for a secret science friend.

### Ages 17–18 (more agency; co-hold; AP / Common App integrity)

Parent co-holds / can audit. Turning 17 or 18 is **not a licence**. Same bans. The student may sit next to the parent while the parent uses a tool. They may hear revision questions after they have drafted a claim or lab report. They may not have an unsupervised companion account as only partner during the attempt. They may not be asked to “prompt the tutor until it agrees.” Unaided first. SCRIPT after the try. **Integrity add-ons:** no AI-generated AP Test submissions (College Board 2026 AP Exam Terms); Common App fraud examples include misrepresenting AI substantive output as one’s own — revision questions after *the student’s* draft remain allowed; “write my Common App essay” / “write my lab report” is the ban.

COPPA does **not bind** ages 15–18. Parent still hosts / stays in the loop.

### You may (parent account; after the student has tried, or before for parent prep)

- Explain this idea *to you* from a named lesson (title, page, CED, or today’s apparatus: system jar, notebook page, fair-test redesign, denser CER card).
- Extra isomorphic practice with the answer key held by the parent (a blank observation line, empty data table to fill from a *new* try, FRQ stem, claim–evidence–reasoning card). The student never sees the key.
- A labelled SCRIPT after the try — a short spoken sequence for *you* to say, not a paragraph for the student to copy as their observation or CER.
- Revision questions after a claim or lab-report draft — not a rewrite of the student’s lab report.
- A hint after an attempt. A hint is a question or a named idea, not the finished observation or a fake data table.
- Diagnose work the student already produced. You hear the diagnosis and decide. Crop to the paper. Do not upload the student’s face.
- Discussion questions you will vet and ask yourself — the student answers you, not the tab.
- A labelled schematic (not a result) to look at together, then put away before the try.

### You may not

- Paste the worksheet / blank data table / FRQ packet and ask the tool to complete it (photo-to-key).
- Ask “what’s the answer” or “just write what I should have seen” during the student’s attempt, or let the student ask that of a window.
- **Invent an observation or a data table** and file it as the student’s looking.
- Put a **model summary** on the table as the observation instead of a real trial.
- Generate a gel, micrograph, sky photo, titration curve, or “what I saw under the glass” and treat it as the student’s result. **Generated image ≠ data.**
- Park an unsupervised chatbot as the student’s only partner during the attempt — not at 15, not at 18 — when the parent is co-teacher. No “scientist friend” account.
- Write the student’s observation, caption, CER paragraph, Materials–Methods–Results–Discussion, FRQ, or lab report (write-my-lab-report).
- Invent facts (what the mass did, what the temperature measured, whether the magnet stuck). Look.
- Use TTS, narrated rewrite, or a voice-clone toy to **replace looking/measuring** at the trial that is today’s lesson.
- Grade the student’s work from a detector score.
- Print a certificate of science.
- Hand the student an agent (Hermes Agent, Grok Bot, or any cloud-computer worker) as a science partner.
- Coach live-exam cheating or college-application fraud.

One crutch sentence, labelled, then teach: in a **high-school math** trial, an unguarded chatbot made practice look better and left students worse on the test with the window closed (Bastani et al., 2025). **That is not a 15–18 science RCT.** **OFF-AGE for science hour.** (*Math for Emerging Adults* may call the same paper ON-AGE for math — keep those labels separate.) The kitchen-table rule it supports: the model may prepare the adult and the next looking page; it may not do the student’s looking, measuring, recording, live exam, or lab report when those are the lesson.

A fluent filled blank from a machine is performance, not the student’s science looking. Keep accounts where the parent can open them. A science tool is not a secret friend.

Names that stay separate: Hermes 4 is not Hermes Agent. Grok is not Grok Bot and is not El Salvador Grok-as-tutor. There is no Grok Bot education SKU. Agents are not chatbots. Detectors fail; do not detector-grade a student’s “the mass went from 12.4 g to 11.1 g.” Fabricated citations are parent-side — if you asked a tool for a paper on fair tests or systems models, open a named Framework PDF or a named curriculum page.

# Chapter 1 — What science looks like at 15–18

![A systems-boundary jar card beside a denser CER card and a dated lab notebook — still-life, no people, no logos.](art/ch01-opener.jpg){width=100%}

## Why this matters

Fifteen-to-eighteen science is not a laminated method poster on the wall. It is not a coverage march through every vocabulary list in a publisher’s scope-and-sequence. It is not an AP unit calendar taped to the fridge as the year’s identity. It is twin spines you can practice this week: **systems looking** and **lab claim with evidence** — denser CER with a warrant that ties *this* notebook to a scientific idea, plus a named limitation or alternative.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

**Everyday picture.** Imagine a closed jar with soil, a seedling, and condensation on the glass. The jar wall is a boundary. Light and water are inputs. Growth and vapor are outputs you can talk about carefully. “What is inside / outside?” is systems looking. “The leaves got bigger because I watered them — see day-1 and day-7 height in the notebook — because plants use water and light as inputs for growth” is denser CER starting to form: claim, evidence pointer, warrant tied to an idea. Saying “ecosystems are systems” while staring at a vocabulary list is not.

**Precise picture.** Systems looking means the learner can (1) draw or state a boundary, (2) name parts and interactions inside, (3) name inputs, outputs, and (when relevant) feedback, (4) say what the model leaves out, and (5) ask how a change inside or at the boundary would matter. Framework crosscutting concept “systems and system models” is the map; the Hour owns the diagram and the talk, not a poster quiz.

Lab claim with evidence means a statement about the material world that answers this week’s question; evidence that is numbers, observations, or labelled secondary data the learner did not invent; reasoning that ties evidence to claim through a scientific idea (the warrant); willingness to revise. America’s Lab Report design principles travel here: clear outcomes; thoughtfully sequenced experiences; content and process together; ongoing reflection and discussion — not isolated “lab day” theater. Framework / NGSS 9–12 are a moves map — practices, crosscutting concepts, core ideas — not a franchise to cover and not a statute for your kitchen. Bio → Chem → Physics is not a universal law of sequencing.

Prior ideas are the mind at rest. Force-as-fuel, cells-as-tiny-mouths, plant food only through roots — these do not magically expire at fifteen. Hear them. Repair with another look + a named idea just-in-time. Do not scold. Do not equation-punish.

Wrong answers you should be able to hear (illustrations, not data):

1. **Force-as-fuel / “the push ran out” / “the table doesn’t push.”** Still heard into high school and college. Miss: Newtonian construction is not obvious even after a mechanics course. Repair: another look; revoice; notebook; name change-of-motion lightly.
2. **Cells as tiny mouths / plant food only through roots.** Miss: living systems need inputs–outputs talk with evidence. Repair: systems arrows; mass or observation question; named figure — not an AP dump.
3. **Coverage panic / “we have to finish the unit.”** Miss: depth is the hour; coverage is a map. Repair: one system question this week; park the rest of the list.
4. **Method-poster theater.** Laminated steps with apparatus as decoration. Miss: practices need a real try and talk. Repair: look first; denser CER from *this* trial.
5. **AP-as-weekly-spine / birthday placement / scientist cosplay.** “They’re fifteen so it must be AP Bio.” Miss: skill, not birthday; AP is edge; looking moves are not an identity costume. Repair: skill check; one system + denser CER this week.



**How the twin spines share a week (illustration, not a calendar).** Monday: SYS-01 boundary on the phenomenon. Tuesday: LAB-01 outcome sentence and a short look. Wednesday: SAFE-01 if heat is coming Thursday; otherwise clean the notebook page. Thursday: talk box + denser CER crumb. Friday: exit ticket + one diagnostic sticky. OUT-01 once this month. Chemistry pictures stay pictures. AP FRQ, if used at all, is an occasional stretch — not every Thursday.

**What “denser CER” adds beyond middle-grade CER light.** Claim still answers the question. Evidence still points at locatable notebook rows. Reasoning now names the scientific idea as a warrant — *why those numbers count for this idea*. Then one limitation *or* one alternative at 15–16; both when ready at 17–18. You do not need a full lab report in week one. You do need a claim that could not float free of the page.

**America’s Lab Report in kitchen language.** Isolated “lab day” with no talk was no better than reading for content mastery in the studies that report reviewed. Units that integrated the try with instruction and discussion did better (often small-scale). Design principles travel: clear outcomes; thoughtful sequence; integrate content with process; ongoing discussion. Implication for your table: the try lives inside the idea and the talk. Refuse isolated lab-day theater. This is a useful study shape, not a promise that every home will see the same result.

**Framework / NGSS as a moves map.** High-school grain emphasizes scatterplots and covariation when the math band supports it, precision and controls, models, alternative explanations, and limits of the data. Translate to the kitchen: one system question; one fair test or focused observation; one stranger-readable page; one denser CER with warrant and a limitation or alternative; RAMP if needed. Do not import AP unit pacing as the family’s year. Do not assign every performance expectation.

**Parent warm-up (5–8 min), alone with tomorrow’s apparatus.** Take the jar or body-subsystem card. Draw the boundary. Write one claim a notebook *could* support before any vocabulary list. Practice hearing “we have to finish the unit” without grabbing coverage. Cut denser CER to claim + notebook pointer + because-the-idea. Stop when you can say today’s system in ordinary words.

## How to teach it this week

**Session shape (~30–50 min).** Warm-up looking (jar or card on the table) → materials down → RAMP if a later try needs heat or chemicals (today may be SAFE-01 on paper only) → frame the system or question → short systems probe or LAB-01 outcome sentence → try / measure / record → one authentic question + wait → denser CER crumb → exit ticket.

**Exact wording you can say.**

- “What system are we looking at — and what claim could this trial’s notebook support — before we explain the tidy idea?”
- “Where is the boundary? What is inside? What is outside on purpose?”
- “Point at a row that could support a claim — even if we have not named the tidy idea yet.”
- “What does this model leave out?”
- “You may change your mind when we look again.”

**First try-it for the student.** SYS-01 Boundary First on a closed jar or a body-subsystem card: draw the boundary; list inside/outside; leave one sticky “what we have not explained yet.” Pair lightly with LAB-01: write the outcome sentence *before* materials. **How to fade help.** At first, draw the boundary with them. Then ask them to draw; you only point. Then they draw and you ask only the talk-box opening. Stop talking when the pencil is moving and the notebook has a date and a question. Do not narrate the tidy idea while they are still looking.

- **15–16:** Scaffold the boundary; one input and one output arrow; denser CER as claim + two notebook bullets + because-the-idea; one limitation *or* alternative prompted; Bio or Chem or Phys as this week’s vehicle; adult present; AP labelled only.

Chemistry vehicle examples: properties before names on safe household substances with honesty card *not Chemistry credit*; conservation weighing picture. (3) “Leaves out ___.” (4) Tag: my measurement / labelled secondary / not invent. (5) Diagnostic sticky: force-as-fuel or coverage panic named and repaired with a next look.

**Refuse this week.** Method-poster theater; AP pacing as identity; Young Minds bank reprint with longer words; Homeschooling survey dump as this Hour’s TOC; invent-data neat rows; generated gel as result; model summary as the observation; isolated lab-day with no talk; kitchen fizz as Chemistry credit.

## Practice that actually builds learning

### Talk box

**Opening (locked):** “What system are we looking at — and what claim could this trial’s notebook support — before we explain the tidy idea?”

**Follow-ups:**
1. Observe: “What do you *see* at the boundary — what is inside, what is outside?”
2. Record: “What does the notebook page show that a stranger could use?”
3. Reason: “How do you know from *this* page — what warrant ties that row to an idea?”
4. Check / alternative: “What does this model leave out? What else could explain what we see?”
5. Revise: “You may change your mind — try another look or redraw the boundary once.”

**How to wait.** Pose. Count three to five silently. Look at the jar or the notebook, not at their face, if the silence is hard. After they speak, wait again before evaluating. Revoice without stealing: “So you’re saying the jar wall is the boundary and condensation is inside — is that what your diagram shows?” This book owns Rowe as science class — still. This book claims no kitchen wait coefficient.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the question was vague; wait-time 1 was zero; they are hunting a vocabulary word because you asked name-first; they are guessing what you want; the materials never landed; they offered a book-only guess; they think science means “repeat the parent’s explanation”; they think looking means “say what the screen already told me”; they invent neat rows because the trial was messy; they are bracing for an AP rubric sentence. Pose, wait, and point back at the notebook — do not fill the silence with the right vocabulary, and do not turn the table into a fight.

### In-home try-its

**SAFE-01 RAMP Card Before Any Heat or Chemical**  
- **Time:** 20–35 min (may precede a paired try another day)  
- **Materials:** RAMP card template; today’s planned materials list; goggles if a later try needs them  
- **Safety:** This *is* the RAMP practice. Adult present. No unauthorized mixing.  
- **The fun:** Decide go / redesign / lab-path neighbor / skip — real authority.  
- **The skill:** Four RAMP lines filled for *this* plan; go/redesign/skip circled.  
- **Steps:** (1) Recognize hazards on the list. (2) Assess who could be harmed and how. (3) Minimize (lowest volume; substitute; PPE; clear space). (4) Prepare (spill / burn / glass — adult actions). (5) Decide go / redesign / lab-path neighbor / skip.  
- **Error patterns:** Skipping to “we’ll be careful”; treating ACS as permission for garage chemistry; learner alone with heat.

**SAFE-02 Stop Authority Drill**  
- **Time:** 15–25 min  
- **Materials:** Two hypothetical unsafe scenario cards (labelled hypothetical); stop-phrase card  
- **Safety:** Verbal rehearsal only; no live unsafe demo.  
- **The fun:** Practicing “stop” without penalty.  
- **The skill:** “I stop when ___. The hazard was ___.”  
- **Steps:** Read scenario; learner says stop and names the hazard; adult confirms stop is always allowed.  
- **Error patterns:** Treating stop as failure; adult pressure to finish the “cool” demo.

**LAB-01 Clear Outcome Before Materials**  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** Outcome card (“By the end I can ___ using evidence from ___”); materials for a short try OR yesterday’s leftover setup  
- **Safety:** Per materials; default no heat.  
- **The fun:** Outcome first feels like a secret adult move.  
- **The skill:** Outcome sentence + one evidence row cited.  
- **Steps:** Write outcome first; only then set materials; run short try or analyze prior data; check outcome met or revise tomorrow.  
- **Error patterns:** Pulling materials first; vague “learn about photosynthesis.”

**LAB-05 Sequence Idea–Try–Talk**  
- **Time:** 35–50 min  
- **Materials:** Three sticky sequence cards; short household-safe phenomenon (ice melt rates, shadow length, battery brightness)  
- **Safety:** Household-safe only.  
- **The fun:** Catching the urge to explain first.  
- **The skill:** “We looked before naming ___.”  
- **Steps:** Order the hour as idea crumb → try → talk; deliberately avoid “talk first dumping the answer”; record; exit.  
- **Error patterns:** Explanation swallow before looking; try with no talk.

**SYS-01 Boundary First**  
- **Time:** 30–45 min  
- **Materials:** Phenomenon (jar garden, aquarium photo, house HVAC vent, watershed map print); blank paper

- **The fun:** Argue about what counts as “inside.”  
- **The skill:** Boundary sentence + two outside items.  
- **Steps:** Draw boundary; list inside/outside; revise after talk; refuse “the universe” as default boundary.

## For the student

Science at your age is looking at a **system** and making a **claim from a notebook** — not memorizing a poster called “the scientific method,” and not racing every chapter by Friday.

A system has a boundary. Inside the jar: soil, seedling, air, water vapor. Outside: the room, the lamp, your hand. Inputs cross the boundary (light, water you add). Outputs or changes show up (condensation, height). The model always leaves something out. Naming what it leaves out is part of the work.

A denser claim sounds like: *I claim ___. My evidence is row / measurement ___. This counts because ___ (the idea). This dataset cannot tell us ___ / another explanation could be ___.*

**Tiny worked example.** Jar wall drawn. Sticky: “outside = room air, lamp.” Notebook: Day 1 height 4.2 cm; Day 7 height 5.1 cm. Claim crumb: “The seedling grew taller over six days — see Day 1 and Day 7 — consistent with water and light as inputs for growth.” Limitation: “We did not measure CO₂.” Alternative: “Maybe the lamp also warmed the soil — we did not separate light from heat.”

**Try 1.** Draw a system boundary on a jar or a body-subsystem card. List two things inside and two outside. Leave one sticky: “what we have not explained yet.”  
**Try 2.** Write one claim a stranger could check against your page — before anyone explains the tidy idea. Point at the exact row.  
**Explain it back.** Tell someone: what is the boundary, which notebook row could support a claim, and what does your model leave out?  
**Challenge.** Redesign once: change one input at the boundary and predict what you would measure — then, if safe and approved, try it and revise.



**Two bands, same respect.** At 15–16, you may need a scaffolded boundary and a prompted limitation *or* alternative — that is normal, not “behind.” At 17–18, you may revise a model against data, name alternative *and* limitation, and integrate two linked trials — that is growth, not a licence to skip the notebook. Either way, the adult is still present for heat, chemicals, glass, living specimens, and outdoor water. Turning seventeen does not unlock unsupervised mixing.

**What this is not.** This is not “you are a scientist now.” This is not a culture-war pamphlet hour. This is not a kitchen fizz filed as Chemistry with Lab. This is not a chatbot CER with no trial. This is looking at a named system and claiming from *this* notebook.

You are allowed to wait. You are allowed to change your mind when you look again. You are not required to sound like a textbook on the first try. You are not a “scientist” because someone bought a binder. You *are* someone who can look, measure, record, and claim from *this* page.

## If it isn’t clicking

**1. They draw no boundary / everything is “the universe.”**  
Next move: Hand a physical jar. Ask only “finger on the wall — inside or outside?” Fade to paper after the finger works. If they still freeze, you draw once while they name inside/outside aloud; then trade roles next day.

**2. They recite vocabulary with no notebook pointer.**  
Next move: Cover the textbook. Ask for one row. If there is no row, run a five-minute look and write one number before any claim. Praise the pointer, not the fancy word.

**3. Coverage panic (yours or theirs) swallows the hour.**  
Next move: Write this week’s one system question on a sticky. Park the unit list in a folder. One question done well beats three topics with no looking. If AP anxiety is driving the panic, label AP as edge and return to the jar.

Slow down when expectation still melds with data, or when invent-data neat rows appear — stop and redesign rather than “fixing” the table. Go ahead when they can draw a boundary and point at a row without prompting. Get a human tutor when anxiety or a long stall makes every hour a fight — no shame. Correct a wrong reason (“the push ran out”) with another look + a named idea, without crushing the look. “Never correct the adolescent” is not this book’s rule.



**If the hour collapses into lecture.** Stop. Put the jar back on the table. Ask the locked talk-box opening. Wait. Point at the notebook. Resume looking.

**If siblings share the hour.** Give each a sticky boundary or a row to own. Use Stahl’s post-response wait so each speaker is heard. Do not turn the table into a competition for the “right” vocabulary word.

**If you feel rusty on vocabulary.** You do not need the full glossary this week. You need to name the system in ordinary words, hear force-as-fuel, and ask where the notebook points. Vocabulary arrives just-in-time after the look.

## Why this matters

High-school science looking dies when the record cannot travel. A stranger — an evaluator, a co-op teacher, future-you next Tuesday — should reconstruct the trial without watching it: question, materials, procedure crumb, table with units, uncertainty crumb, claim pointer. That is the high-school record. Drawing alone was for younger bands. CER light with a notebook pointer was middle grades. Here you raise stranger-reconstruct and uncertainty, and you refuse invent-data neat rows, adult-filled tables, and generated “perfect” logs.

When the notebook can travel, denser CER has somewhere to point. Systems looking has a dated page. “With Lab” on a later transcript becomes a claim you can defend with supervised investigation evidence — not a vinegar volcano sticker. Biology / Chemistry / Physics titles a stranger can read live *inside* the notebook header, never as a brand and never as Emerging Adults Science I.

This chapter ages up from *Science for Young Minds* table→graph→sentence without reprinting middle banks. One pointer, then teach. Place by skill, not birthday. Neatness is not honesty. Honesty is the science.

What unlocks for you this week: stop filling the table for them; ask the stranger question; smell invented neat rows. What they try today: rewrite one messy page so a stranger could follow — without inventing numbers.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

**Everyday picture.** Hand last week’s notebook to a neighbor with the apparatus covered. Can they tell what question was asked, what was measured, in what units, and what claim was attempted? If they cannot, the page is a private diary, not a high-school science record. Fix the page — prettier invented numbers are not the fix.

**Precise picture.** America’s Lab Report defines laboratory experiences as interactions with the material world — or with data drawn from the material world. Fake teacher sets do not count. Framework high-school record densening includes tables, scatterplots when ready, and precision talk. Kitchen translation: table before graph; units; operational definitions; uncertainty crumb in plain language; photo of *this* setup as context (not a stock photo, not an AI bench). Generated gel ≠ data. Model summary ≠ observation. Invented neat rows ≠ student’s data.

Wrong answers you should be able to hear (illustrations, not data):

**Parent warm-up (5–8 min).** Take last week’s notebook. Cover the apparatus. Can you reconstruct the trial from the page alone? Note missing units, missing question, neat rows that smell invented. Practice the sentence: “What would a stranger need in your notebook to reconstruct this trial without watching it?” Cut denser CER to claim + notebook pointer + because-the-idea — the pointer only works if the page can be found.



**Why stranger-readable beats neat.** A portfolio that looks magazine-perfect with blank tables behind it fails the high-school test. An evaluator who asks “what did you measure?” needs a row, not a vibe. NCAA “with lab if offered,” dual-enrollment lab claims, and co-op lab credit all assume a record that can travel. You do not need professional lab-notebook culture in week one. You do need date, question, units, and honest numbers.

**America’s Lab Report honesty card (say once).** Laboratory experiences interact with the material world — or with data drawn from it. Fake teacher data sets do not count. Kitchen translation: my measurement and labelled secondary are allowed; invent and generated “results” are not. This is a useful study shape, not a promise that every home will see the same result.

**Framework densening without a stats course.** High-school grain includes tables, scatterplots when the math band supports it, and precision talk. At 15–16, table + units + “about” language is enough. At 17–18, repeated measures and range talk densify — still not a statistics course. Algebra sits beside physics ideas when ready; it is not a wall that ends the notebook.

**Wrong answer seven: “We already know what should happen, so the numbers can wait.”** Miss: look and record first. Repair: blank table before the tidy idea; fill from the try; revise the claim if needed.

**Wrong answer eight: “The AI lab partner filled my table while I watched a video.”** Miss: unaided first on measuring and recording. Repair: close the window; run a short try; student writes the rows.

They shrug: “I remembered.” That is invent-data theater. Repair: new short try, table first, graph only from rows, uncertainty crumb from repeats. A sixteen-year-old’s page is messy but dated, with units and a crossed-out claim replaced by a better one pointing at row 3. That is high-school looking.

**What you are learning this week as the adult.** Stop filling the table. Ask the stranger question and wait. Smell invent-data. Tag data types. Keep kitchen pictures labelled as pictures. Point denser CER at a locatable row. That fluency is enough to teach tomorrow without a lab-methods course.

## Practice that actually builds learning

### Talk box

**Opening (locked):** “What would a stranger need in your notebook to reconstruct this trial without watching it?”

**Follow-ups:**
1. Observe: “What do you *measure* — not what you expected?”
2. Record: “What does the table show — with units?”
3. Reason: “Which row supports the claim — and why would that measurement count for this idea?”
4. Check / alternative: “Is anything missing that a stranger would need? Could another explanation fit these numbers?”
5. Revise: “Rewrite one page without inventing numbers — you may change your mind about the claim.”

**How to wait.** Pose the stranger question. Count three to five. Look at the notebook, not at their face. After they speak, wait again. Revoice: “So a stranger would need the units on column B and the date at the top — is that what you’re fixing?” Stuck silence usually means: the question was vague; wait-time was zero; they are hunting a vocabulary word; they think neatness is the goal; they invent rows because the trial was messy; they are bracing for an AP rubric sentence; the materials never landed. Pose, wait, point at the page.

## For the student

Your notebook is not only a diary. It is a record a stranger should almost reconstruct without watching the trial.

A stranger-readable page usually has: date; question; materials crumb; what you did (enough that another person could try); a table with units; what you claim — pointing at a row; what you are unsure of. Fancy handwriting is optional. Invented neat numbers are not allowed.

**Tiny worked example.** Question: Does ice melt faster in room light or in shade on this counter? Table:

| Trial | Condition | Time to melt (min) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | light | 42 |
| 2 | shade | 61 |
| 3 | light | 45 |

Caption: “Three trials on the kitchen counter; start mass ~20 g ice cubes; room ~22 °C (thermometer).” Claim crumb: “Ice melted faster in light than shade in these trials — see times in the table — consistent with more energy transfer in light.” Limitation: “Counter drafts may differ; only three trials.” Uncertainty crumb: “Times are ± about 1 min (phone stopwatch, human start/stop).”

**Try 1.** Hand your last page to a parent or sibling as stranger. Rewrite one missing piece — date, units, or question — without inventing numbers.  
**Try 2.** Build a table before any graph from a short measurement try. Check: every plotted point appears in a row.  
**Explain it back.** Tell someone what a stranger would need on your page that is not there yet.  
**Challenge.** Three quick repeats of the same measurement; write “about ___ (range ___ to ___).” Skip inventing a ± to look advanced.

You may rewrite. You may change your claim when the table disagrees with your expectation. You may not invent rows. A generated picture is not your result. A model paragraph is not your observation. Messy honest beats neat fake.

At 15–16, headers and units may need scaffolding — ask for the checklist sticky. At 17–18, add spread talk and a methods crumb another could repeat — still without inventing results. Either way, unaided first on measuring and recording when that is the lesson.

**A second worked crumb (physics vehicle).** Question: How does release height change the roll distance of a toy car on this ramp? Table headers: Trial | Release height (cm) | Roll distance (cm). Three trials at each of two heights. Caption names the track, the car, and the meter stick. Claim points at the table. Limitation: carpet friction may change. Leave out a third height you did not run.

**A third worked crumb (biology vehicle).** Question: Do these bean seedlings differ in height after five days under the window vs under the shelf? Measure in cm. Photograph the setup as context. Tag “my measurement.” Refuse a generated plant photo as your result. Claim points at rows. Limitation: window heat and light are confounded — note that honestly.

**What success feels like for you.** You can hand a page to someone who was not there and watch them retell the trial. You can say “I do not know yet” when the table is thin. You can refuse a pretty fake. That is high-school science looking.

# Chapter 3 — Designing and redesigning fair tests

![A kitchen-table still-life from a high three-quarter view: two trial cards labelled Confound and Fair, a sticky that reads “tell for sure?”, a dated notebook open to a blank three-column sticky (Change / Measure / Same), one safe mini-try apparatus set aside, a short redesign sticky ready for a second page. No people. No logos. No readable package branding.](art/ch03-opener.jpg){width=100%}

## Why this matters

You changed the surface. You also changed the height. You also used a heavier mass. Then you announced that roughness stops the motion — proved.

That announcement is not a character flaw. It is what happens when a try feels busy and the mind grabs the first difference it likes. The hour’s job is smaller and kinder: name the one thing you meant to change, name what you measured, name what you kept the same on purpose, and ask — after the try — whether you can tell for sure. If you cannot, redesign once. Keep the messy first page. Add a new dated page for the repair.

Fair test remains a taught procedure at ages 15–18 — denser materials, same rule. Change one thing. Measure one thing. Keep the rest the same. Ask “can we tell for sure?” Redesign after a confound. Accept “insufficient evidence” as a valid outcome. Adolescents this age can name an independent variable and a dependent variable in ordinary language, hold constants on purpose, catch a confound, and redesign without shame. At 15–16 they catch an obvious confound with a prompt and redesign with a coached constant list. At 17–18 they design an unconfounded try more independently, transfer the same rule to new materials, and say when the data are too thin to claim. They do not become fair-minded because a parent bought an AP binder, and control-of-variables does not appear by staring at a pile of ramps alone.

What this idea unlocks is every later claim that points at a trial. A plant that got more light *and* more water cannot teach you about light alone. A conductivity try that also changed probe depth and water temperature cannot teach you about salt alone. A vinegar-and-soda fizz is still a change picture — adult present, authorized pair only — and it is still not a Chemistry credit. One deliberate change, recorded in a dated notebook a stranger could reconstruct, is how looking turns into a claim you can check. Chapter 2 built the stranger-readable notebook. This chapter puts the fair-test rule inside it. Chapter 7 will densify the claim with a warrant. Do not skip the redesign for the sake of a tidy paragraph.

Two refusals sit beside the idea, or the week becomes noise.

Discovery-only is not the program. A useful classroom study of third- and fourth-graders learning to design unconfounded experiments found that children who received clear teaching of the control-of-variables rule produced far more “masters” than children left to unguided discovery on the same materials — and both groups manipulated the materials. The difference was explanation of the rule. Masters later looked alike on a transfer task that asked them to judge someone else’s experiment poster. That study was a classroom trial on ramps, not a kitchen RCT for ages 15–18, and the percentages are not this adolescent’s score. This is a useful study, not a promise that every home will see the same result. Age the *shape*: show a confounded design and a fair design on the same question before vocabulary; open a blank table before the race; ask “can we tell for sure?”; then let the student redesign. Do not declare discovery dead. Do not import classroom percentages as this kitchen’s score.

Worksheet-only is not the program either. A fair-test sheet with no apparatus on the table is vocabulary without a lookable. Activity mania — ten demos, no record, no “what changed?” — is entertainment. Confirmation bias dressed as science — “prove the textbook claim” by changing three things until the graph looks right — is fraud training. Coverage as the hour — every PE by Friday — is a map mistaken for depth. One try with a named change, a blank table filled from real trials, and one authentic redesign beats all four.

You do not need to be a scientist. You do need to put the materials down, ask what they will change and what they will keep the same, wait, and point back at the notebook when the claim gets ahead of the evidence.

This week you can learn to hear confound pride and to answer it with a redesign, not a scolding lecture. Today the student can catch one confound and redesign once.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

Many adults feel rusty on “variables” and laminated scientific-method posters. That is ordinary. Five minutes here, then the warm-up, is enough for tomorrow. The student still does the trying, the recording, and the redesign.

**Everyday picture.** Two cookies from different batches. One was baked longer. One used a different pan. Someone asks which change made the cookie browner. You cannot tell for sure — two things changed. That kitchen moment is fair test in ordinary clothes. You do not need the word *variable* to feel the problem. At 15–18 you will also use the tool words when they help: independent variable for the one deliberate change, dependent variable for what you measure, constants for what you keep alike. Tool words, not a spelling test with no trial.

**Precise picture.** Four teen words carry the procedure:

- **Change (IV):** the one thing we decide to make different on purpose.
- **Measure (DV):** what we count, time, weigh, or carefully observe — with units and an operational definition when needed.
- **Same (controls / constants):** everything else we keep alike on purpose.
- **Can we tell for sure?** If two things changed, we cannot blame one. If the data are too thin, “insufficient evidence” is a valid outcome.

A second precise picture: a confounded try next to a clean try on the same question. Same question — does the surface change how far the object travels? Confounded: steep-smooth-short with a light mass against shallow-rough-long with a heavy mass. Clean: same height, same mass, same release method, same start line; only the surface differs. Three trials each surface. Distances with units in a blank table drawn *before* the first release. Ask what a stranger would need to reconstruct the trial and whether you can tell for sure. That hour is the rule with examples, then the student’s redesign. It is not a sermon that discovery is dead. It is not an AP FRQ packet as Tuesday’s identity.

Framework and NGSS high-school progressions treat control of variables, precision, and alternative explanations as *moves* — maps for what looking can include, not a mandate to finish every performance expectation this week. Borrow the move. Leave the PE dump.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. *Confound pride.* “We changed the surface *and* the height *and* the mass — and look, rough stops it!” Hear the pride. Do not crush the look. Ask: “Can we tell for sure it was the surface?” Point at the two other changes. Redesign so only one thing differs. Keep their original page; add a new dated page for the redesign.

2. *Variable vocabulary quiz with no trial.* “Define independent variable” while the apparatus stays in the closet. Hear coverage dressed as science. Put the materials down. Name IV on *this* trial.

3. *Invented neat rows.* The trial was messy; the notebook looks perfect. Hear invent-data. Keep the messy numbers. Redesign if needed. A fluent filled blank from a machine is performance, not the student’s looking.

4. *“Prove the textbook.”* The student (or the packet) treats the try as confirmation theater — change whatever it takes until the expected graph appears. Hear confirmation bias. Ask what would falsify the claim. Keep inconvenient trials. If evidence is thin, say so.

5. *Kitchen chemistry as Chemistry credit.* Fizz, foam, excitement — then “we did chemistry.” Hear the credit grab. Name it plainly: this is a change picture. Adult measures. Authorized pair only. No sealed bottle. No other mixes. It does not unlock titration or a Chemistry transcript line. Put the work under Science or Physical Science when the work is actually that — or under Chemistry only when supervised investigation honestly belongs there.

A sixth you will also hear: “Just tell me if the design is right.” The student wants the answer key instead of the redesign. Point at the materials and the blank “same” cards. Ask what they would keep the same next time. Do not grab the answer. Do not fill the silence with the explanation.

**Five-to-eight-minute parent warm-up**

Do this alone with tomorrow’s apparatus — cards for a confounded design and a fair design on the same question, or a safe mini-try — before the lesson.

Minute 1. Set or sketch a deliberately messy try: different height *and* different surface *and* different mass. Say aloud: “Can we tell for sure?” Answer yourself: no.

Minute 2. Reset so only one thing changes. Touch each “same.” Say the change once. Say the measure once, with units.

Minute 3. Draw a blank table with headers: trial #, condition (IV), measurement (DV + units), notes. Leave cells empty.

Minute 4. Practice the locked opening: “What did we change on purpose — what stayed the same — and can we tell for sure?” Count a slow three after you ask. Look at the materials or the cards, not at an imaginary adolescent.

Minute 5–8. Write Change / Measure / Same on three scraps. Put them face down. Tomorrow the student’s words fill them. Decide whether tomorrow is FAIR-01 (confound vs fair side-by-side) or FAIR-02 (name IV, DV, constants before materials) first — both are strong. If notebook habits from Chapter 2 are still thin, keep one stranger-notebook check in the week. Skill, not birthday.

## How to teach it this week

Science Hour stays the five beats you already know from the front of the book: open / system or question + RAMP if needed; short look or prior-idea card; one try / measure / record; talk box + denser CER crumb; exit ticket. This week the plan and try beats own the named change and the redesign. Total often ~30–50 minutes. Stop while a look remains.

**Warm-up looking (3–7 min).** Put the materials or the two design cards in view. Cover any worksheet title that already says “hypothesis proved.” Ask what they notice about the setup — before any vocabulary sentence. Wait a slow three (classroom-origin wait; use the move, not a kitchen coefficient).

**Plan the fair test (4–8 min).** Name change / measure / same. Draw the blank table *before* materials move for the race. At 15–16, co-build the “same” list and gate start until cards are filled. At 17–18, the student names IV/DV/constants more independently; you still gate safety and RAMP. Adult present for heat, electricity, outdoor water, chemicals, glassware, and any kitchen chemistry picture.

**Try / measure / record (15–25 min).** Student runs the trials. You do not grab the notebook. Messy real numbers beat invented neat ones. Two or three trials per condition at 15–16; more rows, uncertainty crumb, and a short methods sentence a stranger could follow at 17–18 when the habit holds. If a confound appears mid-try, stop the claim — do not “fix” by inventing rows.

**Talk + denser CER crumb (5–10 min).** Claim that answers the question. Evidence that points at a row. One “because” that names a scientific idea already on the table this week — a light warrant, not a lecture. Point at “can we tell for sure?” Name one limitation or alternative if the data allow. Chapter 7 will densify; this week the redesign owns the hour.

**Exit ticket (2–5 min).** Name IV/DV/same for *this* trial; or “confound was ___ / fair redesign changes only ___”; or “insufficient evidence — need ___ before claiming ___.” Diagnostic wrong noted by you for tomorrow — not a graded performance.

**Exact wording you can say**

- “What did we change on purpose — what stayed the same — and can we tell for sure?”
- “What are we measuring — in what units?”
- “What else was different — what would we redesign?”
- “You may change your mind after the redesign.”

## Practice that actually builds learning

Named try-its below carry **time, materials, safety, the fun, the skill**. Library, walk, museum, kitchen, yard, and lab-path neighbor *are* the practice — they do not replace the fair-test redesign move.

### Talk box

**Opening (locked):** “What did we change on purpose — what stayed the same — and can we tell for sure?”

**Follow-ups:**

1. What are we measuring — what do you *see* or *time* or *weigh*?
2. What does the notebook / table show for each condition?
3. How do you know from *this* trial — can we tell for sure it was the change we named?
4. What else was different — what would we redesign — what else could explain these numbers?
5. You may change your mind after the redesign. Try another look. Insufficient evidence is allowed.

**How to wait.** After you ask, a slow three is a convention, not a sacrament (elementary science classroom origin). After they stop, wait again. If they are mid-measure or mid-claim, do not cut them off. Look at the materials, the table, or the notebook, not at the adolescent, if the silence is hard.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the question was vague; wait-time was zero; they are hunting a vocabulary word because you asked name-first; they are guessing what you want; the materials never landed; they offered a book-only guess; they think science means “repeat the parent’s explanation”; they invent neat rows because the trial was messy; they are bracing for an AP rubric sentence. Pose, wait, and point back at the notebook — not fill the silence with the right vocabulary, and not turn the table into a fight.

### In-home try-its (FAIR bank)

**FAIR-01 Confound vs Fair Side-by-Side**

- **Band:** both
- **Time:** 35–50 min
- **Materials:** Same question; Card A confounded design; Card B fair design (paper or safe mini-try); notebook
- **Safety:** Prefer paper + one safe try; adult present for any apparatus
- **The fun:** Spotting the trick next to a clean design feels like detective work with real materials.
- **The skill:** Predict which design can “tell for sure”; mark extra variables on A; run B if safe or redesign A on paper.
- **Steps:** Pose one question. Show A and B. Mark confounds on A. Fill Change / Measure / Same for B. Blank table before any run. Talk box. Exit: “We can / cannot tell for sure because ___.”
- **Error patterns:** “More variables = more scientific”; skipping the teach of the rule; inventing results for B without running it.

**FAIR-02 Name IV, DV, Constants**

- **Band:** both
- **Time:** 30–45 min
- **Materials:** Question card; three-column sticky; planned safe try; notebook
- **Safety:** Per try; adult gates start until sticky is filled when hazards are present
- **The fun:** Cards make the plan visible before the mess — then the try lands clean.
- **The skill:** Fill IV / DV / constants *before* materials move; adult checks; then try.
- **Exit ticket:** Three-column sticky photographed or copied into the notebook.
- **Error patterns:** Constants list empty; IV that is actually a pile of changes; starting early “just to see.”

**FAIR-03 Redesign After Confound Caught**

- **Band:** both
- **Time:** 35–50 min
- **Materials:** Yesterday’s confounded notebook page; blank redesign page; optional second try materials
- **Safety:** Per redesign; do not shame-spiral into unauthorized mixes
- **The fun:** Repairing a broken design is satisfying — the second page is progress, not failure.
- **The skill:** Identify confound; redesign; optional second try; compare claims carefully without deleting the first page.
- **Exit ticket:** Old confound named + new constant list.
- **Error patterns:** Shame spiral; keeping old claim without redesign; inventing neat rows to “fix” the first page.

**FAIR-04 “Insufficient Evidence” Is a Valid Outcome**

- **Band:** both
- **Time:** 30–45 min
- **Materials:** Sparse data set (two points only) or a thin notebook page from a prior try; CER crumb card
- **Safety:** None if using prior data
- **The fun:** Saying “not enough yet” feels like intellectual honesty — rare and powerful.
- **The skill:** Attempt a claim; conclude insufficient; name what additional data would be needed; refuse inventing points to finish.
- **Exit ticket:** “Need ___ before claiming ___.”
- **Error patterns:** Forcing a strong claim; inventing points; filing a model summary as the missing data.

**FAIR-05 Multiple Trials, Same Fair Design**

- **Band:** both
- **Time:** 35–50 min
- **Materials:** Fair design card; space for three trials; notebook table
- **Safety:** Per materials
- **The fun:** Watching variation appear across honest repeats teaches that science is not one lucky run.
- **The skill:** Repeat the same fair design; table the variation; talk about range without secretly changing the IV mid-way.
- **Exit ticket:** Trial range + one claim with caution.
- **Error patterns:** Changing procedure mid-way “to help”; dropping the inconvenient trial; smoothing numbers to look professional.

**FAIR-06 Transfer: New Materials, Same Rule**

- **Band:** 17–18 (stretch 15–16)
- **Time:** 35–50 min
- **Materials:** New safe phenomenon (e.g. paper helicopter after a prior ramp or sink try); design sketch paper
- **Safety:** Adult check gate before run
- **The fun:** Owning the design on new materials feels like real agency.
- **The skill:** Learner designs a fair test alone after a short rule review; adult checks before run. Age the transfer *shape* from classroom studies — do not claim equal sample sizes or kitchen percentages.
- **Exit ticket:** Design sketch approved or revised.
- **Error patterns:** Copying prior constants blindly when irrelevant; skipping the adult check; treating transfer as a race.

### Out-of-home try-it

**OUT-03 Stream or Puddle Clarity Soft**

- **Band:** both
- **Time:** 35–50 min on site
- **Materials:** Clear bottle; optional DIY turbidity sense; notebook; adult
- **Safety:** Shore-based default; adult present; never enter unsafe water; no storm-drain “tests”; no unknown reagents; wash hands; local rules
- **The fun:** Edge looking is real-world science without a scavenger packet.
- **The skill:** Observe; compare two sites if safe with *one* named change (e.g. upstream vs downstream edge, or sunny vs shady puddle edge); systems note of watershed soft; refuse chemical dumping tests.
- **Exit ticket:** Site A vs B observation + what changed on purpose + boundary crumb.
- **Error patterns:** Wading floodwater; inventing clarity numbers; changing three site features at once and claiming one cause; scavenger worksheet dump.

### Blocked for a new move / mixed for when to use it

**Blocked (one new move):** FAIR-02 or FAIR-01 alone until the student can name change + same + measure without hunting your face for the “right” word. Then one redesign (FAIR-03).

**Mixed (when the move holds):** Pair FAIR-05 with a stranger-can-read check from Chapter 2; or FAIR-04 with a denser CER crumb that ends in “insufficient”; or Friday FAIR-06 at 17–18 with a short warrant phrase. Pair OUT-03 in the same week when the home fair test is steady.

### One incorrect example to diagnose

**Illustration (composite, not a reported family):** A sixteen-year-old runs two plant setups. One gets a sunny window *and* extra water *and* a bigger pot. The other sits in a closet with less water in a small pot. The notebook announces: “Light makes plants grow — proved.” Rows look neat. One plant species differs. A chat window offered “typical growth numbers” that somehow match the table.

**Next move:** “Can we tell for sure it was the light?” Wait. Point at water and pot and species. Redesign with FAIR-03: same water, same pot size, same starting health; only light exposure differs. Keep the original page; add a new dated page. Trash the chat numbers kindly. Do not paste a generated growth chart as “our” data. Do not file the hour as Chemistry credit or as a brand title on a transcript.

- “Can we tell for sure? ___ because ___.”

### What “fair” does not mean this week

Fair does not mean “the student guessed the parent’s preferred answer.” Fair does not mean “every trial matched.” Fair does not mean “we used science words.” Fair does not mean “we proved the textbook.” Fair means one deliberate change, a real record, an honest answer to “can we tell for sure?”, and a redesign when you cannot. Disagreement between two honest trials is data. Deleting the unpopular trial is not. Inventing neat rows is not. Filing a generated image as the result is not.

If Chapter 1 still needs systems-before-tidy-idea, or Chapter 2 still needs stranger-readable notebooks, those moves are not leftovers — they are the floor under this chapter. Place by skill. A path through high-school fair-test redesign is the promise. A diploma is not. A percentile is not. An AP score is not the only path that counts.



### Confirmation bias (“prove the textbook”)

A packet that says “prove that ___” trains confirmation theater. Prefer questions that could come out otherwise. Prefer redesign when the first try cannot tell for sure. Prefer “insufficient evidence” over inventing the expected slope. If an AP-style stem appears at 17–18, label it occasional stretch — not Tuesday’s identity. Control-of-variables teaching ages the *shape* of clear instruction with materials; it does not license importing classroom percentages as this adolescent’s score, and it does not declare discovery dead.

### America’s Lab Report grain (labelled school-origin)

Integrated looking — clear outcome, sequenced with talk, reflection — beats isolated lab-day theater. Borrow the design principles. Do not crown a kitchen franchise. One fair redesign with talk beats five demos with no notebook.


## For the student

Fair test means you change **one** thing on purpose, you measure what happens, and you keep everything else the same on purpose. Then you ask: can we tell for sure? If you cannot, you redesign once. Messy first pages stay. New pages get new dates.

The change is sometimes called the **independent variable** — the thing you decide to make different. What you measure is sometimes called the **dependent variable**. The things you keep the same are **controls** or **constants**. Those are tool words. What matters is the try on the table and the numbers in your notebook.

At 15–18 the bar rises a little from middle grades: a stranger should be able to reconstruct your trial from the notebook; “insufficient evidence” is a brave answer; inventing neat rows is not looking; a chat window’s “typical results” are not your data.

### Tiny worked example

**Question:** Does the ramp surface change how far the object travels?

**Change (IV):** surface — towel vs foil.

**Measure (DV):** distance from the bottom of the ramp, in centimeters.

**Same:** same mass, same ramp height, same start line, same release (no push), same floor.

**Table (drawn before the race):**

| Trial | Surface | Distance (cm) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | towel | | |
| 2 | towel | | |
| 3 | foil | | |

You fill the empty cells from what you measure. Messy real numbers beat invented neat ones. If you also changed the height, you cannot blame the surface — redesign. If you only have two messy points and a big gap, say insufficient evidence and name what you need next.

### Try 1

Pick FAIR-01 or FAIR-02. Fill Change / Measure / Same cards before materials move. Run at least two trials per condition. Point at one row when you say what happened. Answer the locked question out loud.

### Try 2

Take a confounded page (yours or a scripted Card A). Catch the confound. Redesign once on a new dated page (FAIR-03). Optional: run the redesign. Keep both pages.

### Explain it back

In your own words: What did we change on purpose — what stayed the same — and can we tell for sure? Point at the notebook while you talk.

### Challenge

Design a fair test for a *new* safe question (paper helicopter, dissolving time with adult-controlled temperature, insulation wrap). Get an adult check before you run. At 17–18, try FAIR-06 transfer without copying last week’s constants blindly.

## If it isn’t clicking

**Diagnostic 1 — Confound pride that will not budge.**  
They keep changing two things and calling it “more scientific.”  
*Next move:* Paper-only FAIR-01. Mark extra variables in red. No materials until they can say “we cannot tell for sure because ___.” Then one fair mini-try.

**Diagnostic 2 — Empty constants list / IV that is a pile.**  
They name a change that is really three changes.  
*Next move:* FAIR-02 sticky gate. Adult refuses start until three real constants are named. Practice on yesterday’s apparatus without running.

**Diagnostic 3 — Invent-neat or force-a-claim.**  
Rows look too perfect, or they invent points to finish a CER.  
*Next move:* FAIR-04 with a deliberately sparse table. Practice the sentence “Need ___ before claiming ___.” Keep messy real data. No chat numbers. No generated charts.

**When to slow down.** If Chapter 2 stranger-notebook habits are weak, repair those first for a few hours. Fair test without a readable record becomes theater.

**When to go ahead.** When they catch a confound unaided and redesign once with a constant list that would survive a stranger’s eye, move toward multiple trials (FAIR-05) and transfer (FAIR-06). Mix last week’s redesign into Friday’s exit.

**When to get a human tutor.** If wait-time never happens, if every hour becomes a fight, or if safety gates keep failing, a co-op lab mentor or a trusted science teacher for a few sessions can model the redesign talk. A chatbot is not a tutor for inventing the missing rows.

Correct a wrong reason without crushing the look. “Never correct the adolescent” is not this book’s rule. Shame is not the rule either. Point at the materials. Ask the locked question. Redesign.

## Why this matters

The diagram looks finished. Labels are neat. Someone announces that this *is* the cell — or the energy flow — or the matter cycle — as if the poster were the world.

That announcement is not a character flaw. It is what happens when a picture feels complete and the mind treats the picture as the thing. The hour’s job is smaller and kinder: draw a boundary; name what is inside and outside; mark inputs and outputs; say what the model leaves out; ask how a change at the boundary or inside would matter. Cells, energy, and matter show up here as *system objects* you can look at this week — not as a Biology or Chemistry survey dump. Chapters 5 and 6 carry domain vehicles. This chapter owns the systems move.

Systems looking is how crosscutting ideas become a weekly practice without becoming a laminated slogan. Adolescents ages 15–18 can draw a boundary with help, name one input and one output, and say one thing the model leaves out. At 15–16 the boundary is scaffolded; feedback appears soft. At 17–18 they revise a model against data, name what the model leaves out without coaching, and ask how a scale shift would change the question. They do not become systems thinkers because a parent bought a wall poster of “everything is a system,” and useful models do not appear by copying organelle lists with no phenomenon on the table.

What this idea unlocks is every later explanation that can survive a second look. A cell diagram that never meets a question about inputs and outputs stays art. An energy arrow sketch that never meets a lamp, a plant, or a motion try stays decoration. A food-web craft that never marks which links were observed stays a worksheet biome. One owned diagram — boundary, arrows, “leaves out” sticky — beside a notebook page is how explanation stays honest at high school.

Two refusals sit beside the idea, or the week becomes noise.

“Everything is a system” without a diagram the learner owns is not the program. The slogan feels deep and teaches nothing you can check. Activity mania — five models, no talk about what they leave out — is entertainment. Coverage as the hour — every organelle, every trophic level, every energy form by Friday — is a map mistaken for depth. Model-as-truth is not the program either. “The model *is* the real system” collapses the useful gap between representation and world. A generated “cell under the microscope” filed as Figure 1 is not the student’s observation. A model summary from a screen is not today’s look.

You do not need to be a scientist. You do need to put a system on the table, ask where the boundary is and what the model leaves out, wait, and point back at the notebook when the picture gets ahead of the evidence.

This week you can learn to ask for the boundary before the vocabulary list. Today the student can sketch one system, label one input and one output, and name one thing left out.

A third refusal belongs in the same breath: coverage as systems week. Ten labelled diagrams with no phenomenon on the table teach handwriting. One jar beside a blank page, or one body-subsystem card beside a pulse count with consent and hygiene, or one energy-arrow sketch beside a lamp they turned on, teaches the practice. ## For the parent: understand it yourself

Many adults remember systems as a poster word or as a middle-school food-web craft. That is ordinary. Five minutes here, then the warm-up, is enough for tomorrow. The student still draws the boundary and names the limit.

**Everyday picture.** A subway map. It shows connections. It leaves out street trees, exact walking times, and the smell of the platform. Nobody calls the map a lie because it leaves those out — unless someone treats the map as the city. That gap is the systems habit in ordinary clothes.

**Precise picture.** In high-school science looking, a **system** is a set of parts and interactions you choose to treat together for a question. A **system model** is a representation — diagram, physical build, or mathematical-light sketch — that shows those parts and flows on purpose. Framework teaching materials treat systems and system models, energy and matter, structure and function, and scale as crosscutting moves meant to be *integrated*, not as three separate quiz lists. *Taking Science to School* notes that useful models need instruction — they are not a creativity elective with no science object. Borrow the moves. Leave the PE dump.

Five kitchen moves carry the practice:

- **Boundary:** What is inside this system for *this* question — and what is outside?
- **Parts and interactions:** What pieces matter, and how do they affect each other?
- **Inputs / outputs:** What matter or energy crosses the boundary — in which direction?
- **Feedback (soft):** When a change inside feeds back to amplify or stabilize — careful, plain grain; no medical advice.
- **Leaves out:** What does this model simplify, hide, or refuse to claim — and when would that omission change a prediction?

At 15–16, boundary + one input + one output + one “leaves out” sentence is enough. At 17–18, add feedback soft, scale shift, and revise once when data disagree. Still refuse AP-scale modeling reports as the weekly default. Cells, energy, and matter are *objects* for the move this week — not a year of Biochemistry.

A second precise picture: a closed jar with soil and a seedling. Boundary drawn around the jar. Inputs: light energy, maybe water you add. Outputs: heat, maybe water vapor. “Leaves out” sticky: soil microbes not drawn; CO₂ exchange not measured; this is not a proof of full photosynthesis biochemistry. That hour is systems looking. It is not Chapter 5’s Biology vehicle week — though the same jar may return there.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. *Slogan without a line.* “Everything is a system!” No boundary drawn. Hear empty depth. Ask: “Where is the boundary for *this* question?” Require a line before vocabulary.

2. *Model as decoration.* Beautiful cell craft; no inputs/outputs; no “leaves out”; no link to a question. Hear craft without science looking. Require the sticky before the fridge photo.

3. *Generated cell / gel / micrograph as observation.* An image tool produces a “cell under the microscope” or a fake gel. It lands as Figure 1. Hear generated image ≠ data. Label a schematic if you use one, then put it away before the look — or skip. Real prepared slide, printed micrograph from a book, or soft alternative. Never file a generated result as the student’s observation.

4. *Model summary as the observation.* A chatbot paragraph describes what “usually happens” in a cell or an ecosystem; that paragraph is filed as today’s looking. Hear model-summary-as-observation. Trash it kindly. Blank page. Eyes on the jar, the produce, or the lamp. Then build the diagram *after* the look and name a limit.

5. *“The model is the truth.”* Student defends the drawing against the thermometer, the mass change, or the leaf observation. Hear representation collapsed into world. Say: “Data can win. What would we revise once?” Keep both pages — prediction and result.

A sixth you will also hear: “Just tell me what to draw.” Point at the phenomenon. Ask what a stranger would need to see in the model to understand *this* question. Do not grab the pencil and finish their thinking.

A seventh, quieter miss: coverage as systems week — ten labelled diagrams with no phenomenon on the table. Hear the map replacing the hour. One jar, one body-subsystem card, or one energy-arrow sketch beside something they can point to teaches the practice. Depth is the hour.

**Five-to-eight-minute parent warm-up**

Do this alone with tomorrow’s lookable — a jar, a lamp, a body-subsystem card, or a grocery produce item — before the lesson.

Minute 1. Sketch a quick boundary around the object. List two things inside and two outside.

Minute 2. Add one input arrow and one output arrow. Label matter or energy in plain words.

Minute 3. Write one “leaves out” sticky: “This leaves out ___, which matters if ___.” Place it on the sketch before you sit down with the student.

Minute 4. Practice the locked opening: “Where is the boundary of this system — and what does this model leave out?” Count a slow three. Look at the sketch, not at an imaginary adolescent.

Minute 5–8. Decide SYS-01 (boundary first) or SYS-02 (inputs and outputs) as the first try. If fair-test habits from Chapter 3 are still thin, keep one fair test in the week and let the system diagram serve that trial — do not abandon the notebook spine for craft week. If you are heading toward Biology next, keep today’s diagram reclaimable as a vehicle, not as a finished year.

## How to teach it this week

Science Hour stays the five beats: open / system or question + RAMP if needed; short look or prior-idea card; one try / measure / record *or* systems probe; talk box + denser CER crumb; exit ticket. This week the open and try beats can own a systems diagram — or a diagram beside a short trial. Total often ~30–50 minutes. Stop while a look remains.

**Warm-up looking (3–7 min).** Phenomenon visible first when looking is the point — jar, lamp, leaf, produce, printed watershed map. Cover captions that already explain the model. Ask what they notice. Wait a slow three (classroom-origin wait; use the move, not a kitchen coefficient).

**Frame the system (4–8 min).** Draw or reveal a blank boundary. At 15–16, co-draw the line and list inside/outside together. At 17–18, the student draws; you ask what the model leaves out. Refuse “the universe” as the default boundary. Adult present for heat, electricity, outdoor water, chemicals, glassware, and living-specimen work.

**Try / probe / record (15–25 min).** Student adds arrows, measures one flow if the try allows (temperature change, mass, growth notice, light on/off), or runs a soft structure–function probe. You do not grab the pencil for the “right” organelle set. Messy owned diagrams beat copied poster perfection. Pair with a short fair test when the question needs it — Chapter 3 still holds.

**Talk + denser CER crumb (5–10 min).** Claim about what is happening in the system. Evidence that points at the diagram *or* a notebook row. One warrant phrase naming a scientific idea already on the table (energy transfer, matter conservation as weighing honesty, structure–function). Name what the model leaves out. Chapter 7 densifies; this week the boundary and the limit own the hour.

**Exit ticket (2–5 min).** Boundary sentence + one input + one output; or “leaves out ___, which matters if ___”; or scale A answers ___ / scale B answers ___. Diagnostic wrong noted by you for tomorrow — not a graded performance.

**Exact wording you can say**

- “Where is the boundary of this system — and what does this model leave out?”
- “What is inside — what is outside — for *this* question?”
- “What crosses the boundary — in or out — matter or energy?”
- “What would change if we moved the boundary?”
- “What does this model leave out — and when would that matter?”
- “You may revise the diagram after another look.”

**Age-band moves: 15–16 / 17–18**

**15–16.** Scaffolded boundary; one input and one output labelled; one “leaves out” sentence prompted; feedback introduced soft if ready; denser CER oral or short write with warrant crumb; still refuse full systems-essay theater as every hour.

**17–18.** More independent system model; name what the model leaves out without a prompt list; feedback soft with stabilizing vs amplifying careful language; scale shift; revise once when data disagree; still kitchen-scale; still refuse AP modeling FRQ as weekly spine. If boundary habits are weak, stay on 15–16 SYS longer. Skill, not birthday.

**Student tries today.** Sketch one system, label one input and one output, name one thing left out.

**First try-it.** SYS-01 Boundary First, then SYS-02 Inputs and Outputs. Fade help: co-draw → they draw while you watch → they name leaves-out → scale shift or structure–function at week’s end.

**When to stop talking.** When you are narrating every arrow while their page is blank. One question. Stop. Look at the jar or the diagram if the silence is hard.

## Practice that actually builds learning

Named try-its below carry **time, materials, safety, the fun, the skill**. Library, walk, museum, kitchen, yard, grocery, and lab-path neighbor *are* the practice — they do not replace the systems-looking move.

### Talk box

**Opening (locked):** “Where is the boundary of this system — and what does this model leave out?”

**Follow-ups:**

1. What do you *see* inside the boundary — what is outside?
2. What does the notebook / diagram show for inputs and outputs?
3. How do you know from *this* model or trial — what warrant ties the arrows to the idea?
4. What else could be crossing the boundary — can we tell for sure — what does the model leave out?
5. You may revise the diagram. Try another look. Move the boundary once and say what changes.

**How to wait.** After you ask, a slow three is a convention, not a sacrament (elementary science classroom origin). After they stop, wait again. If they are mid-draw or mid-claim, do not cut them off. Look at the materials, the diagram, or the notebook, not at the adolescent, if the silence is hard.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the question was vague; wait-time was zero; they are hunting a vocabulary word because you asked name-first; they are guessing what you want; the materials never landed; they offered a book-only guess; they think science means “repeat the parent’s explanation”; they think looking means “say what the screen already told me”; they are bracing for an AP rubric sentence. Pose, wait, and point back at the diagram — not fill the silence with the right organelle list, and not turn the table into a fight.

### In-home try-its (SYS bank)

**SYS-01 Boundary First**

- **Band:** both
- **Time:** 30–45 min
- **Materials:** Phenomenon (jar garden, aquarium photo, house vent, watershed map print); blank paper; notebook
- **Safety:** Observation only unless a paired try is approved; adult present for living specimens
- **The fun:** Drawing the line turns a familiar object into a question you can own.
- **The skill:** Draw boundary; list inside/outside; revise after talk; refuse “the universe” as default.
- **Steps:** Phenomenon visible. Locked opening. Student draws. Talk. Exit: boundary sentence + two outside items.
- **Error patterns:** No line drawn; everything inside; copying a textbook diagram without a question.

**SYS-02 Inputs and Outputs Arrows**

- **Band:** both
- **Time:** 30–45 min
- **Materials:** System diagram from SYS-01 or new; arrow stickies; notebook
- **Safety:** None for diagram; per any paired measurement
- **The fun:** Arrows make invisible flows feel visible — then honesty about what you did not measure.
- **The skill:** Add energy/matter arrows; label direction; ask what is conserved vs transformed in plain words (honesty about model limits).
- **Exit ticket:** Two labelled arrows.
- **Error patterns:** Arrows with no labels; perpetual-motion vibes; claiming measured CO₂ from a jar you never instrumented.

**SYS-03 Feedback Soft**

- **Band:** 17–18 (introduce 15–16)
- **Time:** 35–50 min
- **Materials:** Thermostat analogy card; body-system or other plain feedback example; notebook
- **Safety:** Diagram only; no medical advice; no diagnosing family members
- **The fun:** Spotting a loop feels like seeing the hidden gear in a machine.
- **The skill:** Identify a change that feeds back; sketch loop; mark stabilizing vs amplifying carefully.
- **Exit ticket:** Loop sketch with one sentence.
- **Error patterns:** Every loop called “balance”; health diagnosis from a diagram; climate-as-policy sermon as the hour.

**SYS-04 What the Model Leaves Out**

- **Band:** both
- **Time:** 30–45 min
- **Materials:** Simple model (food-web card, cell-as-factory metaphor card labelled *metaphor*); notebook
- **Safety:** None
- **The fun:** Naming omissions makes the student the editor of the model, not its victim.
- **The skill:** List three omissions; ask which omission would change a prediction; revise model note.
- **Exit ticket:** “Leaves out ___, which matters if ___.”
- **Error patterns:** Treating model as the thing; mocking models as “just lies”; refusing all models as useless.

**SYS-05 Scale Shift**

- **Band:** both
- **Time:** 30–45 min
- **Materials:** Same phenomenon at two scales (leaf ↔ canopy; molecule picture ↔ beaker — picture honesty); notebook
- **Safety:** Pictures OK; no unauthorized chemistry
- **The fun:** Switching scale feels like zooming a camera — different questions come into focus.
- **The skill:** Name which questions each scale answers; refuse forcing one scale’s answer onto the other.
- **Exit ticket:** Scale A answers ___; scale B answers ___.
- **Error patterns:** “Atoms explain everything today”; ignoring macro measurement; filing a molecular animation as today’s observation.

**SYS-06 Structure–Function Probe**

- **Band:** both
- **Time:** 30–45 min
- **Materials:** One structure (leaf stomata print, bird-bone image, tool hinge); optional magnifier + real leaf; notebook
- **Safety:** Magnifier; living no-harm; wash hands
- **The fun:** Guessing function from structure, then asking what evidence would be needed, feels like real inquiry.
- **The skill:** Propose function; name needed evidence; optional observe real leaf; refuse just-so sermons.
- **Exit ticket:** Structure + hypothesized function + needed evidence.
- **Error patterns:** Just-so stories with no test; teleology sermon; inventing micrograph “evidence.”

### Out-of-home try-its

**OUT-04 Grocery Produce Structure–Function**

- **Band:** both
- **Time:** 30–45 min
- **Materials:** Produce; notebook; adult
- **Safety:** Food hygiene; knives adult-controlled if any cutting; venue rules
- **The fun:** The produce aisle becomes a lab without glassware.
- **The skill:** Structure observation; hypothesized function; what evidence would be needed beyond the aisle.
- **Exit ticket:** Structure + function guess + needed evidence.
- **Error patterns:** Eating as the only method; unsupervised knife work; scavenger worksheet dump.

**EARTH-02 Water in the System (neighbor week)**

- **Band:** both

- **The skill:** Boundary watershed; inputs/outputs; human-use note without culture-war dump.
- **Exit ticket:** Watershed boundary + one flow.
- **Error patterns:** Worksheet packet scavenger; inventing stream data; policy sermon as the hour.

## For the student

Systems looking means you choose a boundary for a question, you name what is inside and outside, you mark what crosses the boundary, and you say what your model leaves out. Then you ask whether the notebook agrees.

A **system** is the set of parts and interactions you are treating together for *this* question. A **model** is a picture or build that shows those parts and flows on purpose. Models are useful because they simplify. Models are honest when you name the simplification.

At 15–18 the bar rises from middle grades: the diagram is yours, not a colouring sheet; “leaves out” is required, not optional; a generated cell picture is not your observation; a chat summary is not today’s look.

### Tiny worked example

**Phenomenon:** A closed jar with a small plant on a sunny sill.

**Boundary:** The jar (glass + lid + air + soil + plant inside).

**Outside:** The room, the sun, you.

**Input:** Light energy from the sun (and water if you open the lid to add it).

**Output:** Heat to the room; water vapor if you measure condensation — say so only if you saw it.

**Leaves out:** Soil microbes not drawn; exact gases not measured; this jar is not a proof of the full photosynthesis equation.

**Claim crumb:** Light energy enters the jar system; the plant is part of how matter and energy are transformed inside — evidence: growth notice / condensation / temperature crumb from *this* notebook — warrant: energy transfer / matter in systems — limitation: we did not measure gases.

### Try 1

### Try 2

### Explain it back

### Challenge

## For the parent: understand it yourself

## Practice that actually builds learning

Named try-its below carry **time, materials, safety, the fun, the skill**. Library, walk, museum, zoo, kitchen, yard, and lab-path neighbor *are* the practice — they do not replace systems-and-claim looking on a living system.

### Talk box

**Opening (locked):** “What is happening in this living system — and which notebook evidence makes you say that?”

**Follow-ups:**

1. What do you *see* or *measure* in this living system?
2. What does the notebook / table show — which row or notice?
3. How do you know from *this* — what warrant ties the evidence to the idea?
4. What else could explain these notices — what does the model leave out — can we tell for sure?
5. You may change your mind after another look. Redesign once if the try was confounded. Insufficient evidence is allowed.

**How to wait.** After you ask, a slow three is a convention, not a sacrament (elementary science classroom origin). After they stop, wait again. If they are mid-measure or mid-claim, do not cut them off. Look at the plant, the plot, the card, or the notebook, not at the adolescent, if the silence is hard.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the question was vague; wait-time was zero; they are hunting a vocabulary word because you asked name-first; they are guessing what you want; the living system never landed; they offered a book-only guess; they think Biology means “repeat the parent’s explanation”; they invent neat growth numbers because the trial was messy; they are bracing for an AP rubric sentence. Pose, wait, and point back at the notebook — not fill the silence with the right kingdom list, and not turn the table into a fight.

Hearing those wrong turns is teaching. Filling the silence with an equation lecture is not.

Picture two Fridays. In one, a dramatic fizz sits in a photo album labelled “Chemistry finished,” with no RAMP, no mass table, and a transcript line that claims Lab. In the other, a small planned reaction or a ramp trial has a RAMP card, goggles when splash risk is real, a table with units, a claim with a warrant, and an honesty tag: picture — or lab-path neighbor planned for next month. Both rooms feel busy. Only one taught looking you can defend to a stranger.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

Many adults remember chemistry as a memorized list of elements and a fear of the word *mole*. Many remember physics as “the class after you finished algebra.” Rust on both is normal. It is not a character flaw. Five to eight minutes of this section, then the warm-up, is enough for tomorrow. The student still measures.

**Everyday picture — Chemistry.** Three adult-approved household solids sit in labelled dishes: salt, sugar, baking soda. No tasting. No mixing beyond the plan. The student records color, grain, whether each dissolves in a small measured water volume you approve. Names come *after* properties. Later in the week, a tiny vinegar-and-soda fizz in an open cup (or a sealed-bag contrast for conservation weighing) runs only after RAMP. On the table sits a card: *Kitchen change-of-matter picture ≠ Chemistry credit.* That honesty is part of the lesson.

**Everyday picture — Physics.** A ball rolls across a table and slows. You ask what changed in the motion. If they say “it ran out of force,” you do not scold. You put another look on the table: same ball, smoother path, or a gentle push after it slows — and name the idea just-in-time: forces change motion; they do not ride inside the ball like fuel.[^6] A stopwatch, a metre stick or tape, a simple table, and later a slope sentence turn the look into rate talk. Algebra sits beside the graph when ready. A qualitative path is still physics looking when the idea is clear and the numbers are honest.

**Precise picture.** *Chemistry as Hour vehicle* means properties before names; a reaction you can stop; conservation as weighing honesty (open vs closed systems); concentration sense with food-safe dilution, not stock acids; mole as “amount of substance” meaning before a drill packet; and a lab-path neighbor planner when glassware, burners, or regulated chemicals are needed. *Physics as Hour vehicle* means change-of-motion (not force-as-fuel); energy transfer storyboard with thermal honesty; wave patterns without lasers in the default bank; circuits with battery packs only or skip to a labelled simulation / OUT; graph slope as rate. Both vehicles use the twin spines from earlier chapters: systems boundary and denser CER from *this* notebook.

**Safety is not optional at fifteen.** Turning 15 or 17 does not unlock unsupervised mixing. Adult present for heat, electricity beyond toys, outdoor water, chemicals, glassware, and any kitchen chemistry picture. RAMP before the try. No unmarked bottles. No bleach mixes. No drain cleaners. No alcohol flames. No sealed pressurized fizz bottles scaled for drama. No lasers in the default bank. Living things: care, return, wash hands; mold cultivation refused by default; home vertebrate dissection refused as default. Regulated chemicals, Bunsen-style burners, and electrophoresis belong on a lab-path neighbor, not in a default home bank.[^1] [^2]

**Algebra as tool, not wall.** When the student can read a table and a graph, slope-as-rate and simple proportional talk earn a place beside the idea. When they cannot yet, keep the qualitative path and the notebook honest. Do not make the Science Hour a graphing unit with no system on the table. Do not dump calculus as a gate for ordinary change-of-motion looking. AP Physics 1 is algebra-based; Physics C is calculus-based — both are College Board vendor edges, not this week’s required identity.[^7]

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. *Force-as-fuel.* “The ball stopped because it ran out of force / energy fuel.” Repair: another look; name that forces change motion; ask what was different between trials; point at the table. Do not equation-punish.

2. *Kitchen-as-Chemistry-credit.* “We did a volcano, so it’s Chemistry with Lab.” Repair: honesty card; list what a supervised secondary path would add; plan CHEM-05 / OUT-06 if credit-path lab skills are a real family goal.

3. *Unsupervised “they’re old enough.”* Learner alone with heat, glass, or mixing. Repair: stop; adult present for the entire try; RAMP or skip.

4. *Algebra-as-wall / bare “AP Physics” mash.* “We can’t do physics until Precalculus” or transcript line “AP Physics.” Repair: qualitative path now; algebra beside the idea when ready; title **Physics** or **Physics with Lab**; name Physics 1 / 2 / C only as exam edges if they sit them.

5. *Invent-data / generated curve as result / coverage hour / AP packet as every Friday.* Neat rows that never happened; AI titration curve filed as theirs; vocabulary march with no try; FRQ every session. Repair: stop; honesty; redesign or labelled secondary data; one vehicle try; occasional WRITE-06 stretch only when denser CER is stable — point back to the Science Hour AI box.

A sixth: *name-first vocabulary dump* — “define molarity” before any property or dilution look. Repair: properties or dilution series first; names just-in-time.

**What “With Lab” would need that a picture lacks (say once).** Supervised investigation that actually happened; dated notebook with question, method, data, claim; adult present for higher-hazard work; often glassware controls and assessment of lab skills a stranger could recognize. America’s Lab Report does not convert a baking-soda picture into Chemistry credit.[^3] PhET or a vendor note that experiments are “encouraged but not mandatory” is a teaching feature or publisher copy — not an automatic lab credit for every reader of a transcript. Keep the three stacks separate: lawful (your state’s map), safe (RAMP + adult), readable to a stranger (title + evidence).


**Five-to-eight-minute parent warm-up**

Do this alone, with tomorrow’s apparatus.

Minute 1. Decide Chem *or* Phys for this week’s vehicle. One is enough.

Minute 2. Complete a RAMP card for the planned try — or circle skip / redesign / lab-path neighbor. If you cannot Minimize, you do not run.

Minute 3. Write the kitchen honesty sentence if Chem is on the table: “This is a picture, not Chemistry credit.” Put the card where they can see it.

Minute 4. Practice the locked opening aloud. Practice one change-of-motion sentence without force-as-fuel, or one conservation sentence about open vs closed.

Minute 5. Cut denser CER to claim + notebook pointer + because-the-idea. Save multi-trial synthesis for later.

Minute 6. Decide band exit: 15–16 scaffolded RAMP + one try + oral CER crumb; 17–18 more independent design check before run + uncertainty crumb or slope sentence.

Minute 7. Lay out goggles if splash/heat/glass risk is real. Clear the food surface if non-food chemicals will touch it — or keep the try food-safe and labelled.

Minute 8. Put the model answer away. You are ready. They measure.

## How to teach it this week

The Science Hour chapter holds the full session shape. Here is the same five beats scaled to Chem or Phys as this week’s vehicle. Keep thirty to fifty minutes. If a day only holds thirty: RAMP (if needed) + one measurement or one conservation / motion look + exit ticket.

**1. Open / system or question + RAMP (3–7 min).** Name today’s outcome in ordinary words: “By the end you can say what changed in the matter (or the motion) and point at evidence from this trial — safely.” If heat, chemicals, glass, or electricity beyond toys: finish RAMP before materials leave the cupboard. Adult present for the whole try.

**2. Short look or prior-idea card (3–8 min).** What do you already think will happen? Hear force-as-fuel or “mass vanishes” without scolding. Do not dump the textbook answer yet. Optional: one labelled secondary figure with honesty card — not your data.

**3. One try / measure / record (15–25 min).** One Chem *or* Phys card — not five stations. Record in the notebook a stranger could almost read. Table before graph. No invent-data to make the graph pretty.

**4. Talk box + denser CER (5–10 min).** Locked opening. Wait. Point at the row. Warrant: why would that measurement count for *this* idea? One limitation or alternative when the band is ready.

**5. Exit ticket (2–5 min).** Spoken CER crumb, RAMP hazard named, open-vs-closed sentence, slope sentence, or honesty tag (picture / lab-path). Done-enough is right, or wrong-with-a-reason you can use tomorrow.

**Band 15–16.** Adult scaffolds RAMP and IV/DV. One fair-ish compare or one focused measurement. Denser CER with warrant; one limitation *or* alternative prompted. Kitchen pictures stay small volume. Circuits: battery kit or skip. AP labelled only if the family already chose that edge — not this week’s spine.

**Band 17–18.** Learner fills more of the RAMP and design before you check the gate. Multi-trial comparison; uncertainty crumb; mole-as-amount one calculation with meaning; slope-as-rate tied to claim; occasional OUT-06 lab-path neighbor when home cannot host. Still refuse AP as weekly spine. Still refuse invent-data.

**Exact wording you can say**

- “Before we open anything — RAMP. What could go wrong, and how do we make it smaller?”
- “What will you change on purpose? What will you measure? What stays the same?”
- “What changed in the matter or the motion — and how do we know safely from this trial?”
- “Point at the row. Why does that number count for the idea we named?”
- “This week is a kitchen picture — not Chemistry credit. If we need a supervised bench later, we plan the neighbor.”
- “You may change your mind when we look again.”

**When to stop talking.** After the locked question, wait. If they are mid-measure, do not narrate the result. If the silence is hard, look at the table or the RAMP card, not at their face. Struggle before rescue: ask, wait, hint (a question or a named idea), then model. Do not grab the answer. Do not paste a machine’s observation as theirs.

**Fade help.** Week one you may co-fill RAMP. Week two they fill Recognize and Assess; you check Minimize. By a stable month they run the card and you only gate the go/skip decision for higher-hazard tries.

**A sample Chem week (hypothetical composite).** Mon: SAFE-03 honesty + SAFE-01 RAMP for a planned tiny fizz. Tue: CHEM-01 properties before names. Wed: CHEM-02 small reaction you can stop *or* skip. Thu: CHEM-03 conservation weighing if volumes stay tiny; denser CER. Fri: exit ticket + honesty tag in the notebook; if the family wants real titration skills later, start CHEM-05 planner. Refuse titling the week “AP Chemistry complete.”

**A sample Phys week (hypothetical composite).** Mon: prior-idea card for force-as-fuel without scolding. Tue: PHYS-01 change-of-motion with table. Wed: PHYS-05 slope as rate from Tuesday’s numbers. Thu: PHYS-02 energy storyboard *or* PHYS-04 battery circuit. Fri: talk box + CER crumb; optional wave look Saturday. Algebra sits beside Wednesday’s graph only after the table exists.

**Dose honesty.** School chem and physics blocks run long with fume hoods and specialists. A parent hour is not a failed block. One named vehicle try done well beats a rushed medley of demos. Lab-path neighbors are scheduled honesty, not guilt about the kitchen.


## Practice that actually builds learning

Pick one named try-it per Science Hour. Do not assign the whole bank. Map from memo 02: CHEM-01…06, PHYS-01…05, SAFE as needed, OUT-06 when the neighbor is real.

### Talk box (locked)

**Opening question:** “What changed in the matter or the motion — and how do we know safely from this trial?”

**Follow-ups (3–5):**

1. Observe: “What do you *see* / *measure* — not what you hoped?”
2. Record: “What does the notebook / table show? Units?”
3. Reason: “How do you know from *this* trial? What is the warrant — why would that measurement count for this idea?”
4. Check / alternative: “What else was different? Can we tell for sure? What else could explain these numbers?”
5. Revise: “You may change your mind. Try another look — or redesign once.”

**How to wait.** After you ask, count a slow three (classroom-origin wait from Rowe’s elementary science tapes; Stahl’s think-time convention — use the pause; do not claim a kitchen RCT).[^8] After they stop, wait again. If they are mid-claim, do not cut them off. Look at the materials or the notebook if the silence is hard.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the question was vague; wait-time was zero; they are hunting a vocabulary word because you asked name-first; they are guessing what you want; materials never landed; they offered a book-only guess; they invent neat rows because the trial was messy; they are bracing for an AP rubric sentence. Pose, wait, point back at the notebook — do not fill the silence with the right vocabulary.

### In-home try-its

**SAFE-03 — Kitchen picture honesty card**  
*Time:* 20–30 min. *Materials:* Honesty card (*Kitchen change-of-matter picture ≠ Chemistry credit / ≠ secondary lab path*); one food-safe demo description if you will run anything. *Safety:* Food-safe only if a demo runs; adult present. *The fun:* Saying the honest sentence out loud removes the shame and the inflation at once. *The skill:* Path honesty before the fizz. *Steps:* Source the card; list what a transcript Chemistry path would need that this picture lacks; decide picture vs lab-path neighbor for this week. *Exit:* “This week is picture / lab-path because ___.”

**CHEM-01 — Properties before names**  
*Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Adult-approved household substances (e.g. salt, sugar, baking soda — no unauthorized mixing); observation table; goggles if dust/splash risk. *Safety:* RAMP mandatory. No tasting. Adult present. Food/chem separation rules. *The fun:* Sorting by what you can see and measure before the textbook names arrive. *The skill:* Properties → optional names. *Steps:* Record properties; sort; names only after; refuse tasting and secret mixing. *Exit:* Three properties + “I did not mix beyond plan.”

**CHEM-02 — Reaction you can stop (picture)**  
*Time:* 35–50 min. *Materials:* Food-safe planned reaction (e.g. vinegar/baking soda, **small volume**) OR skip to lab-path; stop plan; goggles; honesty card. *Safety:* Lowest volume; ventilation; adult present entire time; stop on cue. *The fun:* A change you planned and can end. *The skill:* Predict → run small → record → CER + honesty tag. *Steps:* RAMP; predict; run; stop; CER; tag picture ≠ credit. *Exit:* Claim + honesty tag.

**CHEM-03 — Conservation as weighing honesty**  
*Time:* 35–50 min. *Materials:* Closed vs open contrast with safe fizz in sealed bag vs open cup — adult-approved tiny volumes; scale. *Safety:* Bag pressure caution — tiny volumes; goggles; adult present. No sealed pressurized drama bottles. *The fun:* Watching mass “disappear” in the open cup and staying put when the system is closed. *The skill:* Systems boundary + conservation reasoning crumb. *Steps:* Mass before/after; talk where mass went if open; refuse magic vanishing and invent-mass. *Exit:* Open vs closed result + reasoning crumb.

**CHEM-04 — Concentration / dilution sense (no stock acids)**  
*Band:* 17–18 (careful 15–16). *Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Drink-mix or food coloring dilution series; measuring tools. *Safety:* Food-safe only; adult present; no lab acids brought home. *The fun:* Seeing “more dilute” as a series you made. *The skill:* Operational concentration sense. *Exit:* Order cups dilute→concentrated with evidence.

**CHEM-06 — Mole as an amount (meaning before drill)**  
*Band:* 17–18. *Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Counting analogy; one labelled calculation; optional dry-rice weighing honesty. *Safety:* Paper / dry food. *The fun:* Amount-of-substance as a meaning, not a punishment worksheet. *The skill:* Mole means ___; one calculated amount. *Refuse:* Twenty identical drills as the Hour. *Exit:* Meaning sentence + calculated amount.

**PHYS-01 — Change-of-motion card**  
*Time:* 35–50 min. *Materials:* Cart/ball; ramp; stopwatch or phone; force-as-fuel misconception sticky. *Safety:* Clear path; no heavy falling objects on feet. *The fun:* Catching the “ran out of force” story with another look. *The skill:* Force changes motion; evidence from the trial. *Steps:* Predict; measure; repair force-as-fuel if it appears; optional fair surface compare. *Exit:* “Force changed ___ ; evidence ___.”

**PHYS-02 — Energy transfer storyboard**  
*Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Pendulum, rubber-band launch, or warm-from-rubbing safe try. *Safety:* Eye protection if projectiles; soft projectiles only. *The fun:* Drawing where the energy went — including thermal honesty. *The skill:* Systems boundary + transfer story, not “used up.” *Exit:* Storyboard arrows.

**PHYS-04 — Circuit with household-safe limits**  
*Time:* 35–50 min. *Materials:* Battery pack kit OR skip to labelled simulation / OUT; bulb or LED kit. *Safety:* Batteries only at home default; adult present; stop if hot; refuse wall-outlet hacks. *The fun:* Brightness that matches a prediction. *The skill:* Diagram + brightness claim with evidence. *Exit:* Diagram + claim.

**PHYS-05 — Graph slope as rate**  
*Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Position–time or speed table from PHYS-01. *Safety:* None beyond prior try. *The fun:* Slope as a sentence about the motion, not a naked formula. *The skill:* Math as tool beside the idea. *Refuse:* Inventing a smooth curve. *Exit:* Slope sentence tied to motion.

### Out-of-home try-its

**CHEM-05 + OUT-06 — Lab-path neighbor planner and bench session**  
*Planner time:* 25–40 min at home. *Bench time:* host-dependent (often 60–120 min — still bring Hour grain home). *Materials:* Planner card (community college / co-op / supervised maker chem night); skills list; host rules; prefilled RAMP; notebook intention card. *Safety:* Host rules supersede home defaults; adult/guardian as required by site; ACS ceiling mindset — not a DIY hood. *The fun:* Real glassware when home cannot host — honestly labelled. *The skill:* Decide what home cannot host; CER from *your* measurements; do not auto-title the visit “finished AP Chemistry.” *Exit:* Date/plan + notebook intention; after the visit: outcome + own data rows + site tag. If you only watched a demo, do not file the host’s numbers as yours.

**PHYS-03 — Wave pattern look (safe outdoor or home)**  
*Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Slinky, water tray, or tone app + honesty about model limits; optional outdoor water-ripple look with adult. *Safety:* No lasers in default bank; water tray wipe-up; outdoor adult. *The fun:* A pattern you can make and describe. *The skill:* Pattern description + one measurement crumb. *Exit:* Pattern + crumb.

### Blocked, mixed, and one incorrect example

**Blocked (new move).** First Chem week: SAFE-03 then CHEM-01. First Phys week: PHYS-01 with the force-as-fuel sticky visible. Do not mix mole drill with a first conservation try on the same day.

**Mixed (when the move is stable).** Conservation weighing + denser CER; change-of-motion + slope-as-rate; honesty card + lab-path planner in the same week.

**Incorrect example to diagnose (hypothetical composite, not a reported family).** A sixteen-year-old runs a large vinegar volcano, invents three neat mass rows “because the scale was annoying,” files a phone photo of a perfect internet gel as “my result,” and the parent writes Chemistry with Lab on a draft transcript. Repair: stop; SAFE-03 + AS invent-data / generated-result cards (ch08); redesign small or skip; title **Chemistry** only if the year’s work was chemistry — **with Lab** only when supervised investigation evidence exists.

### Library, walk, museum

A museum exhibit on energy or materials can feed PHYS-02 or a systems boundary (OUT-01). A library hour with a trade book on motion can prepare PHYS-01 — then the try still happens with a ball on your floor. Outings motivate. They do not replace the measure-and-claim move.

## For the student

Chemistry and Physics in this book are not a dare to mix unknown bottles. They are ways of looking at matter and motion with a plan, a notebook, and an adult in the room when heat or chemicals are involved.

**What the idea is.** Something changed — in the stuff, or in how something moved. You measured. You wrote it down. You say what you claim, point at your numbers, and say why those numbers count for the idea. You also say how you kept the try safe — or why you skipped and planned a better place to try.

**Tiny worked example (illustration).** You weigh a bag with a tiny planned fizz sealed inside: 52.4 g before, 52.4 g after. Same try in an open cup: mass drops. Claim: in the closed bag, mass stayed the same under these conditions; in the open cup, some matter left as gas we did not catch. Warrant: if mass is conserved in a closed system, the closed bag should hold steady; the open cup’s drop fits matter leaving the system boundary. Limit: cheap kitchen scale; tiny volumes; not a proof of atoms from one bag alone.

**Try 1.** With your adult, complete a RAMP card for a food-safe property look or a ball-and-ramp look. Run the try. Fill three rows. No inventing.

**Try 2.** Write a denser CER: claim; evidence with row numbers; because-the-idea (warrant); one thing you still cannot tell.

**Explain it back.** In your own words: What changed in the matter or the motion — and how do you know safely from this trial?

**Challenge.** Catch one force-as-fuel sentence in your own first draft and repair it with another look — or redesign a Chem picture that was too big for drama into one you can stop.

## If it isn’t clicking

**Diagnostic 1 — Fear of chemicals or “I’m bad at physics.”**  
Next move: Start with SAFE-03 honesty and PHYS-01 or CHEM-01 with zero mixing beyond looking. Keep volumes tiny. Celebrate a complete RAMP card as real work. Place by skill, not birthday — a fifteen-year-old consolidating fair-test notes may need more Young Minds–band practice before mole talk.

**Diagnostic 2 — Force-as-fuel or vanishing-mass story will not budge.**  
Next move: Another look, not a lecture. Side-by-side trials. Name the idea in one sentence after the look. If equations make them freeze, stay qualitative this week.

**Diagnostic 3 — Invent-data, generated curve, or kitchen-as-credit inflation.**  
Next move: Stop the portfolio fiction. Honesty cards. Redesign or labelled secondary data. Separate picture weeks from lab-path weeks in the records language. If the urge is strong, use ch08 invent-data and gel-refusal objects on Friday.

**When to slow down.** RAMP incomplete; PPE missing; learner alone with heat; algebra panic swallowing the system. Slow or skip. Safety first.

**When to go ahead.** Stranger-readable table, warrant that names an idea, honesty tag stable — then dilution sense, slope-as-rate, or lab-path planner.

**When to get a human tutor.** Persistent unsafe improvisation; deep algebra wall that blocks every physics look even after qualitative path; family needs supervised wet skills for a real Chemistry path and no neighbor is planned. A tutor or co-op bench is help, not a failure.

Correct a wrong reason without crushing the look. “Never correct the adolescent” is not this book’s rule. Silence in the face of invent-data is not kindness.

# Chapter 7 — Talking and writing lab claims

![A denser CER three-box card with a warrant line and a falsify sticky, beside a dated notebook open to a data table and a pencil. Still-life. No people. No brand logos.](art/ch07-opener.jpg){width=100%}


## Why this matters

What do you claim — which evidence from the notebook supports it — and what warrant ties them to the idea?

That is not a timed Advanced Placement free-response every Thursday. It is not a debate-club win. It is not a fluent paragraph a machine could have written with no trial on the table. It is the question that turns a stranger-readable notebook into a sentence another person can check. A claim without evidence is a vibe. Evidence without a claim is a caption. Reasoning that only restates the claim is a shrug in fancy clothes. A warrant that never names a scientific idea is a pile of numbers hoping to mean something. A claim with no limitation pretends one trial finished the question.

If your family used *Science for Young Minds*, you already practiced CER light: claim, point at the notebook, a short because. This chapter ages that habit up. Keep “points at the notebook.” Raise the warrant (why would *that* measurement count for *this* idea?), the limitation (what this dataset cannot tell), and the alternative explanation (what else could explain these numbers?). At 15–16 the exit is oral CER then a short written paragraph with warrant and one limitation *or* alternative. At 17–18 it grows toward multi-sentence reasoning, competing claims on the same data, falsify prompts, soft error analysis, and occasional multi-trial synthesis — still not weekly AP as identity. *English for Emerging Adults* holds sentence craft. *Critical Thinking for Emerging Adults* holds judgment on ordinary present stakes. *History for Emerging Adults* holds sourced claims about founding documents. One pointer each, then teach science: a spoken or written claim about *this trial’s* material world, with the notebook in view.

What this idea unlocks is the explanation frame Ready, Set, SCIENCE! and IQWST-shaped materials put in teacher language: claim; evidence from the investigation; reasoning that links evidence to claim through a scientific idea.[^10] Framework high-school argument progressions already expect alternative explanations and limits of the data — as moves, not as a packet franchise.[^11] America’s Lab Report asks for ongoing student reflection and discussion sequenced with the look — not a silent procedure and a grade.[^12] Rowe’s wait-time work began in elementary *science* classrooms; extending the pause after a question and after an answer changes discourse, including evidence–inference talk. Copy the pause. Label the classroom origin. Do not invent a kitchen discussion RCT.[^13] That is a useful study grain, not a promise that every home will see the same result. Then teach.

Willingham’s knowledge constraint travels carefully: CER is a move inside *this* investigation, not a portable “scientist identity” minted by one worksheet.[^14] Knowledge first — the system and the tiny fair test — before multi-investigation synthesis of ecosystems in week one of age fifteen. Coverage is a map. Depth is the hour.

PFI 2016 found scientific inquiry or experiments taught that year to only about 25 percent of homeschooled ninth-through-twelfth-graders — while biology that year sat near 29 percent and chemistry or physics near 22 percent.[^16] Many high-school science years have been reading years. A denser CER from a real table is how inquiry becomes more than a checkbox. It does not require a fume hood. It does require a notebook and a wait.


You do not need to be a science writer. You do need to hear a claim with no row, a row with no claim, a warrant that only restates the claim, a chatbot CER with no trial, a model summary filed as the observation, invent-data to make the warrant pretty, and AP FRQ as every Thursday — and to ask the locked question, then wait. Hearing those wrong turns is teaching. Filling the silence with your own perfect paragraph is not.

This week you can learn to model one warrant stem aloud (“This measurement counts because ___”), press for a locatable row, and wait after “what else could explain these numbers?” Today the student can speak a denser CER, then write a short paragraph with one limitation named.

Picture two Fridays. In one, a tidy paragraph could have come from any review book — or from a window — and no one can find the numbers in the notebook. In the other, a paragraph stars two cells, names the idea in the warrant, admits the scale’s limit, and offers one rival explanation that the next trial could test. Both pages look like writing. Only one is lab looking on paper.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

Many adults remember science writing as “copy the conclusion from the lab manual,” as a graded lab report they never understood, or as no writing at all — only talk that drifted into a lecture. Rust on essay formulas is normal. It is not a character flaw. Five to eight minutes of this section, then the warm-up, is enough for tomorrow. The student still writes the blanks.

**Everyday picture.** Yesterday’s table is open. Three numbers are enough. You ask: “What do you claim — which evidence from the notebook supports it — and what warrant ties them to the idea?” You wait. The student says, “I claim blue light yielded a higher bubble rate than green under these conditions, because rows 2 and 3 show means 18 and 7 bubbles per two minutes. This counts because chlorophyll absorbs blue more readily than green — so more bubbles fit that idea if other conditions stayed the same. Limit: we did not measure filter transmission with a meter.” They point. If they say “because photosynthesis” with no row, you send them back: “Which evidence from the notebook?” That pointing is the move.

**Precise picture.** A *claim* here is a sentence about the material world that answers this week’s question and could be wrong. *Evidence* is numbers, observations, or labelled secondary data the learner did not invent — and another person can locate in the notebook. A *warrant* (the denser piece of reasoning) ties evidence to claim through a scientific idea: why *this* measurement counts for *that* claim. A *limitation* is one honest sentence about what this dataset cannot tell. An *alternative explanation* is another story the same numbers might fit — prompted, then owned. At 15–16 the exit is oral then written: claim; locatable evidence; warrant; one limitation *or* alternative. At 17–18 add competing claims on the same data, falsify prompts, soft error analysis, and occasional synthesis across linked trials — still not a Document-Based-Question mash and not AP every week.

Talk is not a luxury before writing. Pose one authentic question about the evidence. Wait. Revoice without stealing the claim: “So you’re saying rate rose because temperature rose — is that what columns B and C show?” Point at the notebook. Recitation (“define osmosis”) may warm up for two minutes. It is not the talk box.

**Warrant in one kitchen sentence.** The warrant is the bridge: *why these numbers count as evidence for this idea.* “Temperature was higher and rate was higher” is evidence talk. “Higher temperature increases collision frequency in this enzyme picture, so a higher rate fits the idea if volume and concentration stayed the same” is warrant talk. You do not need university prose. You need the idea named and tied to the row.

**Limitation vs alternative (keep separate).** Limitation: what the dataset cannot tell (scale precision; n=2; filter transmission unmeasured). Alternative: another explanation the same numbers might fit (volume also changed; light distance differed). At 15–16 prompt one. At 17–18 ask for both when the week can hold them. Insufficient evidence remains valid — Framework and fair-test chapters already licensed that honesty.


**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. *Claim with no evidence / vibe evidence.* “It worked” / “everyone knows.” Repair: “Where in the data?” Highlight and arrow to a cell. Delete unlocatable phrases.

2. *Evidence with no claim.* A perfect table and then “so that’s the data.” Repair: “What do you claim *about the material world* from this trial?” One sentence.

3. *Warrant that only restates the claim / idea dump with no evidence.* “This supports my claim because my claim is true” / a paragraph of textbook with no numbers. Repair: “Why does *that row* count for *that claim*?” Circle the idea words; if none, add. If no rows, return to the table.

4. *Chatbot CER / model summary as observation / write-my-lab-report.* Fluent encyclopedia voice; summary filed as looking; tool rewrote the report. Repair: close the window; student writes from *this* notebook; tool only after a real draft, on your account, as revision questions — not a rewrite. Point back to the Science Hour AI box.

5. *AP FRQ as every Thursday / debate-club winning / insufficient evidence refused.* Rubric language with no own data; vibe winner; inventing points to finish a strong claim. Repair: AS-06 AP-as-map card; “what else could explain these numbers?”; FAIR-04 “insufficient evidence” is valid; occasional WRITE-06 stretch labelled *practice, not spine*.

A sixth: *quote dump from the textbook* — three copied definitions and no sentence of their own tied to the trial. Repair: sentence 1 is the claim about *this* trial; then evidence; then warrant; then limit.

**Five-to-eight-minute parent warm-up**

Do this alone, with yesterday’s data (or three numbers cut from it).

Minute 1. Cut the table to three numbers you can point at. Put the rest aside for now.

Minute 2. Write one sample warrant aloud: “This measurement counts because ___.” Put it in your pocket. You will not read it first.

Minute 3. Star one limitation the dataset cannot tell. Star one alternative explanation you could prompt.

Minute 4. Write one revision question that targets evidence or warrant, not polish: “Where is the claim? Point to the evidence. Why does that row count?”

Minute 5. Plan the wait after the locked opening. Count three aloud once, now, so you remember. Plan the second wait after they answer.

Minute 6. Decide the band exit: oral then paragraph with warrant + one limit/alternative (15–16) or competing claims / falsify / multi-trial soft (17–18). Do not assign CER-07 synthesis before a stable three-box exists.

Minute 7. If two learners will sit, plan one uptake prompt: “Add one idea to what ___ said, tied to a *different* row.”

Minute 8. Put the model warrant away. You are ready. They speak, then write.

## How to teach it this week

The Science Hour chapter holds the full session shape. Here is the same five beats scaled to denser CER talk and a short written claim. Keep thirty to fifty minutes. If a day only holds thirty, open the notebook, one talk-box turn with wait, and a paragraph exit.

**1. Open / notebook on the table (3–7 min).** Name today’s outcome: “By the end you can claim something about this trial, point at evidence, and say why that evidence counts for the idea.” No new demo required if yesterday’s table is enough. If a new try is planned, RAMP first when needed.

**2. Short look at the record (3–8 min).** Table before graph. Units visible. Operational DV clear. Optional: highlight two cells that could become evidence. Do not write their claim for them.

**3. One build (15–25 min).** Three-box CER, or competing claims, or oral-then-written — one focus. Not a packet of ten stems. Record in the notebook.

**4. Talk box (5–10 min).** Locked opening. Wait. Evidence press. Warrant press. Alternative or limit. Revoice without upgrading into the “right” AP sentence.

**5. Exit ticket (2–5 min).** Written CER crumb, circled idea phrase, falsifier sentence, or “insufficient — need ___.” Done-enough is right, or wrong-with-a-reason usable tomorrow.

**Band 15–16.** Scaffold the three boxes. Prompt one limitation *or* one alternative — not both as a burden on day one. Oral before written most days. Adult models one warrant stem once, then fades.

**Band 17–18.** Multi-sentence reasoning; competing claims; falsify; soft error analysis; occasional multi-trial synthesis or labelled FRQ-shaped stretch — still not weekly spine. Integrate evidence across linked trials when two notebook pages already exist.

**Exact wording you can say**

- “What do you claim — which evidence from the notebook supports it — and what warrant ties them to the idea?”
- “Where in the data? Draw an arrow to the cell.”
- “Why would that measurement count for *this* idea — not only repeat the claim?”
- “What else could explain these numbers?”
- “What can’t this dataset tell us?”
- “If we only had Trial 1, what would we wrongly conclude?”
- “You may change your mind when we look again.”
- “Insufficient evidence is allowed. What would you need next?”

**When to stop talking.** After the locked question, wait. After they answer, wait again before evaluating. If they are mid-warrant, do not finish the sentence for them. Look at the notebook if the silence is hard. Struggle before rescue: ask, wait, hint (a question or a named idea), then model one stem. Do not grab the answer. Do not paste a machine’s CER as theirs.

**Fade help.** Week one: co-fill Evidence box from highlighted cells. Week two: they fill Claim and Evidence; you prompt Warrant with “because the idea ___.” Week three: they draft oral CER alone; you only press alternatives. By a stable month, written paragraph after oral is routine.

**A sample claim week (hypothetical composite).** Mon: CER-01 three-box from last week’s Chem or Bio table. Tue: CER-02 evidence must be locatable (highlighter arrows). Wed: CER-03 reasoning names the idea + TALK-01 one question, three seconds. Thu: CER-06 oral then WRITE-02 paragraph. Fri: CER-04 competing claims *or* CER-05 falsify; exit ticket. Optional Saturday: museum one-exhibit CER (OUT-01). Refuse FRQ dump as the week’s identity.

**Dose honesty.** One denser CER from real numbers beats five empty stems. If the table is thin, FAIR-04 / “need ___ before claiming ___” is the scientific move — not invent-data.

**Uptake moves (keep on a sticky).** Evidence press: “Where in the data?” Systems press: “Inside or outside the boundary?” Fair-test press: “Can we tell for sure?” Revision press: “What would you change in the design?” Honesty press: “Is this your measurement or a labelled secondary set?” Warrant press: “Why does that row count for this idea?” Limitation press: “What can’t we tell yet?” Alternative press: “What else could explain these numbers?” Use one press per turn. Do not stack seven questions before they answer the first.

**Sibling or co-op note.** If two learners share the hour, post-response wait so each speaker is heard. Whose turn? One uptake prompt: “Add one idea to what ___ said, tied to a different row.” Refuse cold public quiz of strangers. Refuse turning disagreement into contempt.

**What not to do this week.** Do not assign a CER worksheet with no apparatus and no notebook. Do not mash Wineburg document sourcing as if it were lab CER — history owns founding pieces; this chapter owns *this trial’s* data. Do not make multi-trial synthesis weekly. Do not fill silence with the AP rubric sentence.


## Practice that actually builds learning

Pick one named try-it per Science Hour. Map from memo 02: CER-01…07, TALK-01…04 and TALK-06, WRITE-01…03 and WRITE-05…06. Pair lightly — do not assign the whole menu.

### Talk box (locked)

**Opening question:** “What do you claim — which evidence from the notebook supports it — and what warrant ties them to the idea?”

**Follow-ups (3–5):**

1. Observe: “What do you *see* / *measure* in the table — not what the book said would happen?”
2. Record: “What does the notebook show? Can a stranger find that cell?”
3. Reason / warrant: “How do you know from *this*? What idea makes that row count as evidence?”
4. Check / alternative / limit: “What else could explain these numbers? What can’t this dataset tell us? Can we tell for sure?”
5. Revise: “You may change your mind. Try another look — or redesign once — or call insufficient.”

**How to wait.** Pose one question. Count three to five silently (Rowe wait-time; Stahl think-time — classroom-origin; use the pause).[^13] After the learner speaks, wait again before evaluating. Revoice tentatively. Point at the notebook. Do not mash Rowe and Stahl as a kitchen RCT.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the question was vague; wait-time was zero; they are hunting a vocabulary word; they are guessing what you want; the materials or table never landed; they offered a book-only guess; they think science means “repeat the parent’s explanation”; they invent neat rows because the trial was messy; they are bracing for an AP rubric sentence. Pose, wait, point back at the notebook — do not fill the silence with the right vocabulary or the rubric sentence.

### In-home try-its

**CER-01 — Three-box build**  
*Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Notebook page with Claim / Evidence / Reasoning boxes; one completed table. *Safety:* None. *The fun:* Watching a vague “it worked” become three honest boxes. *The skill:* Claim + locatable evidence + reasoning that names an idea. *Steps:* Fill claim; cite evidence rows; write reasoning; refuse evidence-free claim. *Exit:* Complete three-box.

**CER-02 — Evidence must be locatable**  
*Time:* 25–40 min. *Materials:* CER draft; highlighter. *Safety:* None. *The fun:* Detective arrows from phrase to cell. *The skill:* Delete unlocatable “everyone knows.” *Exit:* Two arrows to cells.

**CER-03 — Reasoning names the idea**  
*Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Idea bank sticky (energy transfer; selection; conservation of mass as weighing honesty; Newton’s first in plain words; structure–function). *Safety:* None. *The fun:* Circling the idea words so reasoning is more than a restatement. *The skill:* Warrant that ties row to idea. *Exit:* Circled idea phrase.

**CER-04 — Competing claims, same data**  
*Time:* 35–50 min. *Materials:* One table; two plausible claims. *Safety:* None. *The fun:* Scoring which claim the data support better without inventing a winner. *The skill:* Data decide; insufficient allowed. *Exit:* Winning claim + one deciding row — or “insufficient.”

**CER-05 — Falsify prompt**  
*Band:* 17–18 (careful 15–16). *Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Learner’s claim; blank “would falsify if ___.” *Safety:* Per any new try. *The fun:* Naming what would change your mind before ego digs in. *The skill:* Falsifier sentence; optional fair test aimed at it. *Exit:* Falsifier sentence.

**CER-06 — Oral CER then written**  
*Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Talk-box timer; then paper. *Safety:* None. *The fun:* Hearing the claim before polishing it. *The skill:* Speak with wait; then write; compare strength. *Refuse:* Adult dictating the speech. *Exit:* Written CER after oral.

**CER-07 — Occasional multi-trial synthesis (not weekly AP)**  
*Band:* 17–18. *Time:* 40–50 min. *Materials:* Two or three related notebook pages; optional AP-style stem **labelled occasional stretch**. *Safety:* None. *The fun:* One claim that honestly spans trials. *The skill:* Synthesis + citation of which trials. *Refuse:* Making this every Thursday. *Exit:* Synthesis claim + trial citations + “practice not spine” sticky if an AP-shaped stem was used.

**TALK-01 — One question, three seconds**  
*Time:* 20–35 min. *Materials:* Notebook open; timer optional. *Steps:* Adult poses one evidence question; silent count 3–5; learner answers; wait again; no stack of quizlets. *Exit:* Learner restates the question they answered.

**TALK-02 — Revoice without stealing**  
*Time:* 25–40 min. *Materials:* Prior CER draft. *Steps:* Learner speaks; adult revoices tentatively; learner confirms or repairs; point to data. *Refuse:* Upgrading into the “right” AP sentence. *Exit:* Confirmed claim in learner words.

**TALK-03 — Evidence press round**  
*Time:* 25–40 min. *Materials:* Two sticky claims. *Steps:* For each, “where in the data?”; discard claim with no cell. *Exit:* Surviving claim + cell ID.

**TALK-04 — Systems press round**  
*Time:* 25–40 min. *Materials:* System diagram. *Steps:* Questions only about boundary/flows/omissions; wait; revise diagram. *Refuse:* Vocabulary quiz dressed as systems talk. *Exit:* One revised arrow or boundary.

**TALK-06 — Disagreement without contempt**  
*Band:* 17–18 (careful 15–16). *Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Two CER drafts on same data. *Steps:* Name disagreement location (claim vs evidence vs reasoning); revise; refuse personal attack. *Exit:* Disagreement located + next check.

**WRITE-01 — Captioned figure**  
*Time:* 25–40 min. *Materials:* Graph or photo of *this* setup. *Steps:* Caption what was measured and what varied; no novel claim required yet. *Refuse:* AI figure caption filed as own; stock photo as your setup. *Exit:* Caption ≤3 sentences.

**WRITE-02 — CER paragraph**  
*Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Three-box notes → paragraph. *Steps:* Convert boxes to flowing paragraph; keep evidence locatable; star the evidence phrase. *Exit:* Paragraph with starred evidence.

**WRITE-03 — Methods crumb another could repeat**  
*Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Yesterday’s try. *Steps:* Write steps so a stranger could repeat; include what was held constant. *Refuse:* “We did the lab.” *Exit:* Methods crumb + constant list.

**WRITE-05 — Error analysis soft**  
*Band:* 17–18. *Time:* 30–45 min. *Materials:* Messy data set. *Steps:* Name measurement and design error sources; what next; refuse self-insult as analysis; refuse invent-excuses for invent-data. *Exit:* Two named error sources + one fix.

**WRITE-06 — Occasional FRQ-shaped stretch (not weekly spine)**  
*Band:* 17–18. *Time:* 40–50 min. *Materials:* One AP-style or textbook FRQ stem **labelled practice**; own data if possible. *Steps:* Answer using evidence; meta-note: assessment practice, not this week’s identity; return next session to SYS/CER try. *Exit:* Response + “practice not spine” sticky.

### Out-of-home try-its

**OUT-01 + CER — Museum one-exhibit claim**  
*Time:* 40–50 min on site (+ travel). *Materials:* Notebook; one exhibit chosen. *Safety:* Venue rules; adult present. *The fun:* One exhibit, one systems-or-claim look — not a scavenger race. *The skill:* Boundary or CER crumb from observations you wrote down. *Refuse:* Whole-museum worksheet dump. *Exit:* Exhibit name + claim with evidence you recorded.

**WRITE-06 at a quiet library table (optional stretch)**  
*Time:* 40–50 min. *Materials:* One labelled practice stem; own notebook if the stem allows. *Safety:* None. *The fun:* Timed practice that stays labelled practice. *The skill:* Evidence from your work, not only rubric phrases. *Exit:* Response + sticky: practice not spine. Then next Science Hour returns to a real try.

### Blocked, mixed, and one incorrect example

**Blocked (new move).** First denser-CER week: CER-01 then CER-02. Do not assign competing claims and multi-trial synthesis on day one.

**Mixed (when stable).** Oral CER + written paragraph same day; evidence press + systems press across two days; captioned figure before CER paragraph.

**Incorrect example to diagnose (hypothetical).** A seventeen-year-old pastes a chatbot paragraph that never cites their table, invents two neat rows so the warrant “looks scientific,” and the parent files an AP FRQ packet as every Thursday’s Science Hour. Repair: close the window; CER-02 arrows; AS invent-data refusal (ch08); AS-06 AP-as-map; return Thursday to a real notebook page.

### Library, walk, museum

Library and museum motivate and supply secondary figures — labelled secondary, not your data. The claim still points at *this* notebook when the lesson is denser CER. Shared looking does not replace the warrant.

**Practice ladder across a month (illustration, not a mandate).** Week 1: three-box + locatable evidence. Week 2: warrant names the idea + oral then written. Week 3: limitation or alternative prompted every exit. Week 4: competing claims *or* falsify once. Then return to a fresh Bio, Chem, or Phys vehicle so the claim move stays attached to looking — not floating as a worksheet genre. America’s Lab Report design principles travel here: clear outcome; sequence look with talk; integrate content with process; ongoing reflection.[^12] Recipe-with-no-talk is not the ladder’s top rung.


## For the student

A lab claim is a sentence about what happened in *your* trial that another person could check. Talking it first makes the writing stronger. Writing it down makes the talk leave a trail.

**What the idea is.** Claim: what you say is true about the trial. Evidence: the numbers or observations in your notebook — with row numbers a stranger can find. Warrant: why those numbers count for a scientific idea (not “because I say so”). Limitation: what you still cannot tell. Alternative: what else might explain the same numbers.

**Tiny worked example (illustration).** Claim: In our closed bag, mass stayed the same under these conditions. Evidence: Table row 1 — 52.4 g before and after. Warrant: If mass is conserved when matter cannot leave the system, a closed bag’s total mass should hold steady. Limitation: kitchen scale precision; tiny volumes. Alternative: a leak too small to see could still matter on a better scale — next try checks the seal.

**Try 1.** Open yesterday’s table. Speak a denser CER with your adult waiting after the question. Point at two cells.

**Try 2.** Write the same claim as a short paragraph. Star the evidence phrase. Add one limitation *or* one alternative.

**Explain it back.** What do you claim — which evidence supports it — and what warrant ties them to the idea?

**Challenge.** Write one falsifier: “My claim would be in trouble if ___.” Optional: redesign a fair test aimed at that falsifier.

**Oral checklist (keep in the notebook cover).** (1) Claim in one sentence. (2) “Evidence: row ___ shows ___.” (3) “This counts because [idea] ___.” (4) “Limit / other explanation: ___.” (5) Stop. Let the adult wait. You do not need a speech. You need a trail.

**Writing checklist.** Star every evidence phrase. Arrow to a cell. Circle the idea words in the warrant. If you cannot circle an idea, add one. If you cannot star evidence, return to the table before you write more adjectives.


## If it isn’t clicking

**Diagnostic 1 — Blank page / freeze when asked for a warrant.**  
Next move: Oral only today. Model one warrant stem once. Three numbers only on the table. TALK-01 with a full wait. Do not upgrade their sentence into AP language.

**Diagnostic 2 — Fluent paragraph with no locatable evidence — or invent-data to finish.**  
Next move: CER-02 highlighter arrows. Delete unlocatable phrases. If invent-urge appeared, stop and use honesty / redesign (ch08 AS-04). Wrong-with-a-reason beats pretty fiction.

**Diagnostic 3 — Every Thursday became an FRQ packet / chatbot rewrite / debate without a notebook.**  
Next move: AS-06 card on the fridge. Return to three-box from *their* table. Tool only after a real draft, revision questions only. Disagreement must locate claim vs evidence vs reasoning (TALK-06).

**When to slow down.** No table yet; warrant still only restates claim; silence filled every time by the adult. Slow. Build Evidence box first.

**When to go ahead.** Locatable evidence stable; warrant names an idea; oral-then-written routine — then competing claims, falsify, soft error analysis, occasional synthesis.

**When to get a human tutor.** Persistent inability to point at a row after weeks of practice; writing anxiety that blocks every exit; family wants AP-shaped practice and denser CER is not yet stable — tutor for writing craft, still with the notebook in view.

Correct a wrong reason without crushing the look. “Never correct the adolescent” is not this book’s rule. Silence in the face of invent-data is not kindness.

Point back to the Science Hour AI box. Short version for this chapter:

You may, on your account, after the student has tried: ask a tool to explain warrant vs restatement *to you*; generate blank CER cards or isomorphic practice stems with the key held by you; get a labelled SCRIPT for *you* to say after the try; ask revision questions after *their* draft — not a rewrite; diagnose a paragraph already written (crop to paper).

Before you move on, tick by skill — not birthday. Life-of-the-habit tips live here (and in ch08), not in a ninth teaching chapter.

- [ ] Speaks a denser CER that points at locatable evidence.
- [ ] Writes a short paragraph with warrant that names an idea (not only a restatement).
- [ ] Names one limitation *or* one alternative when prompted.
- [ ] Survives an evidence-press round without vibe claims.
- [ ] Accepts “insufficient evidence” instead of inventing rows.

**17–18**

- [ ] Competing claims or falsify prompt completed on own data.
- [ ] Soft error analysis with two named sources + one fix — without self-insult as the whole analysis.
- [ ] Occasional multi-trial synthesis *or* labelled FRQ-shaped stretch — sticky says practice not spine.
- [ ] Revoices a peer’s or sibling’s claim without contempt; locates disagreement in claim / evidence / reasoning.
- [ ] Methods crumb a stranger could repeat, including constants.

**Ready for ch08.** When oral and written denser CER are routine, short assessments check whether the skill holds on a *fresh* trial, after a night, and two ways — without turning Friday into a percentile personality.

**Progress without a percentile.** A chart with skill names — locatable evidence, warrant names idea, limitation, alternative, oral-then-written, falsify — and dated notes (“holds on new table; warrant still restates; invent-urge caught and stopped”) tells you Tuesday’s move. A single score on the fridge does not. Screening ≠ identity. A shaky warrant means another look and another stem, not “not a science kid.”

Place by skill. A fifteen-year-old with stable warrants may try a careful falsify prompt. A seventeen-year-old who still cannot point at a row needs CER-02, not an AP packet. The birthday is not the curriculum.

An isolated “lab day” with no talk, or a CER worksheet with no apparatus, is not.

## Why this matters

What can you show me two ways today — and what still needs practice?

That question is the spirit of kitchen assessment for ages 15–18. Assessment here means a dated investigation notebook, a systems boundary with one flow, a stranger notebook reconstruct, a fair-test redesign, a denser CER with warrant and limitation, an invent-data refusal, a generated-result refusal, a kitchen ≠ Chemistry credit honesty check, an exit ticket, one explained miss, and a done-enough checklist by skill. It does not mean a percentile as a personality. It does not mean NAEP. It does not mean a homemade high-stakes battery. It does not mean “not a science kid.” It does not mean an AP score as the only honest path.

Families need two truths at once. First: every Science Hour already assesses. The RAMP card, the table with units, the talk-box claim, the warrant — those are information. Second: a dedicated Friday rhythm keeps the week honest when hope, coverage itch, or a publisher’s quiz would skip the look. Screening a skill is a teaching signal. It is not an identity. Formal evaluation, when a family seeks it for another reason, is a different column. Kitchen checks decide Tuesday’s try.

Wineburg’s century of “kids don’t know” panic stories, built on fact tours, is a useful caution even though that review sat in history: light coverage leaves few traces; more encyclopedia hours are the usual panic, and they still leave few traces.[^17] This chapter will not panic you into a binder of every PE. National Assessment scores for science exist for schools. They are not a sixteen-year-old’s census, and the federal statistical agency has never published a nationally representative *homeschool* NAEP Science score.[^18] Passing or missing a state’s reading-and-math sitting does not prove a science year worked. A publisher’s review booklet checks recall inside a scope. It does not license a national percentile.

What this idea unlocks is calm evidence you can hold in a notebook a stranger could read: they can name a system boundary and one flow; they can reconstruct a trial from the record; they can catch a confound and redesign; they can write denser CER with warrant and limitation; they can refuse invent-data and generated gels as results; they can label a kitchen picture as not Chemistry credit. You will know what to reteach Tuesday. You will know when a skill holds after a night. You will know when to grow from a paragraph to a multi-trial stretch — by skill, not birthday.

AP Biology / Chemistry / Physics 1–2 / Physics C / Environmental Science, CLEP Biology / Chemistry, and dual enrollment sit in this chapter as **labelled edges**. They are useful signals when the student and the kitchen are ready. They are never the default kitchen check and never this book’s weekly identity.[^19] There is no course titled simply “AP Physics.” A full timed FRQ-shaped stretch may appear occasionally for 17–18 when denser CER is stable. It is still not Monday–Friday.

You do not need to be a psychometrician. You need a dated notebook, a pencil, three kitchen skill objects you can rotate, the locked question, and — if an edge is in view — the honesty to label it as an edge.

This week you can learn to run one diagnostic wrong explained and a done-enough checklist on Friday. If an edge is in view, label it as an edge — title the credit **Biology** / **Chemistry** / **Physics** / **Earth Science** / **Environmental Science** / **Science**, and record “AP … exam, score n” as a separate signal. Today the student can show the same skill two ways — oral and written, or two different trials — and name one thing still in practice.

Picture two Fridays. In one, a printout of a score sits on the fridge and no one can say what the student can *do* with a fresh fair-test sticky or a blank CER card. In the other, a chart with skill names — boundary+flow, stranger notebook, redesign confound, denser CER, invent-data refusal, kitchen honesty — has a date and a note: “holds on a new try; warrant still shaky; AP practice labelled practice.” Both rooms feel like they measured something. Only one told you what to put on the table next week.

## For the parent: understand it yourself

Many adults remember assessment as a secret test, a red number, and a hallway conversation. Others remember no checks at all — only “we finished the chapter” or “we bought the AP binder.” For a Science Hour, assessment is frequent, short, kind, and useful. Five to eight minutes of this section, then the warm-up, is enough for Friday. The student still shows the skill.

**Everyday picture.** Friday, about twenty-five to thirty-five minutes inside a Science Hour. You put a *fresh* short try or yesterday’s unused table on the table — not Monday’s answered page. One kitchen object (10–15 minutes). The same skill a second way — oral if the first was written, or a new materials set (5–10 minutes). You ask what still needs practice (2 minutes). You tick two done-enough boxes and write Tuesday’s hold (3 minutes). The student leaves knowing what worked. You leave knowing whether the skill transferred.

**Precise picture — four columns**

| Kitchen check | Optional program review | Optional external edge | State or clinic sitting |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What to teach Tuesday | Recall and listening inside a purchased scope | AP / CLEP / dual enrollment when fit | Compliance or documentation in *that* lane |
| Systems boundary + one flow; stranger notebook reconstruct; redesign a confound; denser CER with warrant + limitation; invent-data / gel refusal; kitchen ≠ credit; exit tickets | Chapter questions, pretests, quizzes when you already use that program | Labelled edge; title stays Biology / Chemistry / Physics / Earth / Environmental / Science; score as separate signal | Reading/math portfolios, commercial batteries, evaluations — not a science NAEP |
| Parent-run; skill-based; screening ≠ identity | Not a national percentile; not a kitchen trial | Never the default weekly spine; CLEP ≠ NCAA core | Not replaced by this book; not a proof the science year “worked” |

Keep the columns separate until they stick.

**What denser kitchen assessment is (memo 03 grain).** Age up from *Science for Young Minds*’ dated notebook + fair test + one CER sentence. The honest high-school objects are denser: (1) dated investigation notebook or course folder; (2) named question / method / data / claim; (3) short lab report or multi-sentence CER from the notebook; (4) optional vendor review inside a purchased scope; (5) optional external edge. “Denser” does not mean every Friday is a college lab report. It means the middle-grades one-sentence CER is no longer the default ceiling. A sixteen-year-old claiming Biology with Lab should be able to show investigations that actually happened. A reading-only year can still be titled Biology if the work was biology reading and assessment — but it should not be titled Biology with Lab.


**Kitchen skill objects (rotate; one Friday can hold two if you stay short)**

1. **Systems boundary + one flow.** Fresh jar, body-subsystem card, circuit, or watershed sketch. Draw boundary; one input or output arrow; one thing the model leaves out.
2. **Stranger notebook reconstruct.** Hand a recent page to the parent (or a sibling) who did not watch. Can they say what was asked, what was done, what was measured, what was claimed — with units?
3. **Redesign a confound.** Show a confounded design card (or last week’s messy page). Name the confound. Redesign once. “Can we tell for sure?”
4. **Denser CER with warrant + limitation.** Fresh table or short new try. Claim; locatable evidence; warrant; one limitation. Oral then written, or written then explain-back.
5. **Invent-data refusal / generated-result refusal.** Spot a labelled FAKE pretty table or FICTION gel/curve. Practice “insufficient evidence” and the house rule: generated image ≠ data.
6. **Kitchen ≠ Chemistry credit / AP-as-map.** Honesty card: picture vs lab-path vs course title. Or AP CED snippet labelled College Board map — this week’s spine named SYS/CER/FAIR/CHEM/PHYS, not “AP unit.”

Add crumbs: daily exit tickets (2–5 minutes); one diagnostic wrong explained each Friday; a retest after a night on the sticky skill; a progress chart with skill names, not percentages.

Among homeschooled ninth-through-twelfth-graders in 2016, scientific inquiry or experiments that year sat at about 25 percent; earth sciences about 36 percent; biology about 29 percent; chemistry or physics about 22 percent. Ever taught among 9–12: earth 77 percent; biology 66 percent; inquiry 53 percent; chem/physics 41 percent.[^20] You are not inventing a subject. You are checking the looking move inside years many families already name — and noticing that inquiry *that year* was the thinner neighbor. There is no national study of daily homeschool science minutes. Do not write one into your notebook. Regular investigation sittings, a stranger-readable notebook, and several denser CER pages across a term are an honest dose.

No state in the five-state grain this book uses requires a course titled Emerging Adults Science I. Texas’s leading case names reading, spelling, grammar, math, and good citizenship — not the word science. North Carolina’s test list is English grammar, reading, spelling, and mathematics — not science; the homeschool issues its own diploma. Pennsylvania secondary homes shall include science; biology and chemistry may be included; graduation science three years; the portfolio tests in earlier grades are reading/language arts and mathematics, not science. Virginia lets the parent write the list and show evidence of progress. New York grades 9–12 require science (two units).[^21] None of that is legal advice. Read your own statute. File the kitchen work under **Biology** / **Chemistry** / **Physics** / **Earth Science** / **Environmental Science** / **Science** — Life Science or Physical Science only when the work is actually that. **With Lab** only when supervised investigation actually happened. Never a brand. Never Emerging Adults Science I. Never bare “AP Physics” as the credit line.

**Edges, labelled (not defaults)**

- **AP** (Biology; Chemistry; Physics 1; Physics 2; Physics C Mechanics; Physics C E&M; Environmental Science): optional external signal. Homeschoolers typically need a participating school’s AP coordinator. Opened fee grain for the 2026–27 cycle runs about **$99** (U.S./territories/Canada/DoDEA pattern) and **$129** outside, plus possible local proctoring costs — confirm with the coordinator.[^19] Safer transcript practice: title **Biology** / **Chemistry** / **Physics** / **Environmental Science**, with “AP … exam, score n” as a separate line. Tools for learning and labelled practice; not for live exams. College Board 2026 AP Exam Terms: no AI-generated Test answers or submissions.[^22]
- **CLEP** (Biology; Chemistry): optional credit-by-exam edge. Opened register fee pattern about **$97** plus administration fee — confirm locally.[^19] Each institution sets its own credit policy. CLEP Chemistry’s experimental-chemistry content is exam content about experiments, not a lab year. **Neither is NCAA core.**[^23]
- **Dual / concurrent enrollment:** state-variant college coursework. When it fits, designate the course on the homeschool transcript with college name; keep the official college transcript. Eligibility and age rules are local. There is no national dual-enrollment right to assume — eligibility and age rules stay local.
- **Enhanced ACT Science:** optional section; not required for the Composite on opened ACT pages. A signal, not a Biology credit.
- **SAT Subject Tests:** discontinued (last international sitting June 2021). One sentence, then stop.[^24]

Edges sit on top of the kitchen spine. They are not the spine.

**NCAA note (if athletics are in view).** Science is core. Live Division I public fact-sheet pattern: two years of science, including one year of lab if offered; CLEP not core; worksheet name equals transcript title. “With Lab” is a claim. Virtual labs vs “lab if offered” is silent on opened sheets — treat that as unsettled and point to the NCAA Home School Toolkit rather than a blog rule. Do not tell a parent Division I dropped the lab rule as a settled fact when the live sheet still carries lab language.[^23]

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear**

1. *Birthday gate.* “You’re only fifteen, so we don’t measure” or “You’re seventeen, so you must do AP packets every Friday.” Repair: run the skill objects at this week’s grain. Adjust length. Place by skill. Label AP as an edge if you choose it.

2. *Percentile identity / NAEP panic.* “You’re a 40th-percentile science student” or a news story becomes an encyclopedia year. Repair: translate to a skill — stranger notebook shaky; warrant still restates; invent-urge appeared. One try, one move.

3. *“Not a science kid.”* A miss filed as a type of person. Repair: screening ≠ identity. Shorter try. Clearer RAMP. One claim only. Retest after a night.

4. *Vendor quiz or AP packet as the only measure.* The pretest booklet or a weekly FRQ stands in for looking. Repair: keep the booklet if it helps recall; keep occasional AP-shaped practice labelled *practice*. Add a denser CER on a real table the booklet never saw. Refuse AP as every Friday.

5. *CLEP as a substitute year of Chemistry for NCAA — or Emerging Adults Science I as a credit — or kitchen fizz as Chemistry with Lab.* Repair: CLEP ≠ NCAA core; title stranger-readable courses; never brand the credit; honesty card for pictures vs lab path.

A sixth: *no checks — just the next lesson.* Hear abandoned transfer. A skill that only works on Monday’s familiar table is not done-enough.

**Five-to-eight-minute parent warm-up**

Do this alone, before Friday’s sitting.

Minute 1. Open the dated notebook to a blank progress grid (skill names, not percentages).

Minute 2. Pick one diagnostic wrong you actually expect this month: invent-data neat rows; force-as-fuel; kitchen-as-credit claim; generated gel as result; warrant that only restates; AP packet as every Friday; confound left unchallenged.

Minute 3. Write the repair move in one sentence. Note which bank ID would help Tuesday (FAIR-03, CER-02, SAFE-03, AS-04…).

Minute 4. Practice the locked opening aloud: “What can you show me two ways today — and what still needs practice?”

Minute 5. Choose today’s object: boundary+flow, stranger notebook, redesign, denser CER, invent-data/gel refusal, or honesty/AP-as-map — or a two-ways pair (oral + written on the same skill). If an edge is in view this term, write one line: “Edge = optional; kitchen = spine.”

Minute 6. Print or sketch a done-enough checklist with *skill names*.

Minute 7. Choose a fresh try or a fresh unused table. Transfer dies if Friday reuses Monday’s answered page with the answers still in the air.

Minute 8. Put the pencil down. You are ready. They show you two ways.

## How to teach it this week

Assessment is not a separate subject titled Emerging Adults Science I. It lives inside Biology / Chemistry / Physics / Earth Science / Environmental Science / Science as short probes. Friday often carries the longer sweep. Daily exit tickets stay tiny. The Science Hour’s five beats still hold. The “one try” *is* the check.

**Band 15–16 weekly rhythm**

- Daily: 2–5 minute exit ticket matched to the sit (boundary sentence; stranger header; can-we-tell; warrant crumb; RAMP hazard).
- Mid-week: one CER-01 / WRITE-02 page *or* one redesign sticky.
- Friday: one kitchen object on a *fresh* try or table; one diagnostic wrong explained; done-enough checklist; note Tuesday’s hold.
- Across a term: several denser CER pages saved; invent-data and gel-refusal cards run at least once. Not a battery every Friday. Not AP as weekly identity.

**Band 17–18 weekly rhythm**

- Daily exit may be a limitation sentence, alternative explanation, uncertainty crumb, or slope sentence.
- Mid-week: competing claims or falsify when CER is stable.
- Friday: kitchen object on a fresh piece; optional labelled AP-shaped practice *as practice*; done-enough; Tuesday’s hold.
- Across a term: multi-trial soft samples; occasional WRITE-06 / CER-07 stretch when paragraphs are stable — still labelled occasional. Optional external edge recorded separately.

**Five beats on a check day**

1. **Open / fresh materials or fresh table (3–7).** RAMP if a new try needs it. Name the skill object. No claim yet if the object is boundary or stranger reconstruct.
2. **Short look (3–8).** Enough to attempt the object — not a lecture that answers it.
3. **One check try (15–25).** The object *is* the try. Record. Two ways if time: oral then written, or redesign then CER.
4. **Talk box (5–10).** Locked opening. Wait. What still needs practice?
5. **Exit + diagnostic + done-enough (2–5).** Tick skill boxes. Write Tuesday’s hold. Celebrate wrong-with-a-reason usable tomorrow.

**Exact wording you can say**

- “What can you show me two ways today — and what still needs practice?”
- “Show me on a new table — not Monday’s.”
- “Point. Where is the evidence?”
- “Wrong with a reason we can use Tuesday is useful. Fiction rows are not.”
- “This score / this AP stem is a signal — not your identity, and not this week’s spine.”
- “Kitchen picture — not Chemistry credit. Lab-path when we need it.”

**When to stop talking.** After the locked question, wait. Do not narrate the two ways for them. If they freeze, narrow: “Show me the boundary *or* the warrant — pick one first.” Then the second way.

**Fade help.** Early Fridays you may name which object. Later they choose from a three-item menu. Retest after a night without reteaching the answer.

**A sample assessment Friday (hypothetical composite).** Fresh conductivity or ramp table the student has not CER’d yet. Object: denser CER with warrant + limitation (oral then written). Diagnostic sticky: warrant-restates tagged Tuesday → CER-03. Done-enough: locatable evidence holds; limitation named; invent-urge absent. Optional: one AS-06 glance if the family has an AP calendar on the wall — spine still named CER, not “AP unit 4.”

**Across a term (illustration, not a mandate).** Weeks 1–2: exit tickets + boundary or stranger-notebook Fridays. Weeks 3–4: redesign + denser CER Fridays. Week 5: invent-data and gel-refusal if not yet run. Week 6: kitchen ≠ credit if Chem pictures happened; AP-as-map if an edge calendar appeared. Then rotate. One OUT check per month when travel fits. Skip a 180-day testing calendar. Skip panic into every performance expectation.

**Library / museum as check, not tourism.** NCES outing rows show Homeschool families visiting libraries and museums at higher rates than the all-student average — motivation is real.[^28] Bring back one claim with locatable evidence. The outing is not the transcript line. The notebook that follows can feed one.


## Practice that actually builds learning

Map from memo 02 AS bank plus the skill objects above. Pick one per Friday; rotate across a month.

### Talk box (locked)

**Opening question:** “What can you show me two ways today — and what still needs practice?”

**Follow-ups (3–5):**

1. Observe: “What do you *see* / *measure* on this fresh try or table?”
2. Record: “What would a stranger need in the notebook to reconstruct this?”
3. Reason: “How do you know from *this*? What is the warrant?”
4. Check / alternative: “Can we tell for sure? What else could explain these numbers? Is this your data or labelled secondary — or fiction we refuse?”
5. Revise / practice: “You may change your mind. What still needs practice — name the skill, not a personality.”

**How to wait.** Pose. Count three to five. Wait again after the answer (Rowe / Stahl classroom-origin pause — use it; do not claim a kitchen RCT).[^25] Look at the notebook or the skill card if silence is hard.

**Stuck silence usually means:** the question was vague; wait-time was zero; they think you want a score speech; Friday reused Monday’s answers; they are bracing for an AP rubric; they fear “not a science kid.” Narrow to one object. Point at materials. Wait.

### In-home try-its

**AS-01 — Exit ticket menu**  
*Time:* 15–25 min (or 2–5 daily). *Materials:* Menu card (oral CER / boundary sketch / can-we-tell / RAMP hazard / falsifier / warrant crumb). *Safety:* None. *The fun:* Choosing a tiny close that still checks looking. *The skill:* One exit matched to today’s sit. *Refuse:* Turning exit into a test packet; public cold quiz. *Exit:* The menu item itself. *Parent note:* Tag the diagnostic error type for Tuesday.

**AS-02 — Diagnostic error sticky**  
*Time:* 20–30 min. *Materials:* Yesterday exit; sticky types: confound / invent-urge / systems-no-boundary / reasoning-no-idea / force-as-fuel / weather-as-climate / kitchen-as-credit / AP-as-spine. *Steps:* Tag error; plan tomorrow’s repair activity from banks. *Refuse:* Moralizing; ignoring the tag. *Exit:* Tag + tomorrow bank ID.

**AS-04 — Invent-data refusal card**  
*Time:* 20–35 min. *Materials:* Honesty card; sample “pretty” fake table labelled FAKE. *Steps:* Spot invent patterns (too neat; matches hypothesis exactly; no units; phantom points); practice “insufficient evidence”; redesign. *Refuse:* Laughing it off; using FAKE as real later. *Exit:* “If data are thin I will ___.”

**AS-05 — Generated gel / result refusal card**  
*Time:* 20–35 min. *Materials:* Example AI gel/curve labelled FICTION; Lab Report definition sticky (interact with material world or data drawn from it; fake sets do not count).[^26] *Steps:* Sort fiction vs real photo of own setup; write house rule; remind BIO-05 / CHEM path — electrophoresis on lab-path neighbor, not as a generated image in the portfolio. *Refuse:* “It’s fine if it looks right.” *Exit:* House rule sentence initialed.

**AS-06 — AP-as-map not spine card**  
*Band:* 17–18 (optional 15–16). *Time:* 25–40 min. *Materials:* AP CED unit list snippet labelled College Board map; family week plan. *Steps:* Circle map uses (if any); schedule at most occasional WRITE-06; keep weekly spine as systems + claim Hour. *Refuse:* Replacing Science Hour with FRQ packs; shame if not AP. *Exit:* This week’s spine named (SYS/CER/FAIR/CHEM/PHYS/…) not “AP unit.”

**AS-07 — Kitchen ≠ Chemistry credit card**  
*Time:* 20–30 min. *Materials:* SAFE-03 honesty; transcript/policy note if family keeps records. *Steps:* List what credit path requires; list what pictures teach; update records language. *Refuse:* Inflating transcript; hiding pictures as if shameful. *Exit:* Records sentence: picture / lab-path / course title honesty.

**Two-ways drills (core Friday shapes)**

- *Boundary two ways:* sketch on jar; then explain-back without looking; or sketch two different systems.
- *Notebook two ways:* stranger reconstruct; then student repairs one missing header.
- *CER two ways:* oral then written on the same fresh table.
- *Fair-test two ways:* mark confound on Card A; redesign on Card B.
- *Honesty two ways:* invent-data refusal + kitchen ≠ credit in one sitting if both urges appeared this month.

### Out-of-home try-it as a check

**OUT check — one claim with locatable evidence**  
*Time:* 35–50 min on site (+ travel) or a park transect. *Materials:* Notebook; one question written beforehand. *Safety:* Adult; venue rules; leave-no-trace. *The fun:* Can the looking travel? *The skill:* Bring back one claim with evidence you recorded — not a scavenger packet. *Exit:* Claim + evidence + “still needs practice: ___.” Museum, park, grocery structure–function, or night sky with adult all work when the check is one focused look.

### Blocked, mixed, and one incorrect example

**Blocked.** First assessment month: exit tickets + one object Fridays. Run invent-data and gel-refusal before any AP-shaped stretch.

**Mixed.** When stable: CER object + honesty card same Friday; stranger notebook + redesign across two Fridays; OUT check once a month.

**Incorrect example (hypothetical).** A family files weekly AP FRQ scores as the only science record, titles the transcript “AP Physics,” treats a vinegar volcano photo as Chemistry with Lab, and calls a sixteen-year-old “not a science kid” after one thin CER. Repair: AS-06; title **Physics**; AS-07 honesty; screening ≠ identity; return Friday to two-ways on a fresh table.

## For the student

Checks are how you and your adult find out what you can *do* — not who you are as a person.

**What the idea is.** Show a science skill two ways (say it and write it; or do it on a new try). Name what still needs practice. A thin day is information. Invented neat rows are not practice — they are fiction. A generated picture of a gel is not your result.

**Tiny worked example (illustration).** Friday object: denser CER on a fresh ramp table. Way 1 — oral: “I claim the steeper ramp gave a shorter travel time under these conditions because rows 2–3 show 1.8 s and 1.2 s. This counts if friction and mass stayed the same — forces and motion idea. Limit: phone stopwatch.” Way 2 — written paragraph with the same stars. Still needs practice: naming an alternative (maybe the release height also changed). Tuesday hold: FAIR-02 constants list before the next ramp.

**Try 1.** Pick one skill from the chart with your adult. Show it two ways on fresh materials.

**Try 2.** Complete invent-data refusal *or* gel-refusal if you have never run those cards — or if the urge appeared this month.

**Explain it back.** What can you show two ways today — and what still needs practice?

**Challenge.** Retest yesterday’s sticky skill after a night *without* looking at yesterday’s answered page first. Then compare.

## If it isn’t clicking

**Diagnostic 1 — Freeze / “just tell me the score.”**  
Next move: Narrow to one object. Two-ways on a tiny table. Celebrate wrong-with-a-reason. Remove percentile language from the fridge for a month.

**Diagnostic 2 — Skill works only on Monday’s familiar try; Friday transfer fails.**  
Next move: Always use fresh materials or an unused table on check day. Retest after a night. AS-02 tag → matching bank Tuesday.

**Diagnostic 3 — Invent-data, gel fiction, kitchen-as-credit, or AP-as-only-measure.**  
Next move: AS-04 / AS-05 / AS-07 / AS-06 in that order of urgency. Stop filing fiction. Separate edge calendar from weekly spine. Screening ≠ identity — still correct the honesty fail without crushing the person.

**When to slow down.** Anxiety spikes; every Friday feels like a secret exam; edges swallowed the Hour. Slow. Return to exit tickets only for a week.

**When to go ahead.** Two-ways stable; holds after a night; honesty cards initialed — then occasional FRQ-shaped stretch or external edge planning.

**When to get a human tutor.** Persistent transfer failure after fresh-try Fridays; writing block that stops every CER exit; family needs documentation for a path this book does not replace (state evaluation, college advising) — tutor or counselor in *that* lane, still with kitchen spine intact.

Correct a wrong reason without crushing the look. Silence in the face of invent-data is not kindness.

# Chapter 9 — Records

![An open notebook titled Biology / Chemistry / Physics, a dated investigation with question, method, data, claim; a denser CER card with a warrant line; a pencil; a kitchen honesty card labelled not Chemistry credit. Still-life. No diploma seal. No brand. No adolescent.](art/ch09-opener.jpg){width=100%}


The science *work* can be as ordinary as a library chapter and a dated notebook, and as serious as any classroom hour. The *credit* is a family claim until a stranger can use it — a college admissions reader, an NCAA Eligibility Center staffer, a dual-enrollment office, a receiving counselor, or the high-school transcript you will someday sign. That stranger will not have sat at your table. They will have a page.

This chapter is how you make that page at ages 15–18. Lawful, safe, and readable to a stranger are three jobs. Keep them in view.

This week you can date one investigation and one denser claim in a notebook so a stranger could almost reconstruct the trial. Today the student tries the page. You write the line, or they write the short lab report.

## What you are making

You are making a **transcript line** plus a **dated investigation notebook** (or course folder) of **Biology**, **Chemistry**, **Physics**, **Earth Science**, **Environmental Science**, or **Science**, with the question named, plus a **method**, plus **data**, plus a **claim**, plus a denser **claim–evidence–reasoning** paragraph or short lab report from *this* notebook, plus optional publisher review when you use a purchased program, plus optional external edges when they fit. You are not making a national diploma. You are not making Emerging Adults Science I.

When a stranger asks, print those ordinary titles — and **Life Science** or **Physical Science** only when the year’s work was that. Never a brand. Never Exploring Creation with Biology, Discovering Design with Chemistry, OpenStax Biology 2e, Science Unlocked Launch Year X, BJU Biology, or a FlexBook name as the credit line. Never bare “AP Physics” as the transcript title. Never **Emerging Adults Science I**. The dated notebook and the denser kitchen checks live *inside* those titles.

There is no federal homeschool diploma and no national science credit a registrar already files.[^29] Texas, North Carolina, and New York do not issue one. Pennsylvania’s supervisor or approved-organization diploma is a Pennsylvania high-school object. Virginia’s list of subjects is a list, not a transcript. A parent-issued diploma can be a real piece of paper. It is not a registrar’s national science credit.

Three readers, if anyone outside the kitchen asks. (1) The **state**, in a thick jurisdiction: IHIP, affidavit, portfolio, evidence of progress — with science samples where the statute named science. (2) A **college admissions** office: transcript, counselor letter (often the parent), optional scores. (3) The **NCAA Eligibility Center**, if athletics are in view: Toolkit packet, Core-Course Worksheets for natural/physical science, proof of graduation. State compliance is not a college transcript. Neither fact is a reason to skip the looking — or to fake a credit.

A family that invents “Emerging Adults Science I, 1.0, A” for a sixteen-year-old has created a slogan. A receiving counselor cannot map it. NCAA will not file it.

As of the 2016 Parent and Family Involvement subject tables, among grades 9–12-equivalent homeschoolers, there is **no overall “science” percent**. Sub-subjects taught *that year*: scientific inquiry or experiments **25 percent**; earth sciences or geology **36 percent**; biology **29 percent**; chemistry or physics **22 percent**. *Ever* among 9–12: earth **77 percent**; biology **66 percent**; inquiry **53 percent**; chemistry or physics **41 percent**.[^30] Those are the high-school science rows — not a curriculum poll and not a mandate to finish AP. Inquiry at **25 percent** *that year* is a quiet neighbor: many science years have been reading years. Informal learning does not arrive as “Biology, 1.0, B+.” Name the task, date it, keep a sample, grade on a stated scale if a credit is claimed.

## Titles a stranger can read

Print these and only these as high-school names, if a name is needed at all:

**Science.** **Biology** (or **Biology with Lab** only when supervised investigation actually happened). **Chemistry** (or **Chemistry with Lab** — same claim rule). **Physics** (or **Physics with Lab** — same claim rule). **Earth Science** / **Environmental Science**. **Life Science** or **Physical Science** (only when the year’s work is that band). Reading / Language Arts / English. Mathematics. History / Social Studies. Art, Music, Physical Education.

Those names travel. The NCAA sample homeschool transcript uses ordinary titles in the same family: Physical Science; Biology with Lab; Chemistry; Physics. The Core-Course Worksheet says the course name on the worksheet should be the same as the title on the transcript.[^31] Pick one title and stay with it.

Never a brand. Never Emerging Adults Science I. The brand, if used at all, sits in a parenthetical: “Biology with Lab (named textbook; sixteen dated investigations; denser CER from the notebook; lab-path neighbor once).” Combining does not mint a new title.

A publisher’s “grade 11 Biology workbook” is a scope, not a legal grade. A year that mixed biology reading, chemistry pictures, and a park transect still splits: **Biology** or **Chemistry** or **Physics** or **Earth Science** or **Environmental Science** or **Science** by the work that actually happened.

Here is an illustration, labelled as such, not a reported family:

> 17 September 2026. Biology with Lab. Question: how does light change measured leaf color over five days in two treatments? Method: same water volume, same pot size, same window shelf; changed wrap vs open; recorded color scale daily. Data: table in notebook. Claim: wrap slowed the measured green fade in this trial. Warrant: less light reaches the leaf surface under the wrap. Limitation: one plant each, one week. Grade recorded on transcript scale.

That is a record a parent, a Pennsylvania evaluator, a New York quarterly narrative, a college reader, or an NCAA worksheet can actually use. It is not NAEP.

## “With Lab” is a claim

**Biology with Lab** is on NCAA’s sample list. “With Lab” is a claim you can defend.

If you claim lab, keep objects you already produce: a dated notebook (question, method, data, claim); a materials list; a note that eye protection and other gear were used when ordinary home-lab safety would require them. No opened statute in the five states below requires a bound lab notebook. The notebook is how you defend the claim.

A kitchen picture is a picture. A vinegar-and-soda fizz is a change picture. It is not a Chemistry credit by itself. A year of kitchen trials can be a lab path if investigations happened, hazards were named, and the title is honest. A year that skipped every experiment in a book that “has labs in the chapters” is a reading year. Title it **Biology**. Leave “with Lab” off unless you built investigation some other way you can show.

Simulations can teach a skill. Whether a virtual lab satisfies an athletic “lab if offered” sentence is for the Eligibility Center in the year you file — this book does not invent that answer.[^32] Integrate looking with discussion (America’s Lab Report’s classroom grain, labelled). Refuse isolated lab-day theater. Kitchen ≠ Chemistry credit until the record says otherwise.

## The law is a pattern, not one form

U.S. homeschooling is state law. There is no federal science office. Read your current department page and the statute it cites. Five official-page examples show the range. **None of them requires “Emerging Adults Science I.”** Science is sometimes named, sometimes left to the parent’s list, and sometimes neither named nor tested.[^33] This chapter is not legal advice.

**Texas.** The Agency does not regulate home-school programs. *Leeper*, restated on TEA’s Alternative Schooling page, requires a bona fide written curriculum: reading, spelling, grammar, math, and a course in **good citizenship**. Science is not named as a word. The state does not award a homeschool diploma. For colleges and NCAA, the parent builds a transcript with stranger-readable titles. Transfer is treated like an unaccredited private school.

**North Carolina.** Notice of Intent. Nine calendar months. Annual nationally standardized test in **English grammar, reading, spelling, and mathematics** — not science. The home school, not the State, issues any diploma. A five-hour day is a recommendation, not law. Do not name the homeschool after a curriculum brand. Career and College Promise on the DNPE page is **North Carolina** dual enrollment with pathway gates — state-variant, not a national right.

**Pennsylvania.** Secondary **shall include** science; biology and chemistry *may* be included at the supervisor’s discretion. Graduation science is **three years**. The statute does **not** name a laboratory. **990** hours secondary are whole-program hours. Portfolio tests in grades 3, 5, and 8 are reading/language arts and mathematics — not science. A supervisor (or approved-organization) diploma is Pennsylvania’s high-school object. The evaluator’s experience list may name biology, chemistry, and physics — that describes the evaluator, not a required student Bio–Chem–Physics trio.

**Virginia.** Notice, a parent-written **list of subjects**, and evidence of progress (fourth stanine, or SAT/ACT/PSAT equivalent, or evaluation / college transcript). Science is not named as required; the parent writes the list. **Biology**, **Chemistry**, **Physics**, **Earth Science**, or **Environmental Science** is the ordinary word to write.

**New York.** Letter of intent, IHIP, quarterly reports, annual assessment. Grades **9–12** require **science (two units)**. A unit is 6,480 minutes. **990** hours for grades 7–12. A brand name is a **material**, not a required unit title. Home instruction does not yield a local or Regents diploma. If a Regents examination has a laboratory requirement, the student may be admitted with evidence the lab was met — IHIP, quarterlies, and/or teacher verification. That is an **exam-admission** object, not NCAA and not a national wet-lab law. For NCAA proof of graduation, the Toolkit notes New York (and Hawaii) paths that are not a local diploma — open the Toolkit.

If you live in none of those states, open your department page.

## What a short check is

Five objects, not a battery — and a sixth when an edge fits.

1. **A dated investigation notebook.** Date, question named, method, data, claim — under **Biology**, **Chemistry**, **Physics**, **Earth Science**, **Environmental Science**, or **Science** if a title is needed.

2. **A denser claim from the notebook.** Claim + locatable evidence + reasoning that names the idea (warrant). At 15–16, one limitation *or* one alternative. At 17–18, alternatives *and* limitations when the week asks for them.

3. **A short lab report or multi-sentence CER** that points at *this* table — oral first when that helps, then written. Revised once when you can. Kept in the notebook.

4. **Optional publisher review** when you use a purchased program — to check reading and recall inside a scope. Those tools do not license a national percentile. They generally do not satisfy North Carolina’s annual battery by themselves.

5. **Safety and honesty tags when they matter.** RAMP before heat or chemicals; kitchen picture labelled as picture when that is what it was; lab-path neighbor named when the try needed real glassware.

6. **Optional external edge** — an AP score, a CLEP score, a dual-enrollment grade — labelled as an edge. Edges sit on top of the kitchen spine. They are not the spine.

**Screening is not an identity label.** A miss on warrant is a teaching signal, not “this adolescent is not a science kid.” Reteach with a shorter try or one claim only.

Bands: **15–16** — system boundary scaffolded; stranger-readable notebook; denser CER with warrant; Bio / Chem / Physics as this week’s vehicle. **17–18** — more independent model revision; multi-trial comparison; optional AP / CLEP / dual-enrollment edge when fit.

## What a short check is not

NAEP. A percentile promise. Homeschooling was 3.4 percent of K–12 students in 2022–23 (~1.765 million); the 9th–12th cell was **3.1 percent**. Instruction-at-home figures (5.2 percent overall; 6.6 percent in the high-school band) are a different bucket.[^34] There is no nationally representative homeschool NAEP Science score and no opened national study of daily homeschool science minutes. SAT Subject Tests were discontinued — last international sittings June 2021.[^35] One sentence. Stop. ACT Science, when taken, is an optional signal (not in the Composite) — not a Biology credit.

## Edges: AP, CLEP, and dual enrollment

**AP Biology**, **AP Chemistry**, **AP Physics 1**, **AP Physics 2**, **AP Physics C: Mechanics**, **AP Physics C: Electricity and Magnetism**, **AP Environmental Science**, **CLEP** Biology and Chemistry, and **dual enrollment** are edges — useful when the student and the kitchen are ready. They are not the default year and not a requirement of this book.[^36] There is no course titled simply “AP Physics.”

Safer transcript practice when an AP exam is sat: title the credit **Biology**, **Chemistry**, **Physics**, or **Environmental Science**, and record “AP … exam, score n” as a separate signal. Homeschoolers typically need a participating school’s AP coordinator; confirm fees locally. Credit is institution-specific.

CLEP may become college credit. It is **not** NCAA core. Audited, CLEP, and credit-by-exam courses are not NCAA-approved core courses. CLEP Chemistry’s experimental-chemistry items are exam content about experiments — not a lab year.

Dual enrollment is a college course taken during high school. Access is local — North Carolina Career and College Promise has pathway gates; New York generally keeps home-instructed students out of the district program; Virginia accepts a college transcript as one evidence-of-progress form. Map the course to **Biology**, **Chemistry**, or **Physics**, with the college named. NCAA wants dual-enrollment designated on the homeschool transcript plus a separate official college transcript. A student who only “CLEPs out” without course lines may look thin to admissions — and NCAA will not count CLEP as core. Edges sit on top of the kitchen spine.

## NCAA, if athletics are in view

If Division I or Division II sports might be in the picture, science planning starts in ninth grade. **Science is a core area** (natural/physical science).[^37]

Division I asks for **sixteen** NCAA-approved core-course credits from the start of ninth grade, including **two years of science, including one year of lab if offered** on the live public fact sheet. One extra core year may be English, math, *or science*. Ten of the sixteen, including seven in English, math, or natural/physical science, must be finished before the seventh semester — the 10/7 rule. Minimum core-course GPA is **2.3**. Waiting until twelfth grade to start science is a 10/7 problem even if two science years eventually appear. That is an athletic calendar, not a teaching claim.

“Lab if offered” is conditional language. It is not “two years of lab.” It is not “lab optional because you homeschool.” Conservative reading for this book: build investigation into at least one of the two science years, title that year without hiding the lab (**Biology with Lab** is the toolkit sample), and keep student work that shows investigations. A standalone “Chemistry Lab” course will not be approved separately from the associated science course.

As of this book’s access date (17 September 2026), a 2026 Division I committee recommended eliminating the lab-if-offered sentence; the live Division I fact sheet still carried lab language. Open the live sheet in the year you file before you claim the rule is gone. Division II saw council action toward elimination; still open that live sheet before you claim status. Point to the current NCAA Home School Toolkit and Core-Course Worksheet — not blogs.

The homeschool packet includes a transcript (ninth-grade start date; administrator signature), an administrator statement that instruction followed *state* law, a Core-Course Worksheet for each core course, and proof of graduation. Evaluation begins after those documents are in **and** an NCAA school has placed the student on an Institutional Request List. CLEP and credit-by-exam do not count as core. There is no preapproved publisher package. Worksheet course name equals transcript title. Hawaii and New York: the Eligibility Center cannot accept proof of graduation from a homeschool-issued diploma — district/state letter or equivalency path per the Toolkit. If athletics are not in view, the same titles still serve colleges and transfers.

## Place by skill, not birthday

Can the student name the system boundary and one flow? If not, they are still in systems looking. Can a stranger reconstruct the trial from the notebook? If not, they are still in recording. Can they write a claim with warrant and limit? If not, they are still before denser CER. High-sounding *titles* cannot skip those moves.

A fifteen-year-old may still need a Physical Science year honestly titled. An eighteen-year-old may still need a Biology reading year before claiming Biology with Lab. Lying on the transcript to match a birthday helps no college and hurts NCAA.

1. A dated investigation notebook under **Biology**, **Chemistry**, **Physics**, **Earth Science**, **Environmental Science**, or **Science**.

Life of the habit, as the file you keep, lives here: date the work; name the skill; keep one wrong turn you can reteach. Revisit next Tuesday is a move in Chapters 7 and 8. The notebook is how Tuesday leaves a trace.

A path through high-school science looking is the promise. A diploma is not. A percentile is not. An AP score is not the only path that counts.

**Fit.** A classical homeschool that wants doing / reading / writing at high-school grain. Still a curriculum, not a lab safety plan. Transcript title: **Biology** / **Chemistry** / **Physics** — never “Rhetoric Biology” as a fake credit.

## Berean Builders — Discovering Design Biology / Chemistry / Physics / Earth Science

The publisher describes Christian science by Dr. Jay Wile, with *Discovering Design* high-school titles in Biology, Chemistry, Physics, and Earth Science. Hands-on work is described as woven in. Exact which title lands at ages fifteen to eighteen for *your* student is **placement by skill**, not a birthday rule. Check a current scope page before you assign a year.[^44]

**Fit.** A Christian family that wants chronological or design-framed high-school science with experiments woven in. Worldview is part of the product. Transcript title: **Biology** / **Chemistry** / **Physics** / **Earth Science** — not the book subtitle as a credit line.

## CK-12 FlexBooks — Biology / Chemistry / Physics

FlexBooks are described as free, online, customizable digital textbooks. Science subjects named on opened pages include Earth Science, Life Science, Physical Science, Biology, Chemistry, and Physics. A FlexBook may include interactive simulations. Simulations can illustrate. They do not replace a real investigation when the skill is measuring and claiming from *this* notebook, and they are not athletic lab credit by themselves.[^45]

**Fit.** A secular or mixed-worldview family that wants free adaptable texts whose titles already match the locked list. Not a packaged wet-lab kit. Parent co-holds accounts; COPPA does not bind ages 15–18, but the Science Hour AI box still holds — unaided first on looking and measuring. Transcript title: Biology / Chemistry / Physics / Earth Science / Science by substance — never “CK-12 FlexBook” as the credit line.

## OpenStax — Biology / Chemistry / College Physics / Astronomy (college OER)

OpenStax is a **college textbook path**, not a homeschool franchise. Biology 2e is described as a typical two-semester biology for science majors, written with an evolutionary lens; free on the web and as PDF. Chemistry 2e is a college chemistry text. College Physics 2e is algebra/trigonometry-based; the preface describes take-home investigations and points students to PhET simulations. Astronomy 2e is an introductory non-majors text.[^46]

**Fit.** A late-high-school student who can read college prose, possibly aiming at AP-adjacent or dual-enrollment rigor without buying a commercial brand. Not a fit for a struggling ninth-grader who still needs Physical Science consolidation. Evolutionary framing in Biology 2e is explicit — relevant to families who chose Apologia or Berean for worldview reasons. Pacing a two-semester majors text as a ninth-grade year is a family decision, not a publisher homeschool cycle. Transcript: **Biology** / **Chemistry** / **Physics** / **Earth Science** — not “OpenStax.”

## Home Science Tools — Science Unlocked Launch + kits-for-use-with

Home Science Tools sells kits and supplies **for use with** Abeka, Apologia, Berean Builders, BJU Press, and neighboring brands, plus its own Science Unlocked sequence. **Launch (8–12)** is the high-school edge; Wonder and Accelerate are younger grain. Chemistry safety equipment and dissection supplies appear in the catalog. This is the **common lab-kit vendor path**. It is not a ranked curriculum.[^47]

**Fit.** A family that wants materials-in-the-box convenience, or supplies matched to a text already in the house. Science Unlocked Launch year-cycles without an opened scope on your desk stay a check-the-page object. Transcript title: **Science** / **Biology** / **Chemistry** / **Physics** by substance — never “Launch Year X” as a credit line. Dissection remains optional, never required, in this book’s Hour.

## Nature / field / co-op lab — method neighbors

A supervised co-op lab hour, a museum bench session, a field-station day, or a community-college open lab is a **lab-path neighbor** — labelled as such in this house. Schedule honesty, not guilt. Follow host PPE and procedures. Write denser CER from *your* measurements afterward. Do not auto-title the week “Chemistry with Lab complete” unless family records policy honestly says the supervised investigations support that claim.

**Fit.** Parent load shifts toward logistics and talk after the session. Wet-lab ambition without owning a secondary chemical stock. Transcript: materials under Biology / Chemistry / Physics when the year’s work earns that noun; name the site in the description. Co-op brand names stay in the notes column.

## Dual-enrollment Biology / Chemistry / Physics — when state gates allow

Community-college or college Biology, Chemistry, or Physics taken for postsecondary credit during high-school ages. Outside teacher of record; official college transcript; NCAA-clean when designated. State gates vary — North Carolina Career and College Promise is one opened pathway with readiness gates; New York home-instructed students generally may not participate in the district instructional program; Virginia accepts a college transcript as one form of evidence of progress.[^48] Eligibility, cost, and age rules are local. This chapter is not a fifty-state dual-enrollment chart.

**Fit.** A student ready for college pacing and a kitchen that wants an outside teacher of record for a wet-lab year. Edges, not the default spine for every household. Transcript: **Biology** / **Chemistry** / **Physics** with dual-enrollment designation — or the college’s own course title clearly marked as college. Never “Emerging Adults DE Science.”

## AP and CLEP — edges on the shelf

AP Biology, AP Chemistry, AP Physics 1 / 2 / C, AP Environmental Science, and CLEP Biology / Chemistry are **edges**, not this book’s weekly spine.[^49] Sit them when fit. Safer transcript practice: title **Biology** / **Chemistry** / **Physics** / **Environmental Science**, with the exam score as a separate signal. There is no course titled simply “AP Physics.” CLEP is not NCAA core. Confirm fees and local ordering with a participating AP coordinator or CLEP test center. Full registrar architecture at grades 1–12 handbook length lives in *Science for Homeschooling* — one pointer, then stay here.

## Combining honestly

Need: $0 start → library + museum + nature.  
Need: Christian conversational student text → Apologia Exploring Creation Biology / Chemistry / Physics.  
Need: Christian textbook / video → BJU Press Biology / Chemistry / Physics; Abeka as vendor neighbor.  
Need: classical doing / reading / writing → Elemental Science HS Biology / Chemistry / Physics.  
Need: Christian chronological / design-framed HS → Berean Discovering Design Biology / Chemistry / Physics / Earth Science.  
Need: free digital adaptable → CK-12 Biology / Chemistry / Physics.  
Need: free college-density prose → OpenStax Biology / Chemistry / College Physics / Astronomy.  
Need: kit / supplies convenience → Home Science Tools Launch or kits-for-use-with.  
Need: supervised glassware → co-op / museum / college lab-path neighbor.  
Need: outside teacher of record → dual enrollment when gates allow.  
Need: external exam signal → AP / CLEP as edges.

Combine without crowning. Put materials in the notes. Keep the credit line ordinary. Multi-age kitchens are common: a younger sibling and a seventeen-year-old can share apparatus with different notebook expectations. The title still follows the work.

Place by skill, not birthday. Vendor age bands are starting guesses. A fifteen-year-old consolidating fair-test notes may need a Physical Science year honestly titled. A seventeen-year-old already writing denser CER with warrant and limitation may be ready for OpenStax or dual enrollment. Moving into review is not a shame label. Jumping ahead because a box says “grade eleven” is not required when the student cannot yet name what stayed the same.

Informal learning — kitchen, garden, museum, outdoor looking without a purchased spine — is a named practice in some homes. It is a poor match for a plan that must list syllabi unless you translate lived science into a course title after the fact. The translation is work: name the task, date it, keep the denser CER. The library, museum, and nature hours above stay available to both the formal and the informal.

No opened national survey ranks these programs inside the homeschooled pond. Sales copy is not a census. “Award-winning” is organization or award-committee language — useful as a breadcrumb, not as this book’s ranking.

**Digital caution in one breath.** Ages 15–18 are off COPPA’s under-thirteen line; parent still hosts and stays in the loop. Ages 15–16: still in the room for Science Hour. Ages 17–18: co-hold plus AP / Common App integrity. A login is not a science year. Tools, if any, stay parent-hosted and point back to the Science Hour AI box — unaided first on looking and measuring; no invent-observation; generated image is not data; no write-my-lab-report.

## What this chapter will not do

It will not crown a winner. It will not invent a four-year cycle for any publisher. It will not reprint the grades 1–12 handbook under this cover. It will not treat AP / CLEP / dual enrollment as the required kitchen spine. It will not put a brand — or Emerging Adults Science I — on the transcript. It will not invent 2026 kit list prices. It will not claim PhET is athletic lab credit. It will not confuse a kitchen fizz with Chemistry with Lab.
