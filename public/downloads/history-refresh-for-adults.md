# Welcome

This book exists because usable civic literacy is ordinary adult work — a feed card about a founding anniversary, a ballot pamphlet in the mail, a museum caption, a courthouse plaque, a historic newspaper page, a short civic excerpt with a credit line, a workplace claim that “the law has always said.” There is you, the piece in front of you, and today’s looking move. You already have a life. The hour is the work.

I wrote this for a capable, busy, willing adult. You may be on a train, at a desk, on a shift, in a trade bay, a clinic, a warehouse, a retail counter, or at a kitchen table after the kids are in bed. Many adults feel rusty when they sit down to history they last saw in school. That feeling is common. It is not a verdict, and it is not proof of a history gene. This book will make you fluent enough in today’s looking move to hear a caption offered as the event, a summary before a byline, a tidy “they were just stupid,” one account treated as a verdict, a model précis offered as a primary, and a feed-first reflex — and to ask yourself one good question before you grab the feed or the chatbot.

You do not need to be a historian. You do need to hear a caption offered as the event, and to open the piece first.

Ordinary stakes live on the go and in deeper sits: a pamphlet you already hold, a plaque, a museum label, a public-domain founding phrase, two short accounts of one event, a feed card *about* a clause. Civic life and work motivate. They do not replace the looking move.

The cover says *History Refresh for Adults*. The work is this commute, this desk, this weekend sit. A job and a degree are not a bicycle you now ride on every document and every feed.^1 You already consume history in pockets of life. That is not the same as having rebuilt usable looking — primary before feed; source before body; a short claim with a named source and a named limit. Adulthood is not a civic birthday.

A large adult skills survey places U.S. literacy near the international average — mean 258 versus about 260 — with about twenty-eight in a hundred U.S. adults at Level 1 or below.^2 That survey is why dense founding prose needs short excerpts. It is not a grade for you. Named civic-fact surveys tell a limited story: many adults can name the three branches; fewer can name a Senate term.^3 Those figures are population context, not this book’s pretest, and not historical thinking. The pamphlet or credited excerpt on the table tomorrow is the work.

## What a good History Refresh Hour looks like

You sit down already knowing today’s looking move. ## The five-minute warm-up

# If You Only Remember Five Things

Keep this page. The chapters will add wording, objects, and this week’s questions. They will not replace these.

**1. Primary before feed summary.** A feed card, a podcast host recap, a news chyron, a search snippet, or a model précis is a later telling until the piece is opened.^10 Library of Congress language is plain: primary sources are the raw materials of history — original documents and objects created at the time under study. Open the piece first. Then judge the card.

**2. Source before body.** Who made this, when, what kind of text it is, for whom, and against what problem — before the body is absorbed as slogan.^11 Cover the first paragraph until attribution is said. If you cannot name the maker, mark unknown. Unknown is honest. Guessing a founder because the prose sounds old is not.

**3. Presentism is the mind at rest.** Viewing the past only through today’s map is not a kid problem that expires at eighteen. It is how the mind sits when no one rings a bell.^12 Interrupt with a their-world question: what would have to be true in *their* world; who else was in the room; what this document cannot tell you. You can understand a line without approving it. Understanding and approving are different jobs.

**4. A short claim needs a named source and a named limit.** “According to [credit line], …” is the floor. Then one warrant — why that line counts. Then one limit — what this claim does not settle.^13 Fluency without a named source is theater. A thesis without a piece on the table is a speech. Dual perspective — the other side’s best point in one sentence — when a prompt asks for it. It is not assumed.

**5. Tools after you try.** A helper, including an AI tool, is a supplement you host. It does not sit in the chair during the first attempt. A model summary is not a source. A generated diary is not a primary. You hold the account. COPPA’s under-13 line does not apply here — that is not a licence for a secret friend.^14 A path through ordinary civic life is the promise. A fake diploma is not. There is no History Refresh for Adults I.

You look, wait, and try before the tool. You hold the account. Five things. Then sit down and try.

# The History Refresh Hour

![A desk still-life: credited excerpt, honesty card, micro/desk/deep stickies, notebook, commute mug, ballot pamphlet — no people, no logos, no readable UI.](book/art/history-refresh-hour-setup.jpg){width=100%}

The hour has a shape. Learn it once. Later chapters will give you today’s looking move. They will not rebuild this hour. When a chapter says “run the session,” it means this.

You do not need a school bell. You need the piece on the table, a try that could come out otherwise, you doing the sourcing and the looking, a record you could almost show a colleague, talk-to-yourself about who made this and what you notice, and stop. Three shapes carry the week: a **micro-session** on the commute or at lunch (~15–20 minutes), a **desk sit** (~25–40 minutes), and a **weekend deep sit** when needed (~45–75 minutes). Space them across days when you can. Cramming one Sunday feels productive. Spacing feels harder and usually wins more for retention.^15 Three to five micros plus one desk sit in a week, with an occasional deep sit, is the honest ambition — not under-running of a proven adult commute history trial, because there isn’t one at this grain.

Sit down having already done the five-minute warm-up from *How to Use This Book This Week*. The object changes. The shape does not.

## 1. Micro-session (~15–20 min, commute/lunch)

One looking move. Brief feedback. No high-stakes countdown timer as default rigor. Interruptible without shame. If the session becomes scrolling, it is not a session — set a fifteen-minute clock, one object, stop.

**Retrieve yesterday’s attribution sticky (2 min).** Close the book. Write or say who made yesterday’s piece, when, and for whom — or mark unknown. Prefer retrieve over reread.^16

**One short primary or one feed claim plus the primary it points at — honesty card (3 min).** Name what you are holding: original, excerpt, translation, later printing, modernized spelling, screenshot, or AI-generated. A feed card is a pointer until the piece is open.

**Source before body (3–4 min).** Talk to yourself: who / when / what kind / for whom / against what problem. Cover the first paragraph until those are said. Then one short look.

**Parallel try (4–5 min).** One sentence of limit, or one other-side best point, or one “this card is a later telling because…” sticky. Hands off the key. Hands off the chatbot. If you stall: ask yourself one question, wait, hint, then look — not the reverse.

**Exit sticky (2 min).** One line dated: the move, one check, one wrong twin you now hear. Put the phone away.

## 2. Desk sit (~25–40 min)

Start from a real artifact when you can: a ballot pamphlet, a workplace policy that cites a statute, a museum caption, a news claim about a founding clause, a nonprofit explainer about an amendment. Order: the primary or statute excerpt first when it is available; then the workplace or feed layer; one lateral hop (Who is behind this? What is the evidence? What do other sources say?).^17

**Source before summary (5–8 min).** Honesty card. Observe column only first if the object is dense.

**Short look (10–15 min).** Reflect and Question. One historical or civic question the text can actually speak to.

**One move from another strip (5–10 min).** A corroboration soft, a context sticky, or a claim-plus-warrant sentence.

**Exit ticket (3–5 min).** A dated note a colleague could follow. Name tomorrow’s retrieval flash.

Proprietary names and unpublished employer numbers stay off public consumer tools. Strip to a public-domain twin or use a tool your employer allows. Open *your* acceptable-use page.

## 3. Weekend deep sit (~45–75 min, when needed)

One central question. Short background — five to eight minutes at most. Two or three short documents, or one denser document plus one feed. Sourcing, then corroboration, then a thin then-world. One dual-perspective sentence when the question asks for it. A claim paragraph with a named source and a named limit. Stop while a look remains.

A deep sit that becomes “finish the survey textbook” is a coverage dump wearing a weekend hat. One question deeply beats seven skimmed.

Open with a diagnostic wrong when you can — the caption treated as the event, the summary before the byline, the “they were just stupid,” the one-must-be-lying, the model précis filed as a primary. Fade scaffolds: first pass guided; second pass you withhold the gloss. Run the chapter’s “done enough” checklist. Schedule next week’s three micro cues on a calendar — not vibes.

## The talk-to-yourself shape, once

Later chapters fill the exact opening. The shape lives here.

**Opening question.** Authentic. About today’s strip. No recitation answer already written on your page. Use one: Who made this, when, and for whom — and am I looking at the piece or at a feed about the piece? What do I notice that I didn’t expect — before I decide what it means? Is this card the evidence or a later telling? Where do these two agree — and where not — without calling a liar? What did this word mean *then*, to *whom* — as a document, not as a slogan? What would have to be true in *their* world — and where / how long as tools, not trivia? What do I claim — which named source shows it — and what does this claim not settle?

**Three to five follow-ups.** Pick; you do not need all five every Tuesday. Meaning: What do you mean, *later telling*? Representation: What does the header, credit line, or map show before the body? Alternative: Is there another legal reading of this line? Check: What can this source not tell me? Revise or diagnose: You may change your mind — where could someone go wrong?

**How to wait alone.** After you ask yourself, cover the answer or the feed. A slow three is a convention, not a sacrament — Stahl’s think-time is classroom-origin; Rowe’s wait-time is science class; neither is an adult history trial.^18 After you stop mid-reason, wait again before peeking. If the silence is hard, look at the credit line, the plaque, the map, or the clock — not at the phone’s answer key or the feed. Rescue at one second is still rescue. Abandonment for forty minutes of scrolling is not a micro-session.

**What a stuck silence usually means.** The question was vague; wait-time after the question was zero; you are searching for a word; you are guessing what a textbook would want; the question was a recitation item in disguise; you offered a keyword where a looking move was asked for; you think “history person” means leave; you think a finished-looking chatbot paragraph is already a check; anxiety is eating working memory — shorten the session, do one attribution win, come back tomorrow. Your job is to pose, wait, and point back at the object — not to fill the silence with the right answer from a model summary.

You do not need five questions stacked. You do not need to perform Socrates. You do not need a live controversy. You do not photo-to-key mid-attempt.

# Chapter 1 — What civic literacy looks like for adults

![A still-life: honesty-card sticky, historic newspaper header with greeking, a described feed card with no product chrome, a courthouse-plaque scrap. No people. No logos. No readable UI.](book/art/ch01-opener.jpg){width=100%}

## Why this matters
You already meet history most days. A feed card about a founding anniversary. A ballot pamphlet in the mail. A museum caption. A courthouse plaque. A workplace sentence that “the law has always said.” The trouble is not that adults “cannot do history.” The trouble is that the feed arrives first, the credit line arrives last, and the looking move never gets a turn.

This chapter is the orientation. Later chapters will teach Observe–Reflect–Question, lateral reading, two accounts, civic texts as documents, maps and timelines, and a short civic claim. All of them lean on the same quiet skill: knowing whether you are looking at a piece from then, or at a later telling about the piece — and naming who made it, when, and for whom before the body becomes a slogan.

Civic literacy in this book is sourced looking. It is not a talent you either have or lack. It is not a three-branch flashcard you finish and frame. Named civic-fact surveys are useful as limited population context. In one recent survey, about seven in ten U.S. adults named all three branches; fewer named a majority of First Amendment rights.^26 In another, majorities knew Court tenure and House apportionment; fewer knew a Senate term.^27 Those figures describe recall items. They are not historical thinking, not this book’s pretest, and not a certificate. An adult who can source a plaque and still miss a Senate-term quiz item is not failing this book. An adult who names branches and treats every caption as the event is not done.

A large adult literacy survey sits nearby as reading stock, not as a history grade. U.S. adults 16–65 averaged 258 in literacy, with about twenty-eight in a hundred at Level 1 or below.^28 Dense founding prose needs short excerpts and honest scaffolds. That survey is not a promise that this book will move a score. It is a reason to keep Tuesday’s object short.

Place yourself by looking-move strip, not by diploma year. Having sat a survey course once is not a sourcing test. Feeling rusty is ordinary. Anxiety around prose you last saw in eleventh grade is real and common; it loads working memory; it is not proof of a history gene; and this book is not a clinical protocol. Short sessions and early attribution wins belong here on purpose. Untimed looking beats a countdown timer dressed up as rigor.

What this chapter unlocks is usable order. When you can cover a body until attribution is said, and name whether today’s object is the piece or a feed about the piece, the rest of the book has a floor. Feed hygiene later. Two voices later. A short claim later. Today: maker before body; primary before feed.

Rust is not failure. Refresh means rebuild usable looking and short civic claim craft. Start where the order is inverted. The rest of the book will still be there when the order holds.

One more reason this strip earns a full chapter: returners often arrive coverage-anxious (“I should restart at 1492”) or quiz-fluent (“I can name the branches, so I am finished”). Coverage is a map. Depth is the hour.^29 Recalling a civic fact is useful context. It is not the looking move. This chapter refuses choosing sides between “I already know this” and “I am too rusty to start.” You start at the object in front of you.

Looking does not automatically mint citizens.^30 Teach looking because it is how history and civic texts work. If citizenship craft is a separate aim, later chapters will point once to *Critical Thinking for Adults* and to *Publius*. Today the job is smaller and harder: open the piece first.


## Understand it yourself
**Everyday picture.** Think of an anniversary week on your phone. A card arrives: a painted scene, a short caption, a date in large type. The caption feels like the event. You could share it in four seconds. You could also cover the caption, find the credit, and open the piece the card is talking about — a letter, a clause, a newspaper page, a museum object. The first path is efficient and inverted. The second path is the hour.

Hold a mental picture of two piles. **Pile A** is the piece-from-then: a credited excerpt, a scan with a header, a statute paragraph, a plaque text, a letter with a date and a sign-off. **Pile B** is a later telling: a feed card, a podcast host recap, a search snippet, a textbook paragraph, a model précis, a generated “letter from 1776.” Civic literacy for adults starts by sorting the piles *before* you decide what the story means.

**Precise picture.** Source before body means attribution first: who made this, when, what kind of text it is, for whom, and against what problem — before the first paragraph is absorbed as slogan.^31 Historians do this almost automatically in published contrasts with able seniors; the contrast is labelled school-origin, not a promise about your kitchen table. The portable habit is the order. Cover the body. Say the credit line. If the credit line is missing, mark unknown. Then look.

Primary before feed means the same order when the spark was a card. Library of Congress language is plain: primary sources are the raw materials of history — original documents and objects created at the time under study.^32 A viral thread was not created at the time under study as the governing text. A model paraphrase was not. A two-sentence chyron was not. Feed literacy is a neighbor skill. It does not replace opening the piece.

Presentism is the mind at rest — including for adults.^33 Viewing 1787 only through today’s party map, or treating a textbook recap as “just the facts,” is the same rest state as a senior who overrode documents with present common sense. Interrupt without a scold. Ask what problem *they* were trying to solve. Ask who else was in the room. Ask what this document cannot tell you. You can understand a line without approving it.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear (including from yourself).**

1. **“I already learned this in school / I have a degree, so I’m done.”** Diploma year is not current skill. Unused pathways rust. Civic-fact scores and literacy stock move at the population level; neither proves you are finished. Place by the object in front of you. If you still absorb a caption as the event, you are still in this strip.

2. **“The caption is enough.”** The caption is a later telling until the piece is opened. Virality is not corroboration. A tidy sentence can be accurate, compressed, or wrong. You will not know which until you look.

3. **“Naming the three branches means I’m done.”** Recalling branches is a useful fact. It is not sourcing, not dual-document looking, and not this book’s finish line. The hour is not flashcard prep.

4. **“Adults don’t do presentism — that’s a kid problem.”** Presentism is how the mind sits when no one rings a bell. Age does not expire it. The repair is a their-world question, not a sermon.

5. **“The feed summary *is* the source.”** A summary can point. It cannot stand in for the piece. A model précis filed as evidence is the same inversion with better grammar.

**Five-minute warm-up before you try.** Put one object in reach — a saved anniversary card, a pamphlet page, a short civic excerpt with a credit line. Cover the body or the caption. Say who made this, when, and for whom — or mark unknown. Then say, out loud: “Am I looking at the piece or at a feed about the piece?” Glance at the wrong answers above. Name the one you would have given last year. Write the locked opening once. Close the book to the try-it. If anxiety spikes, stop after the warm-up and count it as a win. Come back for the try later the same day or tomorrow.

**What “fluent” is not.** A fluent recap is not a check. A fluent chatbot paragraph is not your work during the attempt. Speed without attribution is theater. Attribution without any look at the piece is a stall. Build both, in short doses, on objects you actually meet.

**Common adult scars, named gently.** Some of you were timed until panic. Some of you were told to “just remember the branches.” Some of you were praised for a tidy essay and never asked who made the document. None of that is a life sentence. This week’s repair is small: credit line first, piece before card, tool third. If a scar flares, shorten the session and keep the early win. You are allowed to be an adult who rebuilds a foundation without apology.

**Piece versus feed-about-piece, said again.** A Constitution PDF you open is the piece (still a later printing — honesty card later). A thread that quotes one clause without a link is a feed about the piece. A museum object in front of you is the piece. The caption under it is a later telling *about* the piece — useful, credited if you are lucky, still not the object. A workplace policy that cites a statute is a later telling until the statute excerpt is open. Sort first. Then look.

**Unknown is a legal answer.** Many feed cards have no maker you can name in thirty seconds. Many plaques name a dedicating club and not the author of the sentence. Mark unknown. Then decide what would count as a next hop — a library catalog, a LOC page, a named archive — without turning the micro-session into a forty-minute scroll.

**Podcasts and documentaries sit in pile B.** A confident narrator is still a later telling. Use a show to map a question or to stay company on a commute. Then open a piece. A documentary is not a primary because the lighting is good. Chapter 3 will teach feed hygiene in full. This chapter only needs the sort.

**Materials nostalgia, named once.** “I did document lessons in school, so I still source” is a cousin of diploma nostalgia. Materials on a table, years ago, are not this Tuesday’s move. A classroom study of document lessons found that handing out packets did not by itself produce discussion.^38 Solo adults still need talk-to-yourself and an exit check that forces the order. Copy the *shape*. Leave the old packet in the past.

**What this book is not asking you to become.** You are not training to be a professional historian this week. You are training to hear a caption offered as the event, and to open the piece first. Dual perspective — the other side’s best point — will be prompted later. It is not assumed today. Citizenship dispositions are a separate aim. Today is order.

## Practice that actually builds it
Blocked practice first: several attribution flashes in a row, then several piece-or-feed sorts. Mixed practice later: a piece, a card, a plaque, a pamphlet header — so you have to choose the move. Plaques, pamphlets, and feeds *are* the practice surfaces. They do not replace the looking structure.

Knowledge first, then fluency on the same objects. Hearing a wrong reason — including your own — then asking a better question, then naming a better reason, is the work.

### Talk-to-yourself box

**Opening (locked):** “Who made this, when, and for whom — and am I looking at the piece or at a feed about the piece?”

**Follow-ups:**
1. What do you mean, *later telling*?
2. What does the header or credit line show before the body?
3. Is there another legal reading of this line once the maker is named?
4. What can this source not tell me?
5. You may change your mind — where could someone go wrong?

**How to wait alone.** After you ask, cover the answer or the feed. Count a slow three. If you stop mid-reason, wait again before peeking. Look at the credit line, the plaque, the pamphlet header, or the clock — not at the phone’s recap. Rescue at one second is still rescue. Abandonment for forty minutes of scrolling is not a micro-session.

**What a stuck silence usually means.** The question was vague; wait-time one was zero; you are hunting for a vocabulary word instead of a maker; you are guessing what a textbook would want; you offered a branch name where a looking move was asked for; you think “history person” means leave; you think a finished-looking chatbot paragraph is already a check; anxiety is eating working memory — shorten the session, win one attribution item, come back tomorrow. Your job is to pose, wait, and point back at the object — not to fill the silence with a model summary.

# Chapter 2 — Reading a primary

## Why this matters
Chapter 1 restored order: maker before body; piece before feed. This chapter teaches the look itself. Without a way to stay with a short credited piece — and without an honesty card that says what kind of reproduction you are holding — a denser adult information diet flips the order again. The body arrives first. The maker never does. The snippet feels incomplete, so a recap fills the hole. A model précis fills it faster.

Library of Congress Teaching with Primary Sources calls primary sources the raw materials of history — original documents and objects created at the time under study.^39 Analysis, they say, can prompt curiosity. Students move from concrete observations and facts to questioning and making inferences. Sources are incomplete snippets. That incompleteness is a feature. It forces a further question. It is not a defect you repair by grabbing a tidy summary.

What this chapter unlocks is a usable look on a Tuesday. Observe for a minute before you decide what it means. Reflect with a because-link to something you actually saw. Question what the piece can and cannot answer. Fill an honesty card so a later printing, a translation, a screenshot, or a generated letter cannot sneak into the evidence pile unmarked.

Adults already consume long recaps. A podcast host can sound like an archive. An encyclopedia paragraph can feel like “just the facts.” A chatbot can write a Founder letter in the time it takes to wait for a train. None of those is the look. The look is slower and smaller. One excerpt. One question the text can actually speak to. One limitation named.

Place yourself by this move, not by diploma year. If you can already source a pamphlet and still jump from a letter’s first sentence to a moral, you are still in Observe. If you can sit with a header for sixty seconds and then name one thing the text cannot tell you, you are ready to borrow a second account next chapter — after this week’s honesty card is a habit.

Rust is ordinary here too. Many adults last annotated a document in a survey course. The muscle is not gone. The order is rusty. Short sessions and one unexpected notice beat a Sunday packet dump.

Looking still does not mint citizens. A careful Observe column is not a virtue badge. It is how you keep your own mind from writing the story before the piece has spoken. That is worth the struggle for ordinary adult civic life — a museum afternoon, a pamphlet night, a workplace claim that points at a statute, a family argument that cites “history.”


## Understand it yourself
**Everyday picture.** Think of a letter on the table — or a scan of one. Your eye wants the news in the first paragraph. The useful adult habit is the opposite of inbox skimming. Read the header first: who is writing, to whom, from where, on what date if the date is there, how they sign off. Hypothesize the audience. Then read the body. Then revise the hypothesis. A petition, a private note, a public open letter, and a bureaucratic request are different kinds of objects even when the ink looks similar.

Hold a mental picture of three columns on a scrap of paper: **Observe**, **Reflect**, **Question**. Observe is what you can point at — words, marks, layout, a stain, a printed header, a crossed-out line. Reflect is a hypothesis tied to a notice: “I think ___ because I saw ___.” Question is what you would need next — and what this piece cannot settle. If Reflect starts before Observe has five concrete notices, you are sermonizing. If Question is a gotcha for a present opponent, you left the piece.

**Precise picture.** Observe–Reflect–Question is the Library of Congress analysis tool, aged into adult self-study.^40 It is not an independent-historian exam. It is not an adult trial. It is a way to move from what is on the page to a reasoned inference without skipping the page.

An honesty card sits beside the columns. Name what you are actually holding:

- original or high-quality scan of an original
- excerpt (how much is missing?)
- translation (whose?)
- later printing or reprint
- modernized spelling or punctuation
- screenshot of a feed (not the underlying page)
- memoir written decades later
- textbook or encyclopedia paragraph (a secondary account)
- AI-generated text or image (not a primary from the past)

A primary is created at the time under study. A secondary retells, analyzes, or interprets at a distance. Both can be useful. They are not interchangeable. A textbook paragraph is an account with a publisher and a year — source it as an account. It is not automatically truer than a letter, and a letter is not automatically truer in every respect. The honesty card keeps those jobs from collapsing.

Incomplete snippets are the point. A one-page excerpt cannot tell you “what the decade thought.” It can tell you what this maker, on this occasion, offered this audience. Name the limit. Then decide whether you need a second piece — later, not as a panic recap.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear (including from yourself).**

1. **“I’ll summarize first, then glance at the byline.”** Summary-as-floor. The tidy story becomes the knowledge. Looking never starts. Repair: cover the body; header first; one limitation.

2. **“The encyclopedia paragraph *is* the primary.”** It is an account. Source the publisher and year. Compare it to a piece from then when you can. Notice compression. The encyclopedia can be careful and still not be the raw material.

3. **“This generated Founder letter is close enough to use as a source.”** A chatbot in a historical voice was not created at the time under study.^47 Label it fiction if you use it as a prop. Never file it under sources. Adults can type a prompt. That does not mint an archive.

4. **“I already know what it means — skip Observe.”** Then you will see what you expected. The locked opening this week exists to interrupt that. What do you notice that you *didn’t* expect — before you decide what it means?

5. **“If the snippet is incomplete, a model summary can finish it.”** A model summary is another later telling. It can prepare a next page after you tried. It cannot stand in for the look that is the lesson.

**Five-minute warm-up before you try.** Put one short credited excerpt in reach — a letter header, a newspaper masthead, a public-domain speech opening, a LOC image with a record page. Set a sixty-second timer. Observe only. Write five concrete notices. Do not interpret yet. Then fill one honesty-card line: original / excerpt / later printing / screenshot / other. Say the locked opening once. Close the book to the try-it. If the page feels dense, shorten the excerpt. PIAAC literacy stock is why this book keeps objects short — not as a grade for you, as a design courtesy.^41

**What “fluent” is not.** A fluent plot summary is not a check. A fluent chatbot précis is not your Observe column. Speed without notices is theater. Notices without any Reflect will stall you on a noisy commute. Build both. Question last, and keep it answerable.

**The attribution ladder, said plainly.** Start with what the object itself states: a printed name, a date, a place, a masthead, a sign-off. Climb one rung if needed: the archive record, the LOC bibliographic line, the pamphlet’s own title page. Stop before you invent a famous author because the prose “sounds like” one.^44 Mark unknown when the rung is missing. Unknown plus a next hop is adult work. A guessed founder is fan fiction.

**Reproduction tells.** A later printing may modernize spelling. A textbook crop may cut the masthead. A screenshot may hide the URL and the date you captured it. An AI image may look “old” and still be new. Sort by reproduction type *before* you trust the scene. Chapter 3 will teach leaving a webpage. This chapter only needs you to say what kind of copy is in your hand.

**What counts as a source this week.** A credited piece-from-then you can point at. A labelled secondary account you treat as an account. A feed card you have already marked as a later telling — useful as a pointer, not as the look. A generated letter does not count. A model summary does not count. A painting the model made of a battle does not count as a primary, even if looking at it together is a fine warm-up *after* you name it as made this week.

**Common adult scars, named gently.** Some of you were told to find the “main idea” before you were allowed to notice the odd word. Some of you were handed a packet of five documents and a quiz. Some of you were praised for a moral and never asked what the text could not answer. None of that is a life sentence. This week’s repair is small: sixty seconds of notices; one honesty card; one limit. If a scar flares, shorten the excerpt and keep the early win.

**Incomplete snippets as mercy.** A one-paragraph letter cannot carry a century. That is a relief, not a failure. LOC language treats the hole as a reason to ask a further question.^39 You do not owe the book a finished story. You owe the piece one honest look and one named limit. If the hole makes you anxious, write the limit first: “This excerpt cannot tell me ___.” Then Observe. The recap can wait.

**Memoir written later is a special honesty-card line.** A person who was there may write fifty years later. That is still a source. It is not the same kind of source as a same-week letter. Flag “written later” on the card. Chapter 6 will treat process duration and later-flag more fully. Today, the flag is enough to keep the look honest.

**Dual perspective is not this week’s exit ticket.** If a second voice appears on the weekend, list one agree and one differ without liar language. You are not required to produce an unprompted dual-function paragraph. Even adults manage that about half the time under favorable conditions; civic fights are not easy.^49 Prompt it when the chapter asks. Do not assume it.

## Practice that actually builds it
Blocked practice first: several Observe-only minutes, then several honesty-card sorts. Mixed practice later: a letter header, a masthead, a speech excerpt, a textbook paragraph labelled account — so you have to choose the move. Documents and images *are* the practice. They do not replace the columns.

Knowledge first, then fluency on the same objects. Hearing a wrong reason — including your own — then asking a better question, then naming a better reason, is the work.

### Talk-to-yourself box

**Opening (locked):** “What do I notice that I didn’t expect — before I decide what it means?”

**Follow-ups:**
1. What do you mean, *notice* — can you point at it on the page?
2. What does the header, credit line, or layout show before the body?
3. Is there another legal reading of this line once the unexpected notice is included?
4. What can this source not tell me?
5. You may change your mind — where could someone go wrong?

**How to wait alone.** After you ask, cover the recap and the feed. Count a slow three. If you stop mid-reason, wait again before peeking. Look at the credit line, the header, the image, or the clock — not at a chatbot précis. Rescue at one second is still rescue. Abandonment for forty minutes of scrolling is not a micro-session.

**What a stuck silence usually means.** The question was vague; wait-time one was zero; you are hunting for a theme word instead of a notice; you are guessing what a textbook would want; the question was a recitation item in disguise; you offered a moral where an Observe was asked for; you think “history person” means leave; you think a finished-looking chatbot paragraph is already a check; anxiety is eating working memory — shorten the session, win five concrete notices, come back tomorrow. Your job is to pose, wait, and point back at the object — not to fill the silence with a model summary.

### Named try-its

**Try-it A — One question, one excerpt (commute / lunch) — DOC-01**  
*Time:* 12–18 minutes.  
*Materials:* one short primary excerpt (≤1 page or ≤200 words) with clear attribution; one historical question the text can actually speak to.  
*Safety:* public-domain or archive-credited; no live controversy as the question.  
*The move:* Honesty card → Observe 60 seconds → read → answer *only* the question from the text → note what the text cannot answer. Exit: “This source can / cannot tell me ___ because ___.” Refuse a packet of five documents.

**Try-it B — Observe-only minute (commute / lunch) — AS-01**  
*Time:* 5–8 minutes.  
*Materials:* ORQ grid (paper or notes); one source.  
*Safety:* ordinary.  
*The move:* 60–90 seconds Observe only — no story yet. Exit: five concrete notices. If Reflect opinions appear, cross them out and return to the page.

**Try-it C — Letter header before body (commute flash + desk) — DOC-03**  
*Time:* 10–20 minutes.  
*Materials:* letter or petition excerpt with salutation and sign-off; date if known.  
*Safety:* ordinary.  
*The move:* Header and signatory first; hypothesize audience; then body; revise hypothesis. Exit: audience sticky plus one revised note. Inbox skimming applied to the letter is the miss.

**Try-it D — Attribution flash (commute / lunch) — AS-04**  
*Time:* 8–12 minutes.  
*Materials:* five screenshots or cards.  
*Safety:* blur workplace chrome; private.  
*The move:* Maker / date / type / unknown in under thirty seconds each. Exit: five scrawls. Unknown must be marked, not skipped.

**Try-it E — Reproduction honesty sort (desk or shift) — AS-05**  
*Time:* 15–25 minutes.  
*Materials:* a mix of original scan, reprint, textbook crop, screenshot, and a labelled AI image (labels revealed after the sort).

**Try-it F — Reflect with reason + Question column (desk) — AS-02 / AS-03**  
*Time:* 15–25 minutes.  
*Materials:* a completed Observe list from earlier in the week.  
*Safety:* ordinary.  
*The move:* Two because-links: “I think ___ because I saw ___.” Then three questions ranked by answerability. Refuse vibes without notices. Refuse rhetorical gotchas.

**Try-it G — Newspaper voice (desk; weekend with a second voice) — DOC-02**

**Try-it H — Speech excerpt soft (desk) — DOC-05**  
*Time:* 15–25 minutes.  
*Materials:* short public-domain speech excerpt; occasion card.  
*Safety:* no quoting out of occasion to win a feed fight.  
*The move:* Occasion and audience before body. One claim the speaker makes. One line they offer as support, even if rhetorical. Exit: speaker–audience–purpose in three bullets.

**Try-it I — Political cartoon soft (desk) — DOC-04**

*Materials:* one textbook or encyclopedia paragraph; one primary on the same topic.  
*Safety:* ordinary.  
*The move:* Label the textbook as a secondary account; source publisher and year; compare; note compression. Exit: “The textbook compressed ___ that the primary still shows.” The primary is not automatically truer in every respect — name that limit too.

**Try-it K — Image pair, same event (desk or weekend) — DOC-06**  
*Time:* 20–40 minutes.  
*Materials:* two images of the same event, place, or time window; attribution for each.

*Time:* 25–40 minutes.

## If it isn’t clicking
**Diagnostic 1 — Observation stays blank.**  
Likely meaning: you are waiting for a theme instead of pointing at the page; wait-time one was zero; dense prose froze the hand.  
Next move: return to Observe for sixty seconds on a *header only* — masthead, greeting, sign-off, or image edge. Five concrete nouns. Shorten the excerpt. If founding prose is the freeze, PIAAC-adjacent design applies: shorter is kinder, not lesser. Come back tomorrow if anxiety is eating working memory.

**Diagnostic 2 — Reflect becomes a sermon.**  
Likely meaning: you arrived with the moral; notices were recruited to decorate it.  
Next move: require a because-link. “I think ___ because I saw ___.” If you cannot point at the page, it is not Reflect yet. Separate understanding from approving — you can describe a speaker’s ask without joining it. Present-grid only (“they were just corrupt / stupid”) is the rest state; ask what would have to be true in *their* world, then return to the notice you actually have. Full their-world work lives in Chapter 6.^45 Today, one because-link is enough.

**Diagnostic 3 — Question becomes a gotcha.**  
Likely meaning: the question is aimed at a present opponent, or it is a recitation item in disguise.  
Next move: rank three questions by answerability from *this* text. Keep the one this excerpt can touch. Send the rest to AS-06 as a next-hop plan — archive, newspaper database, map — not as a fight. Name one thing the text cannot answer, out loud.

**Extra signals and next moves.** If every session becomes a plot summary, cover the body again. If you only succeed on typed transcripts and freeze on handwriting or layout, spend two micros on mastheads and headers only. If a chatbot already “observed” for you, you practiced fluency theater; close the window and do sixty seconds unaided. If you keep filing later printings as originals, slow down at the honesty card — that card *is* the work.

**When to slow down.** If Observe stays blank after a good day, return to blocked header minutes. If anxiety spikes, cut the session in half and keep five notices as the win.

**When to go ahead.** When an honesty card is filled without fuss, one Observe lands before Reflect, one limitation is named, and you have said the generated-primary refuse once, you are ready to treat a feed card as a later telling on purpose — Chapter 3.

**When to ask a human.** A librarian, a colleague who is good at slowing down, a tutor, or a class can help when self-talk loops. Bring the object and a blank Observe column, not “I’m bad at documents.” Correct the reason without crushing the attempt — applied to yourself. “Never correct yourself” is not this book’s rule.

## Why this matters

Your default archive is often a phone. A caption arrives on the train. A host summarizes “what the Founders meant” while you wash dishes. A search snippet answers a civic question before you open any article. A chatbot offers a fluent précis of “what happened.” Each of those can be useful as a *pointer*. None of them is automatically the evidence.

This chapter owns the focus lock of the book in its adult form: **primary before feed summary**. A feed card, a push notification, a podcast host’s tidy telling, a headline, or a model summary is a **later telling** until a credited piece — or a honest hop that finds who is behind the card — comes first. Chapter 1 named the civic literacy hour. Chapter 2 taught Observe–Reflect–Question and the honesty card on a short primary. This chapter keeps the primary move and adds feed hygiene so the primary still gets a turn.

Without that flip, looking never starts. You feel informed. You share. You argue. The piece that would settle or complicate the claim stays closed. Virality feels like corroboration. A host’s compression feels like an archive. A fluent model paragraph feels like a source. Headline outrage feels like proof. Those are ordinary wrong turns, not character flaws. They are also repairable with named try-its you can run on ordinary stakes.

Civic Online Reasoning research on middle school, high school, and college students found that young people’s online reasoning needed improvement across a large task set — fifty-six tasks, thousands of responses — and taught portable moves: read laterally (open a new tab; ask who is behind this), practice click restraint, and ask what the evidence is.^50 Those populations are school- and college-labelled. This is a useful study of looking moves, not a promise that every adult on a commute will see the same result, and not an adult-commute randomized trial. You borrow the *move*. You drop the classroom furniture. You apply it to the anniversary card, the workplace “history fact” forward, and the civic meme you already meet.

This chapter is not a culture-war media-studies course. It is not a substitute for looking at a historic primary when the week’s object is a document. It is not a hunt for enemies as hobby. It is hygiene so primary looking can happen: caption is not the event; one lateral hop; one minute of restraint; host summary versus transcript or primary; snippet versus body; anniversary feed versus a piece from then; model summary refused as source; headline versus body on history-adjacent news, softly.

If you can leave one civic claim this week with primary-before-feed order observed, one lateral hop recorded, and one sticky that says “later telling because…,” the chapter did its job. Place by that strip, not by whether you once took a survey course.

Rust on a primary is not proof you “are not a history person.” It is proof the last school hour was years ago and the feed never stopped. This chapter uses the surface you already inhabit — the card, the host, the snippet — as the practice field, then walks you back to a credited line. Commute and lunch motivate. ## Understand it yourself

**Everyday picture.** An anniversary morning. Your feed shows a commemorative image and a caption that moralizes a historic day in one sentence. You feel informed. You almost share. That feeling is the trap. The caption is a later telling — often sincere, often compressed, often modernized. The event, if you want evidence, lives in a speech, a newspaper page, a letter, a statute, a photograph with provenance, or a named archive page. The everyday question is not “Do I agree with the caption?” It is: **Is this card the evidence or a later telling?**

**Precise picture.** Lateral reading means you do not stay vertically inside the first page’s self-description as gospel. You open a new tab. You ask who runs this, what kind of organization it is, whether other named sources discuss the organization or the claim. Click restraint means you wait a short minute before the reward click — write what you already know and what you would need — then click only if it is still worth it. A podcast host summary is secondary storytelling until you find the underlying document or a named historian claim and compare the compression. A search snippet is not the article. A model summary is generated secondary — never a primary, never “the source” for the week’s looking. A headline is a sales pitch for attention; the body may not carry the historical claim the headline sold.

Sourcing still comes first when you finally meet a piece: who / when / what kind / for whom / against what problem — the habit Martin and Wineburg restated from historical problem-solving research, with a labelled contrast between historians and AP seniors on how often attribution came before the body.^51 That contrast is school research origin, portable as a *habit*, not an adult-home effect size. On a feed week, “before the body” often means before you let the caption finish your belief.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear (including from yourself).**

1. **“I felt informed by the caption — that’s enough.”**  
   Belief formed before any piece opened.  
   *What it usually means:* feed-first reflex; efficiency mistaken for looking.  
   *What you try:* Cover the caption; note the image; uncover; write what evidence would be needed; open a primary or named archive page before sharing.

2. **“The podcast host *is* the archive.”**  
   Compression filed as primary.  
   *What it usually means:* vivid secondary storytelling stood in for a document.  
   *What you try:* Label the host summary as secondary; find one underlying line or named claim; write “Host said ___; document shows ___.”

3. **“The chatbot précis is basically a primary.”**  
   Fluent generated summary filed as evidence.  
   *What it usually means:* model-summary-as-source; generate-primary temptation nearby.  
   *What you try:* Label the output as generated secondary; check two concrete claims against a credited piece; refuse to file the précis as the week’s source.

4. **“The headline outrage corroborates itself.”**  
   Emotional match treated as cross-check.  
   *What it usually means:* corroboration confused with agreement-in-tone.  
   *What you try:* Predict the body from the headline; read three paragraphs; note mismatch; name one primary still needed. Outrage is not a second source.

5. **“I stayed on the About page — they explained themselves.”**  
   Vertical reading only.  
   *What it usually means:* self-description treated as provenance.  
   *What you try:* One new tab; search the organization, not only the claim; write who is behind this — or “still unknown.”

**Five-minute warm-up.** Save one historic or civic claim card you already saw (screenshot OK if honesty-labelled). Cover the caption. Write three observations about image or layout. Uncover. Write: “Caption claims ___. Evidence I would need ___.” Do not share yet. Warm-up done. The locked opening for the week is already in your mouth: *Is this card the evidence or a later telling?*

Notice what the warm-up refuses. It refuses instant share. It refuses treating your first feeling as a check. It refuses letting a tool finish the sentence before you write what evidence would look like. If the card is about a remembrance day, keep the tone soft — care, not a gotcha. If the card is a workplace forward, keep stakes ordinary. If you cannot open a piece on the train, your sticky can still say “unchecked.” Unchecked is adult. Pretending the caption closed the case is not.

## Practice that actually builds it

Blocked early: several caption-not-event cards; then several click-restraint minutes; then several lateral hops with a hard stop. Mixed later: a primary interloper in a FEED stack; a model-summary refuse beside a host-summary compare; a snippet trap after an anniversary desk sit.

### Talk-to-yourself box

**Opening (locked):** “Is this card the evidence or a later telling?”

**Follow-ups:**

1. What do you mean, *later telling* — who told it after the fact, and how can you tell?
2. What does the header, credit line, or image show before the caption finishes your belief?
3. Is there another reading of this card that does not treat it as the event?
4. What can this card *not* tell you — even if every word feels sincere?
5. Where could someone go wrong here — and may you change your mind after a hop?

**How to wait alone.** After you ask, cover the caption or the feed. A slow three is a convention, not a sacrament (think-time research is classroom-origin; you translate it as pause-before-feed).^52 Look at the credit line you hope to find, or at the clock — not at a model answer. Rescue at one second is still rescue. Forty minutes of scrolling is not a micro-session.

**Stuck silence usually means.** The question was vague (“is this true?”); wait-time one was zero; you are hunting for a vocabulary word instead of a looking move; you offered a keyword where “later telling” was asked; you think a finished-looking chatbot paragraph is already a check; anxiety is eating working memory — shorten the session, win one caption-covered sticky, come back tomorrow. Your job is to pose, wait, and point back at the card or the piece — not to fill the silence with a summary from a tool.

### Primary before feed — slowed down

Returners often remember a different choreography: see card → feel informed → decide → maybe look later. The choreography is efficient and inverted. The true story is smaller and stronger: treat the card as a pointer; put a maker, date, type, and audience (or their absence) in view; open a piece when the claim matters; only then read the feed as a later telling.

Try this drill once at a desk sit. Take one anniversary card and one short public-domain excerpt on the same day or theme.

1. Cover the card.  
2. Source the primary (who / when / what kind / for whom).  
3. Observe–Reflect–Question lightly on the primary.  
4. Uncover the card.  
5. Write three lines: what the feed modernizes; what it omits; what it moralizes.  
6. Exit sticky: primary-before-feed order observed — yes/no.

The contrast is the lesson. Feeling informed is not a check. Opening the piece is.

### Lateral hop without doomscroll

Lateral reading fails adults in two opposite ways. One: never leave the page. Two: leave the page into an hour of tabs and call the fog “research.” The repair is time-boxed. One new tab. One question: who is behind this? One honesty sentence. Stop. If the hop reveals an advocacy group, that is useful provenance — not a licence to invent their motives, and not a substitute for checking the historic claim against a primary. If the hop ends in “still unknown,” that sentence is a win. Unknown is better than a self-description treated as gospel.

### Model summary and the lunch-break rule

Point back to the History Refresh Hour AI box. After you have tried, a tool may help you explain an idea to yourself from a named page, offer isomorphic practice with *your* key, or ask revision questions after *your* note. During the attempt, a model summary is not the source. A generated diary or Founder letter is not a primary. Photo-to-key is not practice. In a high-school math field experiment (grades 9–11), an unguarded chatbot made practice look better and left students worse on the test with the window closed — labelled **OFF-AGE for literacy/history**, not an adult history RCT, and not to be blended with math-only labels elsewhere.^54 The lunch-break rule it supports: the model may prepare the next looking page; it may not do your looking, sourcing, or claim when those are the lesson.

### Feed cluster is not a second primary

Three cards that repeat the same commemorative sentence are not three sources. They may be one telling with a copy machine. Before Chapter 4’s corroboration move, practice this honesty line on a desk sit: “These cards are / are not independent because ___.” If they share an image crop, a phrasing quirk, or a single upstream post, say so. Independence is a looking question, not a vibe. A pile of screenshots can still be one later telling.

### Podcast and documentary as neighbors

A good history podcast can wake curiosity on a commute. A documentary can put faces and places in mind. Neither replaces a credited piece when the claim is historical. FEED-04 is the bridge: host compression on one side; transcript line or primary on the other. When the show cites a letter, open a public-domain edition or a named archive page when you can. When it cites “historians say,” ask which historian and which work — then decide whether this week’s hour can hold that secondary honestly. Documentaries that compress decades into a cold open are later tellings with a camera. Enjoy them. Label them. Then look.

### Workplace forward, ordinary stakes

Someone sends a “fun history fact” about “what the law always said.” Ordinary stakes only — not legal advice, not HR advice, not a sting. Treat the forward as FEED. Cover the caption. Ask what statute, handbook clause, or public pamphlet would check the claim. If the claim matters for your understanding, open a public text. If it is entertainment, mark “unchecked — not my looking hour.” Do not paste proprietary employer language into a public chatbot to “verify.” Open *your* acceptable-use page when workplace examples appear. The looking move stays the same: card is pointer; piece is evidence.

### Search, snippets, and the feeling of done

Search is built to feel finished at the snippet. Your sticky fights that finish. Two results, lightly sourced, beat one snippet screenshot. Disagreement between results is not failure — it is a reason to open a primary or a named agency table. If both results are blogs summarizing each other, you still do not have independence. Write “still need a piece from then / a named dataset / a named statute.” That sentence is adult civic literacy on a Tuesday.

### What this chapter refuses (without scolding you)

You will meet people who treat virality as proof, host charm as archive, and model fluency as research. You do not need to convert them on the lunch line. You need your own order: primary before feed; hop before belief; piece before précis. This book will not rehearse live fights as content. It will not turn FEED into opposition research as virtue. It will not pretend that adulthood immunizes anyone against caption-as-event. Experts can fail digital evaluation too; the fix is still a named move, not a birthday.

### A week that stays honest

Banks are menus, not calendars. A FEED-focus week might look like: Monday FEED-01 and a wait-before-feed talk turn; Tuesday a short attribution flash on a primary; Wednesday desk FEED-06; Thursday a light source sentence on one claim; Friday exit sticky plus one diagnostic repair; weekend optional related exhibit or library hunt. A light tour of twenty cards leaves few traces; a pattern on a few cards is what the mind can keep.

### Representation before tidy story

Feeds sell tidy story. Looking asks what the representation shows before the moral lands. An image crop that hides a caption on a monument. A quote without a date. A “Founders believed” line with no named text. Your Observe minute from Chapter 2 still applies to the card as object: what do you notice that you did not expect — before you decide what it means? Then ask the Chapter 3 question: evidence or later telling? The two questions are friends. Neither is a recitation item.

# Chapter 4 — Two accounts and corroboration

![A still-life: two short credited document scraps side by side; Agree and Differ stickies; one limit note. No people. No logos. No readable UI.](book/art/ch04-opener.jpg){width=100%}

## Why this matters

Adult discourse often treats disagreement as proof of fraud. Two posts differ; someone must be lying. Two anniversary tellings conflict; one side is fake. A photograph and a paragraph do not match; “photos never lie,” so the text is propaganda — or the reverse. That courtroom reflex is understandable. It is also a weak looking move.

Corroboration, in the sense this book teaches, is simpler and kinder to the record. Put two short accounts of one event beside each other. Source each. List where they agree. List where they differ. Name what neither settles. Forbid “liar” as the first verb. Difference is information. Silence is sometimes information and often uncertainty. A feed cluster that copies itself is not independence. An image can show what a text cannot; a text can name what an image cannot. A witness can know an hour; a later historian can know a pattern with archives neither witness held.

Chapter 3 taught you to treat a feed card as a later telling until a piece — or a provenance hop — comes first. This chapter assumes you can open a piece. It adds a second voice. The habit is light and repeatable: same event, two voices; agree / differ / still wonder; one limitation named. It is not a weekly Document-Based Lesson dump. It is not AP exam theater. It is not a hunt for who is evil. It is not merging both voices into one tidy paragraph with the makers rubbed out.

Historians check accounts against each other as one of the core heuristics of historical problem solving — alongside sourcing and contextualization — in research that began with historians and students working the same documents.^55 Classroom programmes that scaffold excerpts around a central question supply a useful *shape* (short background, sourced looks, corroboration, close reading). That shape is portable to adult self-study when you name the move and drop the classroom furniture. A grade-11 classroom effect size is not a promise about your kitchen table or your lunch break.^56 This is a useful study of how looking can be taught, not a promise that every adult will see the same result.

What this chapter unlocks is usable thickness. Ordinary adult surfaces carry it: two short news-adjacent accounts of one historic anniversary; a museum label beside a pamphlet line; a period image beside a letter scrap; a workplace “history of our trade” story beside a public report; a feed cluster beside one primary. Money, workplaces, and civic life motivate. They do not replace the looking structure. No live controversy as the object. Low-heat events first so the move can land.

If you leave one agree line and one differ line without liar language, plus one limitation named, the week did its job. Place by that strip, not by diploma year and not by how loudly you can prosecute a stranger’s post.

Rust shows up here as speed. Adults who argue for a living may be fast at prosecution and slow at mapping. Adults who avoid conflict may merge too early to keep the peace in their own head. Both patterns are ordinary. Neither is a character flaw. Both have a repair: slow the first pass; keep columns; name the question each voice answers; add a limit. You already practiced waiting before the feed. Now wait before the verdict.

## Understand it yourself

**Everyday picture.** Two short accounts of the same street fair — composite, labelled illustration. Both say the music started late. One remembers the pie contest. One remembers the lost dog. Neither needs to be lying about the fair. Each noticed what their seat and their job made easy to notice. Corroboration starts there: match, differ, and what each account is *for*. Scale that picture to history: two newspapers on one inauguration day; a sailor’s letter and a port report; a photograph of a crowd and a paragraph naming speakers.

**Precise picture.** Corroboration asks: Where do these accounts thicken the record together? Where do they thin or conflict? Which question is Account A answering? Which question is Account B answering? Usefulness depends on the question you wrote down. A diary hour and a later historian’s paragraph can both be honest and still not answer the same question. A map can constrain a narrative’s geography. A silence in a source might mean genre, audience, ignorance, or strategy — mark uncertainty before you invent a conspiracy. A feed cluster is not corroboration if the cards are dependent copies.

Wineburg’s corroboration heuristic is the grown-up name for checking across pieces.^55 You do not need lab jargon on the train. You need two columns, makers still named, and a ban on “liar” as the opening move. False balance is the twin error on the other side: “both sides equal” without asking what each can settle. Corroboration is not automatic 50/50. It is question-tied usefulness.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear (including from yourself).**

1. **“One must be lying.”**  
   Disagreement becomes a villain hunt.  
   *What it usually means:* accounts treated as a single true story with one fake narrator.  
   *What you try:* Write the question each account seems to answer before you judge. List agree and differ. Delete moral verbs from the first pass.

2. **“I’ll just merge them into one tidy story.”**  
   Makers disappear; details blend.  
   *What it usually means:* corroboration collapsed into summary.  
   *What you try:* Keep two columns. Star which piece each detail came from. Exit with makers still visible.

3. **“Three cards saying the same thing — corroborated.”**  
   Dependence mistaken for independence.  
   *What it usually means:* feed cluster treated as one primary times three.  
   *What you try:* Ask whether the cards copy each other; check one primary; write “independent / not independent because ___.”

4. **“Silence proves the cover-up.”**  
   Absence promoted to certainty.  
   *What it usually means:* conspiracy-from-silence without limit language.  
   *What you try:* “Absence might mean ___ or ___; I don’t know yet.” Name what absence cannot prove.

5. **“Eyewitness always wins / later historian always loses” (or the reverse slogan).**  
   Vividness or prestige as automatic crown.  
   *What it usually means:* usefulness not tied to a named question.  
   *What you try:* For *this* question — who was in the room that hour — which helps? For *this* other question — what pattern emerged with archives — which helps?

**Five-minute warm-up.** Save two short cards or excerpts on one low-heat historic event (public-domain or archive-credited). Source each lightly: maker, date, type. Cover the bodies. Say the cards aloud. Uncover. Write one agree line and one differ line. Cross out any “lying” word. Warm-up done. Locked opening: *Where do these two agree — and where not — without calling a liar?*

If you only have one primary and one modern commemorative card, still run the warm-up — but honesty-label the kinds of voice. A period scrap and a feed are different instruments. Agree/differ still works; independence and time-gap notes matter more. If both “accounts” are modern feeds, you are mostly practicing Chapter 3 until a piece from then arrives. That diagnosis is useful. Do not pretend two memes equal two primaries.

### Two columns on paper beat a clever merge in your head

Working memory is small on a noisy commute. Externalize. Two columns. Finger on each piece. If you work in a note app, use two headings with makers in the titles — “Account A — *Evening Post*, 12 June (excerpt)” — so names do not evaporate. When you feel the urge to reconcile early, put the urge on a third sticky labelled “later claim?” and return to mapping. The urge is not the enemy. Acting on it before the map exists is.

### Talk turns you can borrow (then put away)

Say aloud, alone: “A wants me to notice ___. B wants me to notice ___.” Say: “For my question ___, A helps by ___; B helps by ___.” Say: “I am revising my differ line because I used a prosecution verb.” These are not recitations with one right answer. They are accountable to the texts in front of you. If a line cannot point at a word or a feature on the page, it is commentary seeking a home.

### Worked pictures you can steal (then put away)

**Picture 1 — Same event, two voices (CORR-01).** Two short accounts. Source each. Agreements (three lines max). Differences (three lines max). Forbid “liar” on the first pass. Exit: Agree/differ T-chart. Wrong twin: picking the voice that matches your prior politics and stopping.

**Picture 2 — Image vs text (CORR-02).** One image + one text on the same episode. What can each show that the other cannot? Exit: “Image uniquely shows ___; text uniquely shows ___.” Refuse “photos never lie” — crops, captions, staging, and later reuse exist.

**Picture 3 — Witness vs later historian soft (CORR-03).** Contemporaneous excerpt; later historian paragraph with a name. Label the time gap. What could the witness know? What could the historian know with archives? Exit: time-gap honesty sentence. Refuse automatic privilege to whichever feels vivid.

**Picture 4 — Feed cluster vs one primary (CORR-04).** Three related feed cards; one primary. Check dependence. Primary check. Exit: “These cards are / are not independent because ___.” Refuse screenshot pile as proof.

**Picture 5 — Map vs narrative (CORR-05).** Period or modern map + short narrative of movement, migration, or campaign route — ordinary historic stake. Does the narrative’s geography fit the map constraints? Exit: one fit or friction note. Refuse map as decoration only.

**Picture 6 — Silence as evidence soft (CORR-06).** One source that omits something you expected. Note omission. Hypothesize why (genre, audience, ignorance, strategy). Mark uncertainty. Exit: “Absence might mean ___ or ___; I don’t know yet.” Refuse conspiracy from silence alone.

**Picture 7 — Wrong twin (labelled).** Differ line: “Account B is lying about the crowd size.” Repair: “Account B estimates a larger crowd; Account A’s seat was farther from the square — neither settles an official count; I still need a named tally or a reason the count matters for my question.”

Close the pictures. Build one T-chart on a parallel pair yourself. Keep the first pass under fifteen minutes even at a desk. Speed here is a feature: you are mapping, not writing the seminar paper. If the parallel pair is thinner than the worked pictures, good — thin pairs are what life sends. One agree. One differ. One limit. That is a complete sit.

When you finish, ask once: did I leave the makers on the page? If the names vanished, rewrite the exit sticky until they return. Nameless thickness is just a new summary.

## Practice that actually builds it

### Talk-to-yourself box

**Opening (locked):** “Where do these two agree — and where not — without calling a liar?”

**Follow-ups:**

1. What do you mean, *agree* — same fact, same emphasis, or same question?
2. What does each header, credit line, or image show before the body argues?
3. Is there another legal reading of this difference — genre, seat, audience — without a moral verb?
4. What can neither source tell you?
5. Where could someone go wrong — liar theater, false balance, or merge-without-names — and may you revise?

**How to wait alone.** Cover the bodies after you source the cards. Slow three. Look at the two credit lines, not at a chatbot merge. Rescue at one second is still rescue. If silence is hard, point your finger at Account A’s first line, then Account B’s — physical pointing counts.

### Difference without prosecution — slowed down

Returners often remember debate club rules: find the flaw, crown a winner, move on. Looking across historical accounts uses a different first pass. You are not yet writing a closing argument. You are mapping the record’s thickness. Moral evaluation of past harms can come later, with care, and is not identical to “this newspaper is a liar.” Collapsing those tasks produces heat without light.

Try this drill once. Take a differ line that uses a moral verb. Rewrite it three ways: (1) audience/purpose difference; (2) seat/access difference; (3) question-each-answers difference. Keep the version that stays closest to the pieces. Delete the prosecution verb unless you have separate evidence of fabrication — and fabrication claims need their own sources, not a vibe.

### False balance is not corroboration either

“Both sides” can be as lazy as “one side lies.” If Account A is a same-day shipping ledger and Account B is an anonymous meme, they are not equal instruments for a tonnage question. Write the question. Then ask which piece can speak to it. Corroboration can conclude that one account is far more useful for *this* question while still refusing liar theater on the first pass. Usefulness is not a personality prize.

### Feed cluster, revisited

Chapter 3 warned that virality is not corroboration. Here the same warning becomes a method. Sort cards by upstream dependence. Same crop, same misspelling, same odd statistic — mark dependent. Then put one primary on the table. The cluster may still be interesting as a study of later tellings. It is not three witnesses.

### Silence, uncertainty, and adult patience

Absence is tempting because it feels like a secret the record dropped in your lap. Sometimes omission is strategic. Sometimes the genre never included what you wanted (a love letter may not report crop prices). Sometimes the maker did not know. Your exit line protects you from overclaim: two hypotheses and an “I don’t know yet.” Chapter 6 will deepen their-world context. Here, keep the soft limit. Conspiracy from silence alone is a wrong twin you should be able to hear in your own draft.

### Building a T-chart that stays honest

A good agree/differ chart is small on purpose. Three lines each keeps you from rewriting the encyclopedia. Star the piece that supplies each line. If a detail appears in both, it can live under Agree — still name both makers in a parenthetical once (“both A and B note the late start”). If a detail is only in one, it lives under Differ, not under a silent merge.

Adults sometimes overfill Differ with commentary (“B is biased”). Bias language can come later as a careful claim with evidence. On the first pass, prefer descriptive differ lines: larger crowd estimate; omits the rain; written next day vs written that evening; booster audience vs private letter. Description before prosecution. Chapter 7 will ask for warrants. This chapter asks for map-making.

### Question each account answers

Lee’s teaching point, carried lightly: accounts are not only right or wrong; they answer different questions from different standpoints. Your kitchen — train seat — translation is ordinary. Under each credit line, write one question in six to ten words. “What did the parade look like from the square?” “What should out-of-town readers believe about order restored?” When differ lines quarrel, check whether the questions quarrel. Often they do. That is thickness, not failure.

### Image, text, and the body as instrument

CORR-02 is not an art class. It is a reminder that representation has limits. A photograph can show arrangement, clothing, weather, distance — and still not name speakers, motives, or off-frame events. A paragraph can name and still invent scale. Captions on images are themselves later tellings when added by an archive, a newspaper, or a feed. Source the image’s credit. Source the caption’s maker if different. “Photos never lie” is a slogan that erases crops, staging, selection, and reuse across decades. “Texts always clarify” is the twin slogan. Ask what each instrument uniquely affords for *your* question.

### Time gap without romance

Contemporaneous voices feel authentic because they were close. Later historians feel authoritative because they had more boxes to open. Neither automatic crown survives a written question. A witness may not see the pattern. A historian may misread a witness. CORR-03’s exit sentence — time-gap honesty — keeps both gifts and both limits on the table. If you only ever trust diaries, schedule one sit with a named secondary that cites archives. If you only ever trust later synthesis, schedule one sit that returns to a same-day scrap. Flexibility is not cheating. Slogan loyalty is.

### Map friction as corroboration

Geography is a quiet second voice. A narrative that marches an army farther than roads and seasons allow needs a friction note. A migration story that ignores ports and rivers needs one too. You do not need to become a cartographer. You need one fit-or-friction sticky. Chapter 6 owns maps and timelines more fully. Here, the map is a corroborating instrument — or a constraint that thins a confident paragraph. Decoration on a slide are not yet looking.

### From two voices to a limit

Agree + differ without a limit still leaves you overconfident. Add one sentence: “Neither settles ___.” Attendance. Motive. Off-frame decisions. Legal meaning. Causal weight. The limit is not modesty theater. It is accuracy about what your pair can do. When you later write a short civic claim, that limit travels with you. Adults who skip it sound certain and travel poorly.

### Low-heat defaults (and why)

Default to ship arrivals, market days, weather disasters told softly, school openings, parades, bridge dedications, soft anniversary tellings, trade-history forwards with ordinary stakes. Soft entry on hard past still applies when you eventually choose harder material: everyday life, tools, tickets, streets and work before atrocity as the first pair. Graphic violence is not the week’s piece box. Culture-war pairs stay out as the default object. The move must land before the temperature rises. If your life hands you a high-heat card, practice the move first on a parallel low-heat pair, then return — or wait a week. Looking under adrenaline often becomes prosecution.

### A CORR-focus week that stays honest

Banks are menus. A hypothesized week: Monday agree/differ micro; Tuesday image pair; Wednesday CORR-01 desk; Thursday one limit sentence on yesterday’s pair; Friday exit sticky plus liar-theater repair; weekend optional witness vs historian or map vs narrative. If life allows only three sits, keep agree/differ, one independence or image check, and one limit. Depth beats a tour of ten half-finished pairs.

### Representation before tidy reconciliation

Feeds and chatbots love reconciliation: “Here is what really happened.” Your job in this chapter is prior. Keep makers. Keep questions. Keep limits. Reconciliation may come later as a careful claim with warrants — and even then, some differences remain. A tidy story that erases makers is a later telling wearing a lab coat. You already know what to do with later tellings.

### What corroboration is not

It is not a weekly AP Document-Based Question. It is not a SHEG packet dump as identity. It is not automatic citizenship virtue — looking can improve without minting dispositions by magic; teach looking because it is how history works, and point elsewhere for citizenship craft when that is the aim.^58 It is not a licence to harass colleagues with “gotcha” pairs. It is not false balance. It is not liar theater. Hold the center: two short accounts, makers named, agree, differ, limit.

## If it isn’t clicking

**Diagnostic 1 — Liar theater.**  
Every difference becomes a moral verdict.  
*Next move:* Rewrite the differ line without a moral verb. Force the question each account answers. If you still suspect fabrication, list what *separate* evidence would be required — then go find a source for that claim, or mark it unproven.

**Diagnostic 2 — Collapse to one story.**  
You produce a smooth paragraph and cannot say which detail came from which piece.  
*Next move:* Force two columns. Star sources. Only then write three sentences that keep names visible. If a tool merged them for you, discard the merge as a draft of looking — it is not your check.

**Diagnostic 3 — Conspiracy from silence / false balance swing.**  
You over-read absence, or you declare “both equal” to escape judgment.  
*Next move:* For silence — use the CORR-06 exit line with two hypotheses and uncertainty. For false balance — write the question; name which account can speak to it and which cannot. Limits are strength.

When to slow down: high-emotion topic — switch to a low-heat parallel event and practice the move. When to go ahead: agree + differ + limit without prosecution verbs. When to ask a human: librarian for a second period voice; tutor for structured pairs; colleague only on public ordinary claims.

Correct yourself without crushing the attempt. “I went to liar theater” is a useful sticky. Replace the verb. Keep the looking. Anxiety: shorter T-chart, one agree win, stop — not a therapy manual.

If none of the three diagnostics fit, check a quieter failure: you never wrote the central question. Without a question, every difference feels like a fight and every agreement feels like victory. Write the question in one line at the top of the page. Then return to agree and differ. Many “stuck” sits are questionless sits.

## Why this matters

Adults often meet civic texts as slogans, memes, and campaign props. A phrase from a preamble arrives already moralized. An amendment arrives as a blank check for a present desire. A rights word arrives with today’s argument loaded into yesterday’s sentence. Reverence skips attribution. Outrage skips audience. Both skip the document.

This chapter restores a quieter meeting. A civic text is still a **sourced historical object**: makers, date, genre, audience, then-meaning — as far as a short sit can honestly go. You will work a Preamble phrase, one amendment in one sit, a short Article or section close read, a Declaration sentence softly, a rights word then/now, and the micro-habit that even a beloved excerpt still gets a sourcing card. You will refuse pledge-theater and a party-platform spine. You will refuse an Article-by-Article dump and a Constitution-chain table of contents reprinted from a teen parent Hour.

*History for Emerging Adults* owns Declaration → Articles → Constitution → Bill of Rights → Federalist/Brutus as a parent kitchen spine for ages 15–18. One pointer, then teach adult refresh here. Adapt objects. That chain is not this book’s weekly identity. When you want a Federalist/Brutus deep week — newspaper war, Publius as persona, sustained argument across essays — open *Publius*. This chapter will point once and will not rewrite that book here.

Looking at civic texts does not automatically mint citizens. Inquiry can improve while dispositions stay stubborn; teach looking because it is how documents work, and treat citizenship craft as a related aim you may pursue elsewhere.^60 Annenberg and Pew recall items are labelled population context in this book’s front matter — not this chapter’s finish line and not a three-branch flashcard graduation. Dense founding prose is hard work for many adults; keep excerpts short. Scaffolds are access, not a verdict on your character.^61

What this unlocks is usable respect for the page: maker before slogan; then-meaning attempted once; audience named; what a line restrains; a modern misunderstanding checked against text without a rant. Ordinary surfaces carry the practice: phone PDF of a public-domain amendment; ballot pamphlet clause; courthouse plaque; museum label beside a founding excerpt; workplace claim about “what the law always said” met first as a document question — ordinary stakes, not legal advice.

If you leave one civic excerpt sourced (maker / date / audience), one then-meaning attempt, one pledge-theater refuse, and the knowledge that *Publius* owns Federalist depth, the chapter did its job.

Rust here often sounds like two voices in one head: “I should already know this” and “this prose is impossible.” Both can be true as feelings and false as placement tests. Knowing a fragment from a school assembly is not sourcing. Finding prose difficult is not proof you missed a civic birthday. Place by the strip: can you put maker and audience on a recited line? Can you attempt then-meaning on one word without a platform chant? That is the gate — not whether you remember every amendment number in order.

## Understand it yourself

**Everyday picture.** An amendment on your phone as a PDF. You can recite a fragment from school. A meme overlays today’s slogan on the same words. The everyday trap is to treat the meme’s meaning as the document’s meaning. The everyday repair is smaller: open the public-domain text; name who made it, when, for whom; attempt then-meaning of one key term; ask what the line restrains; refuse to use the page as a loyalty test.

**Precise picture.** Genre matters. A constitution is not a speech. A declaration of principles and grievances is not a statute. A preamble states purposes; it is not a blank warrant for every later hope. An amendment is words with a ratification history and an audience of institutions and persons the text meant to bind or protect — then. Rights words shift. “Commerce,” “domestic,” “people,” “arms,” “press,” “religion” — pick one in context, not as a weaponized ambiguity game. Then-sense comes from surrounding lines and named references when needed; modern false friends get a sticky. You separate understanding from approving when the practice in view is one you reject — Chapter 6 deepens that move; here you keep audience and restraint visible.

C3 civics and history pathways map skills like explaining perspectives and using evidence; they are a standards map, not a home statute and not a promise about your week.^62 Your adult version this week is smaller: one excerpt, sourced; then-meaning sticky; pledge-theater refused.

**Wrong answers you should be able to hear (including from yourself).**

1. **Pledge-theater.**  
   The text becomes a loyalty test or campaign chant.  
   *What it usually means:* maker and audience erased; reverence or outrage replaced looking.  
   *What you try:* Run a full sourcing card even if you can recite the lines. Genre + audience sentence. “This is still a source because ___.”

2. **“They meant exactly what my party means.”**  
   Present platform loaded into historic words.  
   *What it usually means:* then-meaning skipped; slogan absorption.  
   *What you try:* Then-meaning sticky; who was meant to be restrained; check one modern misunderstanding against the text without a rant.

3. **Article-by-Article dump as the week.**  
   Coverage theater returns.  
   *What it usually means:* depth replaced by tour.

### Talk-to-yourself box

**Opening (locked):** “What did this word mean *then*, to *whom* — as a document, not as a slogan?”

**Follow-ups:**

1. What do you mean, *then* — which decade, which ratification moment, which audience?
2. What does the header, title, or surrounding clause show before the slogan lands?
3. Is there another legal reading of this line that still stays on the page?
4. What can this excerpt *not* settle — even if every word feels sacred or suspicious?
5. Where could someone go wrong — pledge-theater, party-means-exactly-this, dump, *Publius* creep — and may you revise?

**How to wait alone.** Cover modern commentary. Slow three. Look at the starred word on the public-domain page — not at a chatbot “Founders meant.” Rescue at one second is still rescue. If dense prose freezes you, shorten the excerpt to one sentence and keep the card.

### Audience then is not audience now

A line addressed to states, to Congress, to courts, to persons under a jurisdiction, or to a reading public in a crisis year is not automatically addressed to your group chat. Write audience then in plain words. You may also write why the line still matters to you now — that is a separate sentence, labelled as yours. Collapsing the two sentences produces slogan absorption. Keeping them apart produces adult looking. When a feed erases audience, your repair is to put audience back before you argue.

### Restraint is a looking question

Many civic lines are about what an institution may not do, or what a process must include. “Who was meant to be restrained?” keeps the document from becoming a wish list. You can still argue, later, about how later courts, statutes, or amendments changed the field. That later argument needs later sources. This sit asks what the text in front of you attempts. If your note only lists what you wish the text provided, you are reading silence as hope — label inference or return to written words (CIV-03 exit).

### Principle vs grievance (Declaration soft)

A Declaration sentence may state a principle, list a grievance, or do both nearby. Soft practice: label which. Principles travel in argument; grievances name concrete injuries to a then-audience. Treating every rising line as eternal statute confuses genre. Treating every line as disposable rhetoric confuses genre the other way. Occasion card (year, war/crisis context in one or two sentences of background max) is enough. No pamphlet cherry-pick that lifts a principle and hides the grievance list — or the reverse — without saying what you lifted.

### Rights words and false friends

Words drift. A then-sense sticky is not a lecture on linguistic relativity. It is a warning label: do not equate centuries without work. Surrounding lines first. Named reference second if still stuck. Overlap/gap chart third. Weaponizing ambiguity — “the word could mean anything, so my side wins” — is a wrong twin. So is “the word never changes.” Adults can hold a gap without turning the gap into a gotcha.

## Why this matters

What would have to be true in *their* world — and where / how long as tools, not trivia?

That is not a capitals quiz. It is not a wall of centuries. It is not a sermon about how people in the past were “dumb,” “corrupt,” or “just like us with worse phones.” It is the question that turns background into a tool under a piece you are already looking at. A letter about delay only makes sense if you can feel how long news took. A plaque about a harbor only makes sense if you can point at water and roads. A date only helps after a story has a job and a maker.

You already practiced source before body, Observe–Reflect–Question, feed versus primary, two accounts without liar theater, and civic texts as documents with makers. This chapter adds the next strip: thin contextualization — place and time under a story you own. Presentism is the mind at rest, including for able adults who finished school long ago.^66 A period map is a source with a maker. A modern overview map is often a later telling. Dates attach after the story is owned. Process duration and change-and-continuity soft deepen time without turning the hour into a trivia contest.

What this idea unlocks is usable context on the go. On the commute you can catch your own “they were stupid” sentence and rewrite it as one their-world condition. At a desk or on a break you can annotate where on a simple outline map, flag a memoir written later, or stretch an “overnight” event into a credible duration. On a weekend you can treat a period map as a source and build a short chronology card under one owned story. Coverage is a map. Depth is the hour.^67 You do not need a continent wall this week.

This is worth the struggle because ordinary adult life keeps offering present grids and wallpaper dates. A feed card compresses a century into a caption. A workplace claim about “what the law always said” skips how long a process took. A museum label names a place without asking who commissioned the marker. Context, maps, and timelines are tools under looking — not decoration, not a second textbook, not a culture-war color map.

You do not need to be a geographer or a chronologist. You do need to interrupt presentism without a scold, put one place that belongs to this week’s piece on the table, and add one date only under a story already owned. Hearing a deficit-past jab, an atlas slog with no story, or a memoir treated as a same-day witness without a later flag is the work. Filling the silence with a forty-minute lecture is not.

## Understand it yourself

**Everyday picture.** You open a short credited excerpt — a letter, a newspaper header, a founding clause you already sourced — and your first private sentence is: “They were stupid not to have [modern tool],” or “They were just corrupt,” or “Anyone with common sense would have done X.” Stop. Ask: “What would have to be true in *their* world for this to make sense?” Write three constraints: technology, law, belief, distance, risk. Then put a simple map of the places named on the table, or a date card under the story you just owned. The map did a job. The date did a job. Neither replaced the piece.

Another everyday picture: you walk past a civic plaque on lunch. The bronze says a date and a tidy sentence. You treat the plaque as the whole story. The adult move is MAP-03: who commissioned it, when was it erected, what does it claim, what primary would check it? The plaque is a later telling with a maker. It is still useful. It is not the archive.

**Precise picture.** *Contextualization* here means reconstructing conditions in *their* world — distance, delay, audience, word meanings, who was and was not at the table — without drowning the excerpt in a lecture and without a deficit-past sneer.^68 A *period map* is a source: someone made it, for a purpose; what it includes and leaves out is part of the look. A *modern outline map* you annotate is a tool you made; source whether it is period or modern before you treat lines as GPS truth. A *chronology card* is a short line of owned events or documents under one question, not a generated “complete world timeline.” *Process not snap* means many events told as a single day took weeks or years; name one precursor and one aftermath. *Separate understanding from approving* means one sentence that reconstructs reasons in their world and a separate sentence that keeps your present judgment — without collapsing explanation into endorsement, and without replacing looking with pure denunciation.

Wineburg’s familiar-versus-strange warning still holds: even strong readers default to now-sense.^66 Classroom work has often found sourcing and corroboration easier to move than contextualization; do not pretend one their-world prompt installs period expertise.^69 That is a useful study pattern, not a promise that every adult will see the same result. Then teach. Knowledge nearby helps — a tiny story the piece can carry — without a light-rail tour of the century.^67

Think of three layers you can actually hold in one sitting. Layer one is the piece: who made it, when, for whom. Layer two is thin context: one distance, one delay, one word sense, one absent group labelled evidenced or guessed. Layer three is the tool card: a place sticky, a duration sticky, a chronology of three nodes max. If layer three arrives without layer one, you have wallpaper. If layer two arrives as a scold, you have a sermon. If all three stay under one question, you have an hour.

A composite illustration (labelled as such — not a reported workplace or court case): you read a short historic newspaper notice about a delayed shipment. Your first thought is “why didn’t they just call?” CTX-02 asks how long news and goods took; MAP-04 asks what route or port constrained them; TIME-05 asks whether “delayed” was a day or a season. You still may judge the decision. You first rebuild the world the decision sat in.

### Wrong answers you should be able to hear

1. **Deficit-past sermon.** “They were dumb / primitive / just corrupt.” Repair: the their-world prompt; one condition stated without sneer; point back at the line. Presentism is rest, not a character flaw.^66

2. **Atlas wallpaper.** Capitals colored, continents named, no story owned, no sticky on a place the piece actually cares about. Repair: one question the map can answer; annotate where for *this* excerpt only.

3. **Dates as trivia.** Year-first freeze, or a fluent century chant with no owned story. Repair: date after story — own the narrative, *then* attach the date as a tool and ask what the date changes.

4. **Memoir as same-day witness without a later flag.** A reminiscence written decades later treated as if it were a diary from the day. Repair: double-date honesty card — event date and composition date; name memory and politics risks softly.

5. **Culture-war color map / excuse-everything reverse.** A modern political color scheme pasted onto the past as the hour’s spine — or the opposite: “understanding” used to excuse every practice without a separate judgment line. Repair: period or outline map under *this* piece; CTX-06 when a practice you reject appears — understand and approve are different verbs.

### Five-minute warm-up

Before any try-it, cover this page and work aloud:

- Catch one presentist sentence you have said (or almost said) this month about “people back then.” Rewrite it as: “What would have to be true in their world for ___ to make sense?”
- Name three candidate constraints (technology, law, belief, distance, risk). Keep the ones that touch *this* piece.
- Point at one place the piece names — even if only on a phone map sketch — and say one sentence: “Near here / along this route / across this water mattered because ___.”
- Cover every year. Tell the story in one sentence. Only then uncover one date and ask what changes.
- Write the locked opening on a sticky. Cut any second continent you do not need.

That is enough for tomorrow’s micro-session.

## Practice that actually builds it

Plaques, period maps, and short owned stories **are** the practice — they still do not replace the structure: their-world before sneer, place as constraint, date after story. Tips and tricks below are teachable moves, not slogans. Banks are menus, not calendars.

### Named try-its (CTX, MAP, TIME)

**1. CTX-01 Their World Prompt — commute / lunch or desk**  
*Time:* 15–20 minutes.  
*Materials:* one puzzling action or belief in a primary; sticky.  
*Safety:* no scold; no culture-war sermon; ordinary stakes.  
*The move:* Ask what would have to be true in *their* world. Write three constraints (technology, law, belief, distance, risk). Exit: three-constraint sticky. Refuse presentism sermon and excuse-everything sermon alike.

**2. CTX-02 Technology / Distance Gap Without Deficit — commute / lunch**  
*Time:* 10–15 minutes.  
*Materials:* a labelled travel-time or communication-speed estimate card (estimate, not gospel).  
*Safety:* estimates labelled estimates; no sneer at past actors.  
*The move:* How long for news or people to move? Rewrite a “why didn’t they just…” complaint as a question without deficit. Exit: revised question.

**3. TIME-01 Date After Story — commute / lunch**  
*Time:* 10–15 minutes.  
*Materials:* short narrative with dates covered first.  
*The move:* Own the story in one or two sentences. *Then* attach dates as tools. Ask what the date changes. Exit: story → date card. Refuse date trivia before any story.

**4. TIME-04 Memoir Written Later Flag — commute / lunch or desk**  
*Time:* 10–15 minutes.  
*Materials:* memoir or reminiscence dated long after events (archive-credited).  
*The move:* Flag composition date vs event date. Name memory and politics risks softly. Exit: double-date honesty card. Refuse memoir as instantaneous primary without flag.

**5. MAP-03 Local Marker Spot — on the go (OUT-adjacent)**  
*Time:* 15–20 minutes (walk or photo you already have).  
*Materials:* local historical marker or courthouse/civic plaque (photo OK).  
*Safety:* ordinary noticing; no sting; no cold public quiz of strangers; consent for people in photos.  
*The move:* Who commissioned? When erected? What is claimed? What primary would check it? Exit: commissioning = sourcing note. Pair with OUT-03 when you are at a courthouse or civic building. Refuse plaque as final word.

**6. CTX-06 Separate Understanding from Approving — desk (care)**  
*Time:* 20–30 minutes.  
*Materials:* primary that includes a practice you reject; care with holiday/remembrance labels when relevant.  
*Safety:* no live controversy as the object; no graphic harm; public-domain or archive-credited text.  
*The move:* Explain in their-world terms. Explicitly separate *understand* from *endorse*. One ethical line of your own without rewriting their motives as yours. Exit: “I understand X as ___; I do not approve because ___.” Refuse collapsing explanation into endorsement — or into pure denunciation without looking.

**7. CTX-03 Word That Shifted — desk**  
*Time:* 20–25 minutes.  
*Materials:* one word in a civic or historic text whose sense shifted (for example “domestic,” “commerce,” or another word the surrounding lines can carry — skip inventing a lexicon page — use context and a named reference if needed).  
*The move:* Surrounding lines; possible then-senses; modern false-friend warning; named reference if needed after you try. Exit: then-sense vs false-friend sticky. Refuse weaponizing ambiguity.

**8. CTX-04 Who Else Was in the Room — desk**  
*Time:* 20–25 minutes.  
*Materials:* narrative that names one hero or villain.  
*The move:* List unmentioned groups or roles; mark which are evidenced vs guessed. Exit: two evidenced absences + one guess labelled guess. Refuse inventing people to fit a pamphlet.

**9. CTX-05 Process Not Snap + TIME-05 Process Duration — desk**  
*Time:* 25–35 minutes.  
*Materials:* event often told as a single day.  
*The move:* Stretch into weeks or years; name one precursor and one aftermath (CTX-05). Minimum credible duration; what had to be built or negotiated (TIME-05). Exit: process timeline (five nodes max) + duration sticky. Refuse instant-revolution myth as the only story and snap-history for shareable feed arcs.

**10. MAP-01 Annotate Where — desk**  
*Time:* 20–30 minutes.  
*Materials:* blank or outline map; this week’s events.  
*The move:* Place 3–5 sites; one arrow for movement; source the map itself (modern vs period). Exit: photo or sticky of annotated map. Refuse map without sourcing whether period or modern.

**11. MAP-04 Route / Port Constraint — desk**  
*Time:* 20–30 minutes.  
*Materials:* route narrative + map.  
*The move:* Time/distance constraint; rewrite an impossible “they easily…” line. Exit: constraint sentence. Refuse ignoring terrain or sea as flavor text.

**12. MAP-05 Border Line Soft — desk**  
*Time:* 20–30 minutes.  
*Materials:* map showing a disputed or changing border (historic, credited).  
*The move:* What changed; for whom; on whose authority labelled. Exit: change note + uncertainty. Refuse present border as eternal. No culture-war color map as the hour.

**13. TIME-02 Chain Chronology Card — desk or weekend**  
*Time:* 25–40 minutes.  
*Materials:* three linked events or documents you already looked at.  
*The move:* Order them; name the link (cause? precondition? later memory?) with a hedge. Exit: ordered chain with link labels. Refuse causation from sequence alone without hedge.

**14. TIME-03 Change and Continuity Soft — desk or weekend**  
*Time:* 25–35 minutes.  
*Materials:* two moments on the same theme.  
*The move:* One change; one continuity; evidence for each. Exit: change/continuity pair. Refuse “everything changed” / “nothing changed.”

**15. MAP-02 Period Map as Source — weekend deep sit**  
*Time:* 40–60 minutes.  
*Materials:* period map with maker/date (library, archive site, LOC Free to Use).  
*The move:* Observe–Reflect–Question; what did makers emphasize?; what is distorted for purpose? Exit: purpose/distortion sticky. Refuse period map as GPS truth. Optional weekend pair: MAP-02 + TIME-02 under one owned story.

**16. OUT-03 plaque with MAP-03 — weekend or lunch walk**  
*Time:* 20–40 minutes.  
*Materials:* local plaque; phone for attribution photo if allowed.  
*Safety:* public space; ordinary noticing; no undercover audit.  
*The move:* MAP-03 steps; optional civic-clause link if a founding line is quoted. Exit: commissioning + claim check plan against a primary later.

### Talk-to-yourself while you practice

**TALK-03 Their World Round (desk).** Use CTX-01 as talk: speak three constraints; wait; add a fourth or revise. Exit: revised constraint list. Refuse monologue denunciation.

Keep TALK-01 energy from earlier chapters: attribution before plot summary when a new piece arrives. This chapter’s talk load is their-world and place/time tools — not a second feed chapter.

**Mixed (when the move is stable).** Pair MAP-03 with a their-world sentence the same day. Pair CTX-02 with MAP-04. Pair MAP-02 with TIME-02 on a weekend. Pair CTX-05 with TIME-05. Pair CTX-06 with a careful primary when you are ready — still one focus.

## If it isn’t clicking

No shame. Correct a wrong reason without crushing the attempt — applied to yourself. Short sessions and early wins beat a countdown timer as default “rigor.” This is not a therapy manual.

**1. Context becomes a lecture.**  
*Symptom:* you are ten minutes into background and have not asked a their-world question about *this* piece; or you pasted a chatbot “context paragraph” as the hour.  
*Next move:* one their-world sentence only (CTX-01). Cover the encyclopedia voice. Point back at the line. If dense prose is the blocker, shorten the excerpt, then return to the looking move. Slow down here. Do not add a second continent to “enrich” a stuck Monday.

**2. Map as decoration.**  
*Symptom:* a pretty map on the table; no sticky; no question the map can answer; or a modern overview treated as the whole past.  
*Next move:* one question the map can answer (MAP-01 or MAP-02). Source whether period or modern. Annotate one place that belongs to this week’s story. Ask what the map cannot tell. Then stop. Do not scold yourself for “not being a map person.”

**3. Date-trivia anxiety.**  
*Symptom:* silence while you hunt a year; or a fluent century with no owned story; or memoir dates collapsed into event dates.  
*Next move:* cover every date. Own one story (TIME-01). Attach one date. Ask what changes. If a memoir is in play, run TIME-04 double-date. Say aloud: “I do not need a year someone else is waiting for. I need a their-world condition or a place I can show.”

**When to slow down.** One sticky and a their-world sentence are not stable. Archaic place-names are the blocker — scaffold the reading, then return. Living-memory or family-artifact work needs consent you do not yet have — skip those versions until you do. Anxiety is eating working memory — shorten to one micro; early win; return tomorrow.

**When to go ahead.** You state a their-world condition without sneer, place a sticky with a reason from the line, attach a date after the story, and separate understanding from approving once when the piece needs it. Then MAP-02, TIME-02, MAP-05, or a weekend OUT plaque is fair.

**When to ask a human.** A librarian can help you find a period map or a local marker’s commissioning history. A tutor or community class can sit with archaic place-names. A colleague can be a second pair of eyes on a public plaque — not a quiz victim. Ask for help locating sources and checking a sticky. Do not ask anyone to finish your looking for you.

# Chapter 7 — Talking and writing a short civic claim

![A still-life: dated notebook with claim / evidence / warrant / limit labelled, short credited excerpt, pencil. No people. No logos. No readable UI.](book/art/ch07-opener.jpg){width=100%}

## Why this matters

What do I claim — which named source shows it — and what does this claim not settle?

That is not a timed Advanced Placement essay. It is not a request for a fluent paragraph a machine could have written. It is not a campaign speech. It is the question that turns looking into a sentence another person can check. A claim without a source is a vibe. A source named with no claim is a caption. A warrant that only restates the claim is a shrug in fancy clothes. A claim with no limit pretends one excerpt finished the fight. History talk for an adult on the go is a claim that points. History writing for an adult on the go is the same claim, slower, on a dated page — with a warrant and a named limit.

You already practiced source before body, Observe–Reflect–Question, feed versus primary, two accounts without liar theater, civic texts as documents, and context/maps/time as tools. This chapter is the exit strip for the teaching arc: short claim craft after looking — not instead of looking. Writing here is claim + named source + warrant + limit. It is not a weekly AP Document-Based Question. An occasional weekend two-source mini-argument is enough when paragraphs are stable. Dual perspective appears when prompted — one other-side best point — not as an unprompted demand every exit.^74

What this idea unlocks is usable civic speech and a short written claim you could almost show a stranger. On the commute you can write a source sentence first, point to a line, or limit an overwide claim. At a desk you can add a warrant, paraphrase a civic clause without smuggling a platform, and refuse a model summary as the source of your paragraph. On a weekend you can weave two sourced notes into 120–180 words with a limit — still not a five-paragraph costume.

This is worth the struggle because ordinary adult life rewards fluency and punishes pointing. You can talk for five minutes about a founding dispute without touching a line. A feed card finishes the moral for you. A chatbot offers a tidy précis that feels like homework done. Looking without a short claim leaves you fluent at noticing and silent at deciding what the piece can support. A short claim without looking is theater. The hour needs both — in that order.

You do not need to be a historian. You do need to hear a thesis without evidence, a copied model summary filed as your claim, dual-function demanded unprompted every time, and AP-DBQ-as-weekly-identity — and to ask the locked question, then wait. Hearing those wrong turns is the work. Filling the silence with a perfect paragraph from a window is not.

Picture two Fridays. In one, the notebook holds a tidy summary that could have come from any review page — or from a chat window. In the other, a short paragraph names a credited excerpt, points at a starred line, warrants why that line counts, and admits what the claim does not settle. Both pages look like writing. Only one is civic looking on paper.

If you used *Critical Thinking for Adults*, you already practiced myside and dual-perspective craft on ordinary present stakes. One pointer — then history: a spoken or written claim about a *past* piece, with the maker in the sentence. Keep judgment batteries in that book — this hour stays on history claim craft.^75 Monte-Sano’s neighbor grain for historical writing is the spine here: claim, evidence, corroboration, contextualization of evidence — taught, not hoped into existence by rewriting the same summary.^76 That is a useful study pattern, not a promise that every adult will see the same result. Then teach.

This week you can learn to model one claim sentence that names the source, one warrant stem, one limit sentence, and — when prompted — one other-side best point. Today you can write: “According to [credit], … because …. This evidence counts because …. This claim does not settle ….” A path through ordinary civic life includes this strip. A fake certificate does not. Optional credit exams live later if credit is your goal. For now: one good question, one starred line, one cautious sentence.

Wilke and colleagues caution that inquiry looking does not automatically mint democratic dispositions.^84 Looking still matters. Citizenship is not a button this chapter presses. The promise is usable claim craft on pieces you actually meet — ballot pamphlet language, museum caption honesty, a short founding excerpt as a sourced object, a workplace policy claim about “what the law always said” at ordinary stakes. Money, workplaces, and civic life motivate. They do not replace the looking move.

## Understand it yourself

**Everyday picture.** You have an opinion about a founding dispute, a plaque sentence, or a historic newspaper fight. You can talk for five minutes. Someone asks, “Which line shows that?” You gesture at the air. The adult repair is TALK-02 and ARG-01: finger or cursor on the supporting line; if you cannot point, retract or hedge. Then WRITE-01: first sentence names maker, date, type; second sentence may interpret. Then ARG-02: “This counts as evidence because ___.” Then ARG-06: what this claim does not settle.

Another everyday picture: a chatbot offers a fluent paragraph about “what the document meant.” You paste it into your note as your draft. WRITE-05 is the refuse: strike any sentence that only rests on the model; replace from the primary; redline. The model may ask revision questions **after** your draft. It may not be the only draft.

**Precise picture.** A *claim* here is a sentence about the past (or about what a civic text says as a historical object) that could be wrong and that the piece can speak to. *Evidence* is a starred line — something another person can find. A *warrant* ties the evidence to the claim: why *this* line counts for *that* claim (genre, proximity, corroboration, audience) — not a restatement of the claim. A *limit* is one honest sentence about what this source cannot tell or what the claim does not settle (time, place, population, or source hedge). A *source sentence first* opener names maker/date/type before interpretation. *Quote vs paraphrase*: quote only if the words uniquely matter; otherwise paraphrase and cite; never orphan fragments. *Add-one* (TALK-04): force one additional interpretation consistent with the same evidence; mark which fits better and why. *Counter soft* (ARG-03): steelman one pressure on your claim; adjust scope — without burning a strawman.

Talk is not a luxury before writing. Authentic questions — questions where you have not already scripted the answer for yourself — are the spirit of the talk box.^77 Materials do not discuss themselves. Wait before the feed when the urge to share arrives (TALK-05). Stuck silence gets a diagnosis, not forty minutes of scrolling (TALK-06). Accountable to the text soft (TALK-07): what in *this* text would change your mind? If nothing could, you are not doing history this hour.

A composite illustration (labelled as such — not a reported workplace): you meet a policy memo that says “the statute has always required X.” Your first draft is a three-paragraph rant. The adult sequence is different. Source the statute text or a short historic excerpt the memo gestures at (if public). WRITE-01. Star the line that actually speaks to X. ARG-01. ARG-02 — why that line counts (genre, date, who is restrained). ARG-06 — what the claim does not settle (later amendments, agency guidance you have not opened, cases not on the table). Optional ARG-05: what did the memo’s maker want you to believe? You still may disagree with the policy. You first refuse vibe citations and model summaries as the floor.

Without a teacher, adults skip warrants (“the quote is obvious”). ARG-02 and TALK-02 exist because *you* are the easy audience to con.^76 Practice alone is not enough if practice means rewriting the same summary. The solo check stays: delete the quotation; does the claim still stand? If yes, the quote was decoration.

### Wrong answers you should be able to hear

1. **Thesis without evidence.** “They wanted liberty.” No clause, no maker. Repair: “Which named source shows it?” Star a line or return to the source card.

2. **Copied model summary as claim.** Fluent encyclopedia voice; précis filed as primary or as the week’s evidence. Repair: WRITE-05; close the window; write from the piece in view; tool only after a real draft, as revision questions — not a rewrite.

3. **Warrant that only restates the claim.** “This supports my claim because my claim is true.” Repair: “Why does *that line* count for *that claim*?” One warrant stem (ARG-02).

4. **Dual-function demanded unprompted / myside fluency without limit.** You require a full other-side essay every micro-session — or you never name a limit. Repair: prompt one other-side best point when the week holds two voices (TALK-04); always run ARG-06 once; point to *Critical Thinking for Adults* once for myside craft — keep judgment batteries in that book.^74 ^75

5. **AP DBQ as weekly identity / write-my-op-ed as practice.** Five-paragraph costume; timed multi-document every Wednesday; “write my op-ed” as the only draft. Repair: ARG-04 / WRITE-04 as occasional weekend stretch when sourced paragraphs with warrant are stable; refuse DBQ dump as spine; ban write-op-ed as only draft.

A sixth worth hearing: **quote salad** — three copied lines and no sentence of your own. Repair: WRITE-02; sentence 1 names writer, date, type; then one claim; then one support; then warrant; then limit.

### Five-minute warm-up

Before any try-it, cover this page and work aloud:

- Star one line in a short credited excerpt you already hold. Write one claim that would *weaken* if that line vanished (ARG-01).
- Write a source sentence first: maker, date, type (WRITE-01). Only then write one interpretive sentence.
- Add one warrant stem: “This counts as evidence because ___.”
- Add one limit: “This claim does not settle ___.”
- Point to the line with finger or cursor. If you cannot point, retract.
- Write the locked opening on a sticky. Cut any second excerpt you do not need for a first claim.

That is enough for tomorrow’s micro-session.

### Talk-to-yourself box

**Opening (locked):**  
“What do I claim — which named source shows it — and what does this claim not settle?”

**Follow-ups:**

1. What do you mean by the claim — can you say it in one sentence? *(meaning)*  
2. What does the credit line / starred line show before your interpretation? *(representation)*  
3. Is there another legal or historical reading of this line that still fits? *(alternative)*  
4. What can this source *not* tell me — what does the claim not settle? *(check)*  
5. You may change your mind — where could someone go wrong with a decorative quote or a model précis? *(revise / diagnose)*

**How to wait alone.** After you ask, cover any answer you drafted. Count a slow three.^79 If you stop mid-reason, wait again before peeking. Look at the credit line, the plaque, the starred line, or the clock — not at the phone’s answer key or the feed. Rescue at one second is still rescue. Abandonment for forty minutes of scrolling is not a micro-session.

**What a stuck silence usually means.** The question was vague; wait-time 1 was zero; you are hunting for a vocabulary word; you are guessing what a textbook wants; you offered a keyword where a looking move was asked for; a finished-looking chatbot paragraph feels like a check; blank-page anxiety is eating working memory — shorten the session, win one WRITE-01 opener from one starred line, come back tomorrow. Your job is to pose, wait, and point back at the object — not to fill the silence with a model summary.

## Practice that actually builds it

Pamphlets, plaques, short civic excerpts, and two sourced notes **are** the practice — they still do not replace the structure: claim that points, warrant that explains, limit that hedges. Tips and tricks below are teachable moves, not slogans. Banks are menus, not calendars.

### Named try-its (TALK, WRITE, ARG)

**1. WRITE-01 Source Sentence First — commute / lunch**  
*Time:* 10–15 minutes.  
*Materials:* blank note; one credited piece.  
*The move:* First sentence names maker/date/type; second may interpret. Exit: two-sentence opener. Refuse interpretation-first paragraph.

**2. TALK-02 Point to the Line — commute / lunch**  
*Time:* 10–15 minutes.  
*Materials:* a claim you just made; the piece in view.  
*The move:* Finger or cursor on the supporting line; if you cannot point, retract or hedge. Exit: point-or-retract. Refuse vibe citations.

**3. ARG-06 Limit Your Claim — commute / lunch**  
*Time:* 10–15 minutes.  
*Materials:* an overwide claim you drafted earlier (or a feed sentence you almost believed).  
*The move:* Add time, place, population, or source-limit hedges. Exit: before/after claim. Refuse universal slogans as history claims.

**4. TALK-05 Wait Before Feed — commute / lunch**  
*Time:* 5–10 minutes.  
*Materials:* urge to reply or share a history-adjacent card.  
*Safety:* ordinary stakes; no rage-share as civic virtue; no live controversy as the try-it object.  
*The move:* Timer 3–5 seconds minimum (classroom-origin wait-time translated); ask whether the card is evidence or later telling; then decide. Exit: shared / not shared + reason.

**5. TALK-01 Source Before Summary Round — commute / lunch or desk**  
*Time:* 10–15 minutes.  
*Materials:* any short primary or civic object.  
*The move:* Say aloud (or write) attribution before any plot summary; wait 3+ seconds; then one Observe. Exit: audio note or sticky proving order. Refuse summary-first habit.

**6. ARG-01 Claim from a Starred Line — desk**  
*Time:* 20–25 minutes.  
*Materials:* primary with one starred sentence.  
*The move:* Write a claim that depends on that line; if the line vanished, the claim should weaken. Exit: claim + starred citation. Refuse decorative quote. Solo check: delete the quotation; does the claim still stand?

**7. ARG-02 Warrant Sentence — desk**  
*Time:* 15–20 minutes.  
*Materials:* claim + quotation already chosen.  
*The move:* Add “This counts as evidence because ___” (genre, proximity, corroboration, audience). Exit: warrant sentence. Refuse quotation dump without warrant.

**8. WRITE-03 Civic Clause Paraphrase — desk**  
*Time:* 20–30 minutes.  
*Materials:* short civic excerpt (public-domain).  
*The move:* Paraphrase in plain adult prose; check against words; note remaining ambiguity. Exit: paraphrase + ambiguity flag. Refuse paraphrase that smuggles a modern platform.

**9. WRITE-02 Quote vs Paraphrase Soft — desk**  
*Time:* 15–20 minutes.  
*Materials:* one quotation you want.  
*The move:* Quote only if words uniquely matter; otherwise paraphrase + cite; never quote orphan fragments. Exit: quote-or-paraphrase decision note. Refuse quote salad.

**10. WRITE-05 Model Summary Is Not a Source — desk**  
*Time:* 20–30 minutes.  
*Materials:* a model/chatbot paragraph (generated for this drill); your WRITE-01 opener; the primary in view.  
*Safety:* you hold the account; unaided look first; do not file the model as primary.  
*The move:* Strike any sentence that only rests on the model; replace from the primary. Exit: redlined draft. Refuse model-summary-as-source.

**11. ARG-05 Audience and Purpose in Argument — desk**  
*Time:* 20–30 minutes.  
*Materials:* argumentative primary (pamphlet, editorial; Publius/Anti-Federalist soft optional — point to *Publius* for a Federalist deep week; do not rewrite it here).  
*The move:* What did the maker want the reader to do or believe? How does that shape evidence selection? Exit: purpose→selection note. Refuse “biased = false.”

**12. TALK-04 Add-One Protocol — desk**  
*Time:* 15–25 minutes.  
*Materials:* your first interpretation; the same evidence.  
*The move:* Force one additional interpretation consistent with the same evidence; mark which fits better and why. Exit: two interpretations ranked. Refuse first-thought lock-in. This is dual-perspective when prompted — not a demand for unprompted dual-function every micro.^74

**13. ARG-03 Counter Soft — desk**  
*Time:* 20–25 minutes.  
*Materials:* your claim; one source that pressures it.  
*The move:* Steelman the counter in one sentence; adjust claim scope. Exit: scoped claim. Refuse ignoring the counter; refuse burning a strawman.

**14. TALK-07 Accountable to the Text Soft — desk**  
*Time:* 15–20 minutes.  
*Materials:* a favorite prior belief about the topic; today’s piece.  
*The move:* What in *this* text would change your mind? If nothing could, you are not doing history this hour. Exit: falsifier sticky. Refuse unfalsifiable identity claims as hour products.

**15. TALK-06 Stuck Silence Repair — any band**  
*Time:* 5–15 minutes when you froze.  
*The move:* Diagnose: mushy question / too-hard prose / fear of being wrong. Repair the question or scaffold one sentence (often WRITE-01). Exit: diagnosis + repair. Refuse abandoning the sit for scrolling.

**16. TALK-03 Their World Round — desk (bridge from ch06)**  
*Time:* 15–20 minutes.  
*Materials:* CTX-01 prompt under a claim you are forming.  
*The move:* Speak three constraints; wait; add a fourth or revise; then ask whether your claim still fits. Exit: revised constraint list under the claim. Refuse monologue denunciation.

**17. ARG-04 Two-Source Mini-Argument — weekend**  
*Time:* 45–60 minutes.  
*Materials:* two sourced excerpts on one question.  
*The move:* Claim; evidence from each; one warrant; one limit. Exit: ≤150-word paragraph. Refuse five-paragraph costume without warrants. Still not weekly AP DBQ.

**18. WRITE-04 Two-Source Paragraph — weekend**  
*Time:* 45–75 minutes.  
*Materials:* two sourced notes.  
*The move:* Claim; evidence weave; warrant; limit (Monte-Sano neighbor grain).^76 Exit: 120–180 words. Refuse parallel book reports glued together.

**19. WRITE-06 Revision Pass — Evidence Weave — weekend**  
*Time:* 30–45 minutes.  
*Materials:* *your* draft paragraph (not a model rewrite).  
*The move:* Highlight claims without evidence; add or cut; one corroboration phrase if available. Exit: before/after highlight photo. Refuse longer = better. Tool may ask revision questions after — not rewrite as only draft.

### Talk-to-yourself while you practice

Keep the locked opening in your mouth. On micro days, TALK-02 and TALK-05 carry more weight than a full paragraph. On desk days, point to the line before you polish. On weekend days, add-one and counter soft earn their minutes only after a claim already names a source.

### Blocked, then mixed

**Blocked (new move).** One source sentence *or* one point-to-the-line *or* one limit sentence. WRITE-01, TALK-02, or ARG-06 alone. No second document. No op-ed.

**Mixed (when the move is stable).** Pair ARG-01 with ARG-02. Pair WRITE-01 with ARG-06. Pair WRITE-05 refuse with a real primary beside the précis. Pair TALK-04 with ARG-03 when two voices are on the table. Pair ARG-04 with WRITE-04 on a weekend — still occasional.

### Hypothesized week menu (composite — not a calendar)

Monday: WRITE-01. Tuesday: TALK-02. Wednesday: ARG-01 + ARG-02. Thursday: WRITE-05 refuse. Friday: claim+limit sticky. Optional weekend: ARG-04 + WRITE-04. Ambition shape, not an RCT.

### One incorrect example to diagnose (composite illustration)

> Notebook paragraph: “The Founders clearly meant [modern slogan]. As everyone knows, the document proves it. [chatbot précis pasted, no credit line, no starred words from a primary]. Therefore the debate is over.”

**What went wrong.** Thesis without a named source you opened; model summary as source; no warrant; no limit; pledge-theater energy on a civic text; looking skipped.  
**Repair.** Delete the pasted précis. Open a credited public-domain excerpt. WRITE-01. Star a line. ARG-01. ARG-02. ARG-06. Optional TALK-04 one other-side best point. The debate is not “over.” The claim is scoped.

## If it isn’t clicking

No shame. Correct a wrong reason without crushing the attempt — applied to yourself. Short sessions and early wins beat a countdown timer as default rigor. This is not a therapy manual.

**1. Blank page.**  
*Symptom:* you stare; you rewrite the same summary; you open a chat window to “get started.”  
*Next move:* star one line → WRITE-01 source sentence → one claim that depends on the line (ARG-01). One paragraph is enough. If dense prose is the blocker, shorten the excerpt (PIAAC literacy is adjacent reading stock, not a history talent label).^80 Slow down here. Do not assign ARG-04 to “enrich” a stuck Monday.

**2. Myside fluency without limit.**  
*Symptom:* a confident paragraph that never hedges; or a victory lap that never points at a line; or dual-function demanded of yourself as shame when a micro only needed a source sentence.  
*Next move:* ARG-06 one limit sentence; TALK-02 point to the line; prompt one other-side best point only when the week holds two voices (TALK-04). Point once to *Critical Thinking for Adults* for myside craft — then return to this piece.^75

**3. Tool wrote it.**  
*Symptom:* the only draft is a model rewrite; detector anxiety; fluent voice with no starred line in view.  
*Next move:* delete; unaided re-draft from the piece; then revision questions after *your* draft (WRITE-06). Do not detector-grade your own unaided write-up — false positive rates on human writing can be severe.^81 Do not trust a fluent citation without opening the paper.^82 Photo-to-key stays banned during the attempt.

**When to slow down.** Source sentence and point-to-the-line are not stable. Anxiety is eating working memory — shorten to one micro; early WRITE-01 win; return tomorrow. Archaic prose is the blocker — scaffold access, then return to claim craft.

**When to go ahead.** You can write a claim that names a source, a warrant that is not a restatement, and a limit — and you refused a model summary as source once. Then ARG-04 / WRITE-04 weekend stretch is fair. Still not weekly DBQ identity.

**When to ask a human.** A librarian can help you find a second short primary for ARG-04. A tutor or community class can sit with warrant stems. A trusted reader can ask “where is the line?” — not rewrite your claim for you. Ask for help locating sources and hearing missing warrants. Do not ask anyone to finish your looking or to paste a model paragraph as yours.

| Date | Band (commute / desk / weekend / museum / library) | Object (plaque / pamphlet / primary / feed card…) | Move practiced | One claim + named source | One limit | Minutes | Next cue |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Looking moves this book uses (match the teaching chapters):

1. Source before body (who / when / what kind / for whom / against what problem)  
2. Primary before feed (open a piece from then — or a named secondary with provenance — before the host summary)  
3. Observe–Reflect–Question on a short object (honesty card: original / excerpt / translation / later printing / modernized / screenshot / AI-generated)  
4. Corroborate two accounts (agreement, tension, limit — without calling a liar)  
5. Civic text as sourced object (Preamble phrase, one amendment, short founding excerpt — makers named; not a slogan)  
6. Claim + named source + warrant + named limit  
7. Survey / CLEP edges (optional — chronology breadth and institutions only when credit is the plan)

You do not need every move every week. A shift worker may live in source-before-body, primary-before-feed, and one claim sentence on commute cards. A returning student may add corroboration and civic-as-source deep sits. Placement is by life demand and looking skill, not by a podcast that said “everyone needs Western Civ.”

**Rebuild week vs maintenance week.** A rebuild week opens a rusty or new move: more honesty cards, a deeper sit, blocked practice before mixing. A maintenance week revisits last month’s plaque or feed check with three micros and one mixed interloper — no heroics. Adults who only rebuild and never maintain will re-rust. Adults who only maintain and never rebuild will not open the next gate. Name both in the log (“R” or “M” in the Next cue column is enough).

Life of the habit, here: the note is how the habit leaves a trace. “I used to treat the anniversary caption as the event; now I open a piece first” is growth. It is not a diploma ceremony.

## Self-placement by looking move (not diploma year)

A college graduate can still treat a feed caption as the event. A GED-holder can source a plaque cleanly. **No diploma was required** to open this book, and no diploma year places the next module.^85

Use short probes — mirrors, not oracles. Vendor placement tools (OpenStax chapter previews, DIG lesson central questions, CLEP practice guides, community-college placement, commercial adaptive platforms, citizenship-test prep apps) place by content familiarity; they are not IES-certified RCTs of this book.^86 Annenberg and Pew civic-fact surveys describe a national recall pond. They are not your pretest, not your posttest, and not a grade for your notebook.^87

**Probe ladder (self-check, not a published test):**

- **Source before body.** Cover the body of a short piece or a feed card. Name who / when / type / audience before you decide what it means. If you still leap to the tidy story, stay here.  
- **Primary before feed.** Given a viral claim or a podcast host’s tidy sentence, open one short primary (or a named secondary with provenance) before judging the feed. Fail shape: the host’s sentence becomes “what happened.”  
- **Observe–Reflect–Question.** One object (photo, poster, short letter, plaque photo). Concrete observations → questions → inferences with honesty about incompleteness.  
- **Corroborate.** Two short accounts of one event. Where they agree; where not; one limit — without liar theater.  
- **Civic text as source.** One amendment or Preamble phrase: makers, then-meaning sticky, what the line can and cannot settle. Refuse pledge-theater.  
- **Claim + warrant.** One sentence claim; named source; why the source supports it; one limit.  
- **Survey / CLEP edges (optional).** Chronology breadth; government institutions — only for adults whose study plan needs credit.

Rule of thumb: if you cannot yet name who made a feed card before absorbing its caption, you are not ready to skip to a 120-question survey sit. If a civic excerpt still arrives as a slogan, you are still in civic-as-source work, whatever your diploma. Patch the hole in front of the next real task. You may be Band F at work (clean claim on a workplace policy citation) and Band A on weekend feeds in the same month — that is ordinary, not a character flaw.

## Dated looking note / annotated excerpt / two-source card

Keep what a stranger can use.

**A dated looking note.** Object named. Band named. Move practiced. One claim with a named source. One limit. The date is 13 September 2026 or whatever day it actually was — not “Q3 civics.”

**An annotated excerpt with an honesty card.** Mark original / excerpt / translation / later printing / modernized / screenshot / AI-generated. Credit line first. What you noticed before you decided.
